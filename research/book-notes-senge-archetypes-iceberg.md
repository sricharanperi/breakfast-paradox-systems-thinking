## 1. The eleven laws, as tests for an intervention

The full list with Senge's parables is in `book-notes-fifth-discipline.md` §3. Here each law is restated as the question it asks of a proposed intervention (FD Ch.4, pdf p.42 to 48):

| # | Law | Question it asks of an intervention |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Today's problems come from yesterday's "solutions" | Which current problem is the residue of an earlier fix (the December 2024 cap, the 70 percent ratio, Skip Meal without refund)? |
| 2 | The harder you push, the harder the system pushes back | What compensating feedback will offset this (a supervisor re-hedging after one run-out)? |
| 3 | Behaviour grows better before it grows worse | Does the plan expect the delayed backlash, and will it still be monitoring when it arrives? |
| 4 | The easy way out usually leads back in | Is the chosen step the familiar one (kitchen tweaks) rather than the one where the problem lives (the booking)? |
| 5 | The cure can be worse than the disease | Does the fix build dependency (Shifting the Burden to the Intervenor)? |
| 6 | Faster is slower | Is the pace set by the system's own delays (Yuktāhār's four months to make weighing stick)? |
| 7 | Cause and effect are not closely related in time and space | Is the lever placed where the cause is, not where the symptom shows (the bin versus the portal default)? |
| 8 | Small changes can produce big results, but the areas of highest leverage are often the least obvious | "Learning to see underlying structures rather than events is a starting point; each of the systems archetypes ... suggests areas of high- and low-leverage change" (pdf p.48). |
| 9 | You can have your cake and eat it too, but not at once | Is an apparent either/or (waste versus run-outs) resolved by sequencing over time (calibrated batch plus late batch)? |
| 10 | Dividing an elephant in half does not produce two small elephants | Is the boundary drawn around the interactions (booking, kitchen, records, rule owner), not the org chart? |
| 11 | There is no blame | "You and the cause of your problems are part of a single system." Does the design treat each actor as responding rationally? (Echoed in L14: "Each actor is responding rationally to the incentives and information available to them.") |

## 2. The archetypes: structure, signature, warning sign, way out

Structure and delays are from FD Ch.6 and Appendix 2 unless marked; behaviour-over-time signatures from the FB comparison chart; warning signs are Senge's "Early Warning Symptom" lines (Appendix 2) or the FB's recognition clues; leverage is the Appendix 2 "Management Principle" plus the FB "Strategies" list.

### 2.1 Balancing Process with Delay
- **Structure.** One balancing loop acting toward a goal on delayed feedback. Unaware of the delay, the actor over-corrects or gives up (FD App.2, pdf p.353).
- **Signature.** Overshoot and oscillation around the goal.
- **Warning sign.** "We thought we were in balance, but then we overshot the mark."
- **Leverage.** "In a sluggish system, aggressiveness produces instability. Either be patient or make the system more responsive."

### 2.2 Limits to Growth
- **Structure.** A reinforcing growth loop, then a balancing loop that engages as a limit is approached. The limit may be a resource, or an internal or external response to growth. Collapse is the reinforcing loop running in reverse (FD App.2; Ch.6 pdf p.82).
- **Signature.** Growth, then levelling off or decline (FB chart).
- **Warning sign.** "Why should we worry about problems we don't have? We're growing tremendously," then later "The harder we run, the more we seem to stay in the same place."
- **Leverage.** "Don't push growth; remove the factors limiting growth." "Leverage lies in the balancing loop, not the reinforcing loop" (FD Ch.6, pdf p.88). The FB adds that the real leverage is in the early phase, while you still have slack. L10 teaches the same pattern ("Success contains the seeds of its own limits").

### 2.3 Shifting the Burden, and the special case "to the Intervenor"
- **Structure.** Two balancing loops correct the same symptom. The top loop is a fast symptomatic fix; the bottom loop is the fundamental solution, and it has a delay. Often a third, reinforcing side-effect loop means that reliance on the fix erodes the capacity for the fundamental solution: the "figure eight" of addiction (FD Ch.6, pdf p.92 to 96).
- **Signature.** Three patterns run together. Reliance on the fix rises. The symptom oscillates but drifts up. The fundamental capability declines (FB: "The quick fix continues upward ... the symptom oscillates ... the capacity of the system to fix itself declines").
- **Warning signs.** "Look here, this solution has worked so far! What do you mean, there's trouble down that road?" (FD App.2). Senge gives three clues: a problem that worsens over the long term with temporary improvements; the overall health of the system slowly worsening; and "a growing feeling of helplessness" (FD Ch.6, pdf p.98). The FB adds "I can get out of this any time I want."
- **Leverage.** "Strengthening the fundamental response and weakening the symptomatic response" (pdf p.96). Weakening the fix "requires willingness to tell the truth about palliatives." "Symptomatic solutions must always be acknowledged as such, and combined with strategies for rehabilitating the capacity for fundamental solution." "If symptomatic solutions are employed as if they are fundamental solutions, the search for fundamental solutions stops" (pdf p.97). Appendix 2: "If a symptomatic solution is imperative (because of delays in the fundamental solution), use it to gain time while working on the fundamental solution." For the intervenor: "Teach people to fish ... focus on enhancing the capabilities of the host system to solve its own problems." The FB adds: "Use the archetype as a tool for inquiry, not as a tool for advocacy"; different groups will each name a different "fundamental solution," and dialogue on the difference "often unearths mental models ... as the real root causes."

### 2.4 Eroding Goals (Drifting Goals)
- **Structure.** A Shifting the Burden variant in which the symptomatic fix is lowering the goal. A gap between goal and actual can be closed by corrective action (slow, delayed) or by lowering the goal (instant) (FD Ch.6, pdf p.94; App.2; Kim).
- **Signature.** A slow, barely noticed decline in both standard and performance (Kim: performance "degrade[s] imperceptibly over a long period").
- **Warning sign.** "It's okay if our performance standards slide a little, just until the crisis is over." The manufacturer's story is the diagnostic one: "We've maintained a consistent 90 percent success in meeting the delivery time quoted", while the quoted time kept lengthening.
- **Leverage.** "Hold the vision" (FD App.2). Kim adds that goals anchored outside the system are less prone to drift, so tie the standard to an external frame of reference.

### 2.5 Fixes That Fail (FB: "Fixes That Backfire")
- **Structure.** A balancing loop (fix relieves symptom), plus a delayed reinforcing loop of unintended consequences that worsens the same symptom (FD App.2; FB Archetype 1). L10 gives it as "Problem → Quick Fix → Temporary Improvement → Unintended Consequence → Bigger Problem → More Quick Fixes."
- **Signature.** "Small triumphs and long troughs": the symptom improves, then returns worse (FB chart).
- **Warning signs.** "It always seemed to work before; why isn't it working now?" Also the feeling of needing "to try the same solution just a little more," and powerlessness despite seeing the danger (FB).
- **Leverage.** "Maintain focus on the long term. Disregard short-term 'fix,' if feasible, or use it only to 'buy time'" (FD App.2). FB strategies: acknowledge openly that the fix only relieves a symptom; cut back how often it is used; minimise its consequences; and "every fix that backfires is driven by an implicit target in the balancing loop. So make it explicit." The FB family tree asks, after a Fix That Backfires, "Why are we putting so much attention on quick fixes?", which usually leads to a Shifting the Burden structure underneath (FB Ch.18). L10's lesson: "If you only optimise surface metrics, the system fights back later."

### 2.6 Tragedy of the Commons
- **Structure.** Two or more linked Limits to Growth loops sharing one finite resource, which is the implicit target of every balancing loop. Gain per individual activity falls faster than any single loop would make it, which prompts each actor to step up (FB Archetype 4; FD App.2).
- **Signature.** "Total activity grows, but the gains from individual activities are dropping" (FB chart).
- **Warning sign.** "There used to be plenty for everyone. Now things are getting tough." Senge's business story is diagnostic: divisions sharing a salesforce each inflate their requests "so that the salesforce would at least give them the minimum support."
- **Leverage.** "Manage the commons, either through educating everyone and creating forms of self-regulation and peer pressure, or through an official regulating mechanism, ideally designed by participants" (FD App.2). The FB gives three routes: make the collective cost visible to each actor, close off the resource so it can replenish, or replenish it and remove the constraint. It also warns: tell a true tragedy apart from a case where you "shift the burden of a painful decision to the next level up." L10 asks: "Who maintains the commons? Are incentives balanced?"

### 2.7 Success to the Successful
- **Structure.** Two reinforcing loops coupled through one limited resource: the more successful activity gets more resource and starves the other (FD App.2).
- **Signature.** The two diverge, one rising and one falling.
- **Warning sign.** "One ... is beginning to do very well and the other is struggling."
- **Leverage.** "Look for the overarching goal for balanced achievement of both choices", or break the coupling where it is inadvertent.

### 2.8 Escalation
- **Structure.** Two balancing loops, each side acting on its relative position, which together form a reinforcing figure eight (FD App.2).
- **Signature.** Both sides' efforts ratchet upward.
- **Warning sign.** "If our opponent would only slow down ..." Senge's example of inflating budget estimates is also escalation.
- **Leverage.** Find a way for both sides to win; one side unilaterally de-escalates. Senge also names pure advocacy meeting advocacy as an escalation (FD Ch.10, pdf p.183), which is why balancing inquiry and advocacy is its conversational antidote.

### 2.9 Accidental Adversaries
- **Structure.** An outer reinforcing loop of mutual support. Each partner has a local balancing fix that unintentionally obstructs the other's success, so a middle reinforcing loop of mutual damage forms (FB Archetype 5, Kemeny; the P&G and Wal-Mart case).
- **Signature.** "Each side's performance either declines or stays level and low, while enmity or competitiveness increases over time" (FB chart).
- **Warning signs.** Each side is confident "the solution is to convince the other partner that its strategy is the correct way"; the two do not communicate and are "unconscious of their effects on each other."
- **Leverage.** "Don't push on the well-intended fix ... seek to strengthen your understanding of your partner's fundamental needs." Bring both into one room to map the shared structure: "the other organization's strategy seemed perfectly rational ... from their local perspective." This is Senge's law 11 and L14's "don't blame" in archetype form.

### 2.10 Growth and Underinvestment
- **Structure.** Limits to Growth where the limit is capacity that could be built, but investment is withheld and standards are lowered to justify it: an Eroding Goals loop attached to a Limits to Growth loop (FD App.2; Ch.7 WonderTech).
- **Warning sign.** "We used to be the best ... but right now we have to conserve our resources."
- **Leverage.** Build capacity ahead of demand; "hold the vision ... assessing key performance standards."

**Family-tree rule (FB Ch.18).** For a growth phenomenon, go down the reinforcing trunk; for a problem-fixing phenomenon, go down the balancing trunk. A Fix That Fails usually sits on top of a Shifting the Burden structure.

## 3. Mental models: how to surface and test them

- **Definition and force.** Mental models "determine not only how we make sense of the world, but how we take action." They are "active" because they shape what we see (FD Ch.10, p.174ff, quoting Argyris: people "do behave congruently with their theories-in-use").
- **Espoused theory versus theory-in-use.** What we say against what our actions imply. The gap is useful when it is told truthfully (FD Ch.10, pdf p.190).
- **Leaps of abstraction.** A move "from direct observations ... to generalization without testing." Detect one by asking "What is the 'data' on which this generalization is based?" and "Am I willing to consider that this generalization may be inaccurate?" Then separate the generalisation from its data, and "where possible, test the generalizations directly" (FD Ch.10, pdf p.178 to 180; the Laura case).
- **Ladder of inference** (FB Ch.35). The rungs run from observable data, to selected data, to added meaning, to assumptions, to conclusions, to beliefs, to action. The reflexive loop means beliefs select the next data. The ladder is used in three ways: reflection, advocacy ("make your thinking visible") and inquiry ("What is the observable data behind that statement? Does everyone agree on what the data is? Can you run me through your reasoning?"). "The point ... is not to nail Larry ... but to make our thinking processes visible."
- **Left-hand column** (FD Ch.10, pdf p.180 to 182; FB p.246 to 247). Script a conversation that is not working on the right; write what you thought but did not say on the left. It "always succeeds in bringing hidden assumptions to the surface." Use it on the team's own interviews and pitches, not on the other party.
- **Balancing advocacy and inquiry** (FD Ch.10, pdf p.183 to 185; FB protocols). Advocacy: "Here's what I think, and here's how I got there"; state your assumptions and the data behind them. Inquiry: "What leads you to that position?" Pure advocacy escalates. When both are high, "we are open to disconfirming data."
- **The course's position.** L14's Level 4 (mental models): "These mental models may be among the deepest leverage points." L15, leverage point 6, is "Change the Mental Model", carried out by restating the belief as a healthier one rather than attacking the actors ("Don't compete with coaching at the coaching layer. Change the architecture").

## 4. The iceberg and multi-level intervention

- **Senge's three levels** (FD Ch.3, around p.52): Events (reactive), Patterns of Behaviour (responsive), and Systemic Structure (generative). "Event explanations ... doom their holders to a reactive stance"; structural explanations are "the least common and most powerful." The FB adds that structure "includes attitudes and perceptions ... the ways in which decisions are made," and that structures are "built out of the choices people make ... over time." The four-level iceberg with mental models as its base is the course's form, credited to the Donella Meadows Project (see the Fieldbook note), and taught in L14.
- **Course method** (L12, 15 steps): step 7 Identify Mental Models; step 12 Identify System Archetypes; step 13 Find Leverage Points; step 14 Develop Multi-Level Interventions; step 15 Monitor System Behaviour Over Time. L15 adds "But every intervention can create a new problem ... anticipate second-order effects" and scenario testing.
- **What a multi-level intervention means in practice.** Each level gets its own action and its own indicator:
  - **Events:** a rapid response that is acknowledged as symptomatic, per Senge.
  - **Patterns:** an indicator tracked over time, not a snapshot (law 9).
  - **Structures:** a rule, route, role or delay changed, targeted at the archetype's leverage loop.
  - **Mental models:** a belief surfaced and tested with data (ladder, advocacy and inquiry), with a stated replacement belief.
  - **Monitoring:** watch the archetype's signature (for example, reliance on the fix rising while fundamental capability declines) long enough to catch law 3's "better before worse."
