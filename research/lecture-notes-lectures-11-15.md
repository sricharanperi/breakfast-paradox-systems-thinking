## Lecture 11: Design Thinking vs Systems Thinking
- **Human lens** (design thinking): who? what do they need, experience, value?
- **System lens:** what is happening, why does it keep happening, who and what are connected, what structures produce the behaviour, what might happen next?
- **Design thinking alone** risks "a beautiful solution to the wrong systemic problem" (the flyover that increases car dependency, induced demand and sprawl).
- **Systems thinking alone** risks "a technically perfect system that nobody wants to use": "systemically efficient but humanly poor" (e.g. an automated advising system that students find confusing and impersonal).
- **The ideal relationship:** systems thinking to understand *why*, design thinking to understand *who*, co-design to *generate solutions*, then systems thinking again to *check consequences and system impact*.
- **Use systems thinking when** a problem is complex, recurring, multi-stakeholder, produces unintended consequences, and involves feedback loops, delays and change over time. "Campus waste management" and "university attendance" are listed as good cases.

## Lecture 12: A 15-step method (Delhi air pollution exercise)
1. Define the problem
2. Define the system boundary
3. Identify stakeholders
4. Identify events
5. Identify patterns over time
6. Identify system structures
7. Identify mental models
8. Identify variables
9. Map causal relationships
10. Identify feedback loops
11. Identify delays
12. **Identify system archetypes**
13. **Find leverage points**
14. **Develop multi-level interventions**
15. **Monitor system behaviour over time**

Steps 12–15 are the Phase 3/4 steps.

## Lecture 14: Worked example: the JEE coaching phenomenon
- **Reframe from symptoms to structure:** not "how do we fix coaching?" but "what is the system structure that makes intensive coaching rational, self-reinforcing and difficult to escape?"
- **First principle: don't blame the actor** (the coaching institutes). Ask why, repeatedly, down to social aspiration and mobility.
- **Loops:**
  - R1 Competition escalation;
  - R2 Coaching normalisation ("becomes not merely an option but the default expectation");
  - R3 Coaching success (results → reputation → students → resources);
  - R4 Inequality / opportunity accumulation;
  - a school–coaching balancing/declining loop;
  - a psychological competition loop that becomes dysfunctional.
- **Selection effect:** strong students → coaching → strong results is misread as coaching → strong students. *Distinguish correlation from causation* when evaluating whether an intervention works.
- **Critical systems insight:** "Each actor is responding rationally to the incentives and information available to them, while their collective behaviour creates an undesirable system outcome."
- **Iceberg:** Events → Patterns → System Structure (high-stakes exam, limited seats, information asymmetry, business incentives, ranking mechanisms, labour-market signalling) → Mental Models ("without coaching I cannot compete", "if everyone else is doing it I cannot afford not to"). **"These mental models may be among the deepest leverage points."**

## Lecture 15: Leverage and intervention
- **Leverage** is "a small, well-chosen change that produces a disproportionately large and lasting impact": the right place, not more effort.
  - Option A, *more effort:* people, money, processes, repeated fixes.
  - Option B, *leverage:* change a relationship, improve the flow of information, modify incentives, change a rule, redefine the goal.
- **Low leverage example:** building more roads relieves congestion temporarily, until induced demand brings it back. It addresses a symptom.
- **High leverage example:** ask why people prefer cars (transit reliability, work hours, parking incentives) and change *the structure that generates congestion*.
- **Levels of leverage:** the lecture numbers **Level 1 = Parameters** (budget, staff, capacity, cost, deadlines) as the shallowest. This is the reverse of Meadows' numbering (12 = parameters). Deliverables must name the category, not just the number.
- **"Find the leverage points":** not "how do we eliminate coaching?" but "where can we intervene so that the system no longer *requires* ever-increasing coaching intensity?"
- **Six leverage points in the worked example:**
  1. **Change the information structure** (transparent pathway and outcome information; "the objective is not to tell students what to do, it is to allow better-informed decisions").
  2. **Change the goal** (from rank → selection to capability, a "much deeper intervention").
  3. **Change feedback** (from score → competition to evidence → understanding → improvement).
  4. **Change incentives** ("people optimise what the system measures and rewards").
  5. **Redesign the relationship** between actors (school + open ecosystem instead of school vs coaching).
  6. **Change the mental model** ("may be the deepest intervention").
- **"Every intervention can create a new problem":** the system adapts (the coaching industry shifts to Olympiads, interviews, portfolios). Anticipate second-order effects.
- **Design the future system rather than destroy the actor:** "create a system in which coaching is an option rather than a perceived necessity". "We don't necessarily eliminate X. We change the conditions that create excessive dependence on it."
- **Key design principle:** "Don't compete with coaching at the coaching layer. **Change the architecture of the ecosystem.**"
- **Test interventions with scenarios** (A: current system; B–E: alternatives) across many indicators (participation, hours, expenditure, stress, equity, learning, performance, engagement, unintended consequences). "The objective isn't to predict the future perfectly. It is to ask how the system might respond to each intervention."
- **Activity:** a causal-loop challenge ("AI attendance monitoring"): draw R and B loops and discover unintended consequences (stress, creativity, admin workload).

## What this means for the Breakfast Paradox (Phase 3 checklist)
- Treat **mental models** as explicit leverage points: the student ("I'm registered anyway, it's paid for"), the operator ("we are paid for registrations", "breakfast is about 50%"), the institution ("advance registration minimises waste").
- **Name the archetypes** the loops form and use each archetype's canonical way out.
- **Frame the objective as a future system:** breakfast registration as an option matched to intent, not a default to be escaped, and "not destroy registration".
- **Change the architecture**, not the parameter: the 70% ratio is the "more roads" of this system.
- **Guard comparisons** (Yuktahaar vs Kadamba) against selection effects and confounding.
- **Add the human lens:** desirability for students and operators, and co-design and prototyping with users.
- **Test scenarios** across many indicators, framed as system response rather than prediction, and **anticipate adaptation** (vendor pricing, gaming, metric fixation).
- **Plan the interventions across iceberg levels** and **monitor behaviour over time**.
