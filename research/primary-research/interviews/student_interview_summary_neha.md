**Source:** Synthesized from `student_interview_transcpirt_neha.md` (verbatim
transcripts of 5 respondents: 1 unlabeled daily eater, 1 combined "daily eater
2/3" transcript, and skip-1/2/3). This is analysis, not raw data — read the
transcript file itself for exact wording. Sample size is small (5 students, not
randomly sampled) — findings below are directional, not statistically
representative, and are confidence-tagged against what the rest of Phase 1 already
claims.

Legend: \confirmed = matches/confirms an existing hypothesis in the Phase 1
deliverables; 🟡 = partial or mixed support; ❗ = new finding not previously
captured anywhere in the project.

## 1. Monthly (not weekly) batch registration — ✅ confirms the hypothesis, sharpens the timescale

`prathyusha_04-process-trace.md` flagged a *weekly* batch-registration hypothesis as
the single highest-value thing to confirm. Three of five respondents describe
registering for an entire **month** at a time, not day-by-day and not weekly:

- Daily eater 1: *"I register for a month, not day by day."*
- Skip-3: *"I register for breakfast for the whole month because Kadamba is hard to
  get and it gets full."*
- Skip-1: registers "beforehand," "sometimes all at once."

**This corrects the process trace's timescale**, not just confirms its shape: the
registration decision is decoupled from daily attendance at a *monthly* cadence, an
even wider gap than the weekly hypothesis assumed. `prathyusha_04-*.md` §A should be
updated from "weekly cycle" to "monthly cycle."

## 2. Sleep/late nights, not mess hours or food quality, is the dominant skip driver — ✅ strongly confirmed

Every respondent who described a skip pattern named late sleep (2–4 AM) as the
cause, not system design:

- Daily eater 1: *"if I sleep around 3:00 or 4:00 a.m. or later, I don't tend to
  wake up. I prefer sleep over breakfast."*
- Daily eater 2/3: *"it's because I've slept very late, sometimes around 3:00 or
  4:00 a.m."*
- Skip-3: *"I sleep very late, sometimes until around 3 or 4 in the morning, and
  then I'm not feeling hungry."*

This directly confirms the synthetic pilot's "it's not a system-design thing, it's
willpower/habit" finding (`prathyusha_synthetic-pilot-interviews.md`, Transcript 2)
with three real respondents now behind it — a materially stronger evidence base than
before.

## 3. A genuine class-vs-breakfast trade-off exists, and breakfast sometimes loses — ❗ new, sharper than previously captured

Daily eater 2/3 describes an explicit trade-off decision, not just a scheduling
inconvenience: *"If I have an 8:30 class and I wake up around 8:00 or 8:30, I
prioritize going to class because of the attendance policy rather than going for
breakfast."* This is a cleaner mechanism than the earlier "wakes up earlier to fit
both" adaptation seen in the prior respondent (2026-09-03 interview) — here,
breakfast is the one that gets dropped when the two genuinely conflict, and the
reason named is the academic attendance policy, not personal preference. Worth
folding into Task 1's symptom table as a second, more concrete mechanism behind the
"mess hours overlap 8:30 AM class start" symptom.

## 4. Companion effect is real but not decisive — 🟡 mixed, resolves in favor of "weak, not load-bearing"

`prathyusha_04-*.md` flagged the roommate/companion-prompt mechanism as a high-value
open hypothesis. Results are split:

- **No effect:** daily eater 1 (goes alone, "doesn't matter"), skip-1, skip-2 all
  report no companion influence.
- **Some prompting, but not decisive:** daily eater 2/3 actively wakes friends and
  prefers eating with them, but explicitly says *"even if they don't come, I'll
  still prioritize having my breakfast."* Skip-3 says friends are "a positive
  motivation" but *"if I have to go, I'll go"* regardless.

**Net finding:** no respondent said their own attendance would meaningfully change
if a companion were absent for a week — the counterfactual question the interview
guide specifically added to test this returned a consistent "no" across all five.
This should move the companion-effect hypothesis in `prathyusha_04-*.md` from 🟡
open to a qualified finding: real but secondary, not a load-bearing driver of the
skip/attend decision.

## 5. Skip Meal is understood correctly, and ignored for exactly the reason hypothesized — ✅ confirmed

No respondent has used Skip Meal in any way that returns value, and those who
described it understood it accurately:

- Daily eater 1: *"I never use Skip Meal because it doesn't return the money."*
- Skip-1: *"it basically lets them know that this person is not going to come for
  the meal, so they can account for the food accordingly."*

This isn't a misunderstanding of the feature — students know exactly what it does
and rationally ignore it because it doesn't address their actual cost. Confirms
`01-system-context-brief.md`'s symptom table entry on Skip Meal directly.

## 6. Mess Cell WhatsApp resale is an active, familiar market — ✅ confirmed, no longer just a hypothesis about scale

Four of five respondents have direct experience with it:

- Daily eater 1: bought and sold.
- Skip-1: has seen it happen "a few times."
- Skip-2: bought and sold, mostly for lunch/dinner rather than breakfast.
- Skip-3: uses it "rarely."

## 7. Deliberate "register to guarantee a slot, resell if unused" strategy — ❗ directly confirmed for at least one respondent

`prathyusha_05-*.md` flagged this as materially important and unresolved: is
reselling mostly accidental recovery, or a deliberate strategy? Skip-3 answers this
directly: *"I do register for breakfast for the whole month because Kadamba is hard
to get and it gets full. But I resell breakfast very rarely."* This is a student
registering primarily to **secure scarce capacity**, not primarily intending to eat
every day — reselling is a backstop, not an afterthought. Daily eater 1 explicitly
denies doing this ("No, I don't generally do that"), so this is a real but not
universal behavior — worth a larger sample before generalizing, but no longer a pure
hypothesis.

## 8. Peak crowding sits later than the original hypothesis, and items reliably run out — 🟡 partially corrects Task 5's hypothesis table

`prathyusha_05-*.md` hypothesized an 8:00–8:30 peak. Real respondents cluster later:

- Daily eater 1 & 2/3: peak crowding **9:00–9:30**, worst after 9:15.
- Skip-3: peak **8:00–9:00** (goes early deliberately to avoid it).

Items running out is consistently confirmed — idli, puri, bhatura, and fruit
specifically named by daily eater 1, with staff reportedly promising restocks that
don't arrive before the 9:30 close. **Task 5's within-window hypothesis table should
be updated**: the peak is better described as building through the whole 8:00–9:30
window and cresting right before close, not dipping after 8:30 as originally
guessed.

## 9. Students see close to zero real influence on mess decisions — ✅ confirmed, consistent across the board

Every respondent who addressed this gave some version of "not much" or "not really,"
several without prompting. Daily eater 2/3 goes further, framing it as a management
responsibility gap rather than a consultation gap: *"management needs to be more
involved in addressing the issues students experience."* Confirms the Stakeholder
Map's "inputs exist, outputs don't" finding directly from the student side.

## 10. The single most-requested fix, repeated almost verbatim across respondents: extend the closing time — ❗ new, convergent, and actionable

This is the strongest pattern in the whole dataset. Every respondent who offered a
suggestion converged on the same fix, independently:

- Daily eater 1: *"ending an hour later would help me."*
- Skip-1: extend "until around 10:15... so that people who miss breakfast in the
  morning can still have it afterwards."
- Skip-2: *"If the timing is better, definitely. I might start going."*
- Skip-3: *"Increasing the breakfast time period would definitely help."*

Notably, **nobody asked for an earlier start** — the repeated ask is a later close,
not a shifted window. This is a sharper, cheaper, and more specific intervention
candidate than the general "mess hours vs. class overlap" framing in Task 1, and
should be flagged prominently for Phase 2's leverage-point analysis.

## 11. A genuinely new intervention idea: pay-as-you-go instead of fixed registration — ❗ new

Skip-3 proposed a structural alternative not discussed anywhere else in the
project: *"a canteen-style option where you can just go and buy things for
breakfast... a QR-based system where money can be added as needed, rather than
having a fixed amount of money."* This reframes the whole registration-billing
model rather than patching it, and is worth carrying into Phase 2's design-principles
work as a candidate alternative structure, not just an incremental fix.

## 12. An unverified operational claim worth flagging, not yet a finding

Daily eater 2/3 states: *"the statistics show that only around 30% of registered
students actually come for breakfast."* This number is not sourced or attributed
in the transcript — it reads as the respondent's own impression, not a cited fact.
It should **not** be treated as confirmed data, but it is a useful, specific target
number to verify directly in the pending mess-staff (Role C) interview, since it
would materially sharpen the waste estimate in Task 5.

## Sample caveat

Five respondents, not randomly selected, self-reporting retrospectively rather than
observed directly. Treat every finding above as directional evidence to weigh
alongside the synthetic pilot and the one prior real interview
(`2026-09-03_student-self-account-and-mess-system-overview.md`), not as a settled
result on its own.
