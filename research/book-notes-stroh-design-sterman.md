## 1. David Peter Stroh, *Systems Thinking for Social Change* (Chelsea Green, 2015)

Read via summary. Chapter references are approximate.

| Idea | What it says | Supports lecture slide |
|---|---|---|
| "Systems are perfectly designed to achieve the results they are achieving" | Stroh adopts the quality-movement maxim: the results you get are the output of the structure, not of bad actors. | L14 "Critical Systems Insight": each actor is responding rationally to the incentives and information available to them |
| Don't blame; we unintentionally perpetuate the system | Well-meaning people "unintentionally perpetuate the very systems we wish to change". The method is to help each party see its own contribution, not to find a culprit. His example of "accidental adversaries" (Iowa Department of Education and the regional agencies) is resolved by having both parties sit down and see how they undermine each other. | L14 "First Principle: Don't Blame the Coaching Institutes" |
| Four-stage change process | (1) **Build a foundation**: identify and convene the key stakeholders, build a common aspiration and readiness to change. (2) **Face current reality**: map the whole system and have each stakeholder accept its share of responsibility for producing it. (3) **Make an explicit choice**: build *the case for the current system* (its **payoffs**, and the costs of change) next to the case for the changed system (its benefits, and the costs of not changing), then choose. (4) **Bridge the gap**: high-leverage interventions, bring in more stakeholders, learn and adjust from experience. | L11 "Ideal relationship" (co-design generates solutions); L15 "design the future system" |
| Payoffs of the status quo | The status quo persists because it pays someone. These payoffs (convenience, safety, revenue, avoided workload, identity) must be surfaced and "honestly interrogated", or they quietly drain the will to change. | L14 "each actor responds rationally"; L15 "Leverage Point 4: Change incentives" |
| Multi-stakeholder convening | Change is designed with the people in the system, not recommended to them. The review notes Stroh's difficulty with design teams that saw their job as "rapidly producing recommendations about how others should change". | L11 co-design; Design Council "connector and convenor" |
| Archetypes: fixes that fail, shifting the burden | Quick fixes relieve a symptom while a delayed side effect makes the problem worse. Shifting the burden lets the symptomatic fix erode the capacity for the fundamental solution. | L15 "every intervention can create a new problem"; the project's L17 (a more competent kitchen hides the gap upstream) |

## 2. Tim Brown / IDEO and the Design Council

Read via summary.

| Idea | What it says | Supports lecture slide |
|---|---|---|
| Design thinking definition (Brown, HBR June 2008; *Change by Design*, 2009) | "A discipline that uses the designer's sensibility and methods to match people's needs with what is technologically feasible and what a viable business strategy can convert into customer value and market opportunity." Brown's HBR opening uses Edison: the bulb was "little more than a parlor trick" without the power system around it, so the design unit is the system. | L11 "Human lens: Who? What do they need? What do they experience?" |
| Desirability, feasibility, viability | Three lenses to hold together: desirable (makes sense to and for people), feasible (technically or organisationally possible), viable (sustainable for whoever must run and pay for it). The claim is that design thinkers excel at harmonising the three. | L11 "Systemically efficient but humanly poor"; the risk that systems thinking alone yields "a technically perfect system that nobody wants to use" |
| Prototype early and test with users ("build to think") | Make ideas tangible quickly so real users can evaluate them. "Fail early to succeed sooner." Inspiration, then ideation, then implementation, looping. | L11 "Design Thinking: understand WHO experiences it"; co-design |
| Design Council, *Systemic Design Framework* (2021) | Keeps the Double Diamond but reworks it as **Explore, Reframe, Create, Catalyse**, surrounded by "invisible activities": orientation and vision setting, connections and relationships, leadership and storytelling, continuing the journey. Six principles: people and planet centred; zooming in and out; testing and growing ideas; inclusive and welcoming difference; collaborating and connecting; circular and regenerative. Four roles: system thinker, leader and storyteller, designer and maker, connector and convenor. | L11 "Design Thinking + Systems Thinking should ideally work together"; L15 "design the future system" (vision setting) |

## 3. John D. Sterman, *Business Dynamics* (Irwin/McGraw-Hill, 2000)

Read directly. Book page numbers are given.

| Idea | Location | What it says | Supports lecture slide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy resistance | Chapter 1, section 1.1.1, p. 5 | "The tendency for interventions to be delayed, diluted, or defeated by the response of the system to the intervention itself" (after Meadows 1982). "Our decisions may provoke reactions by others seeking to restore the balance we upset." | L15 "Every intervention can create a new problem / the coaching industry may adapt" |
| Road building | Table 1-1, p. 9; section 5.6, pp. 177-190 | "Road building programs designed to reduce congestion have increased traffic, delays, and pollution." Section 5.6 builds the causal loop diagram: the capacity-expansion balancing loop is defeated by traffic volume responding to shorter travel time. Table 1-1 also lists compensation examples: low-tar cigarettes (smokers smoke more) and set-aside acres (farmers plough new land). | L11 flyover and induced demand; L15 "Build more roads" |
| No side effects, just effects | Section 1.1.2, p. 11 | "In reality, there are no side effects, there are just effects... Side effects are not a feature of reality but a sign that our understanding of the system is narrow and flawed." | L15 "anticipate second-order effects"; Harney "unintended consequences" |
| We are embedded in the system | Section 1.1.2, p. 10 | "We are not puppet masters influencing a system out there; we are embedded in the system." Others react "to restore the balance we have upset". | L14 "each actor responds rationally" |
| Confounding and ambiguity | Section 1.3.1, pp. 21-22; section 1.3.3, p. 25 | Controlled experiments are often impossible. "Many variables change simultaneously, confounding the interpretation." Changes caused by our decisions "are confounded with simultaneous changes in a host of other variables". | L14 "The Selection Effect" and "Distinguish correlation from causation" |
| Poor intuitive scientists | Section 1.3.7, p. 30 | People "do not generate sufficient alternative explanations or consider enough rival hypotheses... do not adequately control for confounding variables". | L14 selection effect |
| Implementation failure and gaming | Section 1.3.9, p. 33 | "Local incentives, asymmetric information, and private agendas can lead to game playing by agents throughout a system." Evaluators "may not know the ways in which the decisions they thought they were implementing were distorted." | L15 "People optimise what the system measures and rewards" |
| Participatory modelling | Section 1.4.2, p. 36 | "Effective learning from models occurs best, and perhaps only, when the decision makers participate actively in the development of the model." The model must be "an open box whose assumptions can be inspected, criticized, and changed." Warns against the "video game syndrome": playing too much and thinking too little. | L11 co-design; L15 scenario testing |
| Causation versus correlation | Section 5.2.1, p. 141 | "Correlations among variables reflect the past behavior of a system. Correlations do not represent the structure... if new policies are tried, previously reliable correlations among variables may break down." | All models are wrong | Section 21.1, p. 846 | "Validation and verification of models is impossible... because all models are wrong." Models are judged as useful for a purpose, not as true. | L15 "The objective isn't to predict the future perfectly" |

## 4. Sterman, "Learning from Evidence in a Complex World", *American Journal of Public Health* 96(3), 2006, pp. 505-514

Read directly.

- Abstract, p. 505: policies "often fail or worsen the problems they are intended to solve". Complexity hides "the delayed and distal impacts of interventions", and "common mental models lead to erroneous but self-confirming inferences". This supports L15 on second-order effects.
- p. 505: people "claim to be side effects, hoping to excuse the failure of our intervention." Long delays mean "we never experience the full consequences of our actions." This supports L15.
- Figure 1, p. 507: policy resistance comes from the unaccounted-for "responses of other agents in the system" and from unanticipated feedback. This supports L14.
- p. 511: implementation is "distorted by asymmetric information, private agendas, and game playing by agents throughout a system." In simulation, "pushing a system into extreme conditions reveals more about its structure and dynamics than incremental adjustments." This supports L15 scenario testing: stress the system and explore its response, rather than fine-tuning a point forecast.

## 5. Goodhart's law and Campbell's law

- **Goodhart (1975)**: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." The popular form, from Strathern (1997), is: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."
- **Campbell (1976; roots in "Reforms as Experiments", 1969)**: "The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor."
- Supports L15, Leverage Point 4: "People optimise what the system measures and rewards." It also connects to Sterman's p. 33 point on game playing. Mitigations in the literature: use several indicators rather than one target, pair quantity with quality measures, keep the measure diagnostic (learning) rather than evaluative (reward or penalty), and rotate or audit indicators.

## 6. Harney, "Systems Theory" (2024), course reading

Read directly (Hutchings, Michailova and Wilkinson (eds), *A Guide to Key Theories for HRM Research*, pp. 312-317).

- **Open systems**: organisations are "interdependent with some larger environment" (Thompson 1967). Their viability depends on resources and inputs from that environment. This supports L14 "Define the system boundary / outside but influences".
- **Equifinality**: "organisations can deploy differing ways to reach the same final state". Systems theory "cautions against universal prescriptions or fixed solutions". This supports L14 selection effect: one successful case does not prove its route is the cause, or the only route.
- **Unintended consequences, informality, power, legitimacy**: open-system theory "accommodates non-rational organisational outcomes". Organisations are "complex adaptive systems composed of multiple stakeholders, who frequently have competing interests". There is a danger of "presumed unitarism", the assumption that everyone works toward the same goal. This supports L15 "the industry may adapt" and Stroh's payoffs.
- Research agenda: "what are the differentiated ways organisations respond to similar challenges and with what consequence?" This is the right question for a two-kitchen comparison.
