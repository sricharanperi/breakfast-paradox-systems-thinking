## Systems, Structure and Mental Models {#sec-lit-structure}

Meadows (2008) describes a system as an interconnected set of elements organised to achieve something, and she reduces it to three parts: elements, interconnections and a purpose. Her central claim is that the characteristic behaviour of a system comes mostly from its structure, and only in part from the people who work inside it. Senge (1994) makes the same point with the Beer Game, a supply-chain simulation in which players with different skills and intentions reliably produce the same cycle of boom and collapse.

Senge (1994) draws out several corollaries that the project used. His eleven laws of the fifth discipline include that today's problems come from yesterday's solutions, that the harder one pushes the harder the system pushes back, that cause and effect are not closely related in time and space, and that there is no blame, because the actor and the cause of the problem belong to one system. The law on dividing an elephant gives a rule for the boundary: the line is drawn around the interactions that matter to the issue, not around the existing departments.

Mental models are the assumptions that people hold about how the system works. Senge (1994) separates the theory that people espouse from the theory their actions imply, and he describes leaps of abstraction, in which a generalisation is reached from a few observations and never tested. The Fieldbook (Senge et al., 1994) adds the ladder of inference. A person climbs from observable data, through the details selected from it and the meaning added to them, to assumptions, conclusions and beliefs, and then to action; the beliefs reached determine which data are noticed next.

## Feedback, Delay and Behaviour over Time {#sec-lit-feedback}

Feedback is the central concept of the field. A balancing loop moves a quantity toward a goal and a reinforcing loop amplifies a change, and delays between cause and effect make both hard to see (Meadows, 2008; Sterman, 2000). A causal loop diagram records the links with a polarity sign on each, plus a plus sign when two variables move in the same direction and a minus sign when they move in opposite directions. The course's formal definition has four elements: variables, arrows, polarity signs and loops marked R or B (Saxena, 2026).

A diagram of a loop is an argument, and Sterman (2000) lists the ways in which such arguments fail. Correlations reflect the past behaviour of a system, which is different from its structure, so a relation seen in past data may break down when a policy changes. Interventions meet policy resistance, defined as the tendency of interventions to be delayed, diluted or defeated by the response of the system to the intervention itself.

Behaviour over time is the companion to structure. Meadows (2008) advises watching how a system behaves before intervening and starting from data, and Sterman (2000) treats the graph of a key variable against time as the starting point of a model. Activity 5 ([[sec:a5]]) draws its charts on that basis, and the scenario model of Activity 10 ([[sec:a10]]) projects the same variables forward.

## The Iceberg and Multi-Level Reading {#sec-lit-iceberg}

Senge (1994) distinguishes three levels of explanation: events, which lead to reactive responses; patterns of behaviour, which lead to responsive ones; and systemic structure, which is generative. Event explanations, he writes, doom their holders to a reactive stance, and structural explanations are the least common and the most powerful. The course teaches a four-level form of the same idea, the iceberg, in which an event is traced down through a pattern and a structure to the mental model that sustains the structure (Saxena, 2026).

The course's fifteen-step method places the iceberg inside a longer sequence: define the problem and the boundary, identify stakeholders, events, patterns, structures and mental models, identify variables, causal relationships, loops and delays, then archetypes, leverage points, multi-level interventions and monitoring over time (Saxena, 2026). The project framework of ten activities covers the same ground in a different grouping. Two cautions from the wider theory apply to a case like this one.

## System Archetypes and Traps {#sec-lit-archetypes}

Archetypes are recurring structures that appear in many systems. Senge (1994, Chapter 6 and Appendix 2) lists nine: balancing process with delay, limits to growth, shifting the burden (and its form, shifting the burden to the intervenor), eroding goals, escalation, success to the successful, tragedy of the commons, fixes that fail, and growth and underinvestment. The Fieldbook adds accidental adversaries and describes five of the archetypes in depth, each with a signature of behaviour over time and a list of strategies (Senge et al., 1994).

Each archetype carries its own leverage. In shifting the burden a quick fix relieves the symptom, reliance on it grows, and the capacity for the fundamental solution withers. The remedy is to strengthen the fundamental response and weaken the symptomatic one, and to use the fix only to gain time. In fixes that fail a fix works on its target and a delayed consequence worsens the symptom; the remedy is to make the implicit target of the fix explicit and to keep the long view.

The course taught three archetypes (fixes that fail, tragedy of the commons and limits to growth) and its syllabus names a different group (Saxena, 2026), so the project worked from the books. [[tab:lit-archetypes]] records which structures it examined and with what result. A claim of presence was made only where the loops supported it. The readings were made in [[sec:a7]] and revisited in [[sec:a9]], where they order the levers.

## Stakeholders, Power and Leverage {#sec-lit-power}

The power-interest grid is usually attributed to Mendelow (1981). It places stakeholders by how much power they hold over a decision and how much interest they take in it, and it prescribes a style of engagement for each cell: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed, or monitor. It is a simple instrument and suits a first reading of who matters. Its weakness, which appeared in Activity 3 ([[sec:a3]]), is that it assigns one position to each actor, whereas the same actor can decide one question and be absent from the next.

Stroh (2015) adds a framing for the question of why a system persists. A system, in the maxim he adopts from the quality movement, is perfectly designed to produce the results that it produces, and people who wish to change it unintentionally help to perpetuate it. Its status quo has payoffs, such as convenience, revenue, avoided workload and safety, and a design that ignores them will meet resistance that no one planned.

## Leverage Points and Depth {#sec-lit-leverage}

Meadows (1999) ranks twelve places to intervene in a system, from the shallowest to the deepest. Counted from the deepest, they are the power to transcend paradigms (1), the mindset or paradigm (2), the goals of the system (3), the power to add, change or self-organise structure (4), the rules (5), the structure of information flows (6), the gain around reinforcing loops (7), the strength of balancing loops (8), the length of delays (9), the structure of stocks and flows (10), the size of buffers (11), and constants and parameters (12). Her warnings guided the project. People push leverage points in the wrong direction.

Two conventions are in circulation and they run in opposite directions. Meadows counts down toward depth, so that parameters are 12. The course presents levels with parameters as level 1 (Saxena, 2026). Senge's own treatment is pre-formal, a search for the archetype and then for the weakest loop.

## Process Tracing, Blueprints and System Maps {#sec-lit-process}

Shostack (1984) introduced the service blueprint, in which the steps of a service are laid out in lanes separated by a line of visibility between what the customer sees and what the organisation does behind it. Service design guidance describes journey maps and blueprints as complementary: the first shows the experience from the user's point of view, and the second shows the processes, systems and policies that support each stage (Government of Ontario, n.d.). The breakfast has exactly this form, because the student sees a display plate, a queue and a scan while the kitchen decides, cooks and tops up behind the counter.

## Reframing, Design Thinking and Design Principles {#sec-lit-design}

Design thinking, as Brown (2008) defines it, matches people's needs with what is technically feasible and with what a viable strategy can turn into value. Three lenses are held together: what is desirable, what is feasible and what is viable. The course sets design thinking beside systems thinking and states the risk of each used alone: a beautiful solution to the wrong systemic problem, or a technically perfect system that nobody wants to use (Saxena, 2026).

Reframing itself is taught through the worked example of coaching for entrance examinations, in which the question changes from how to fix the coaching to what structure makes intensive coaching rational (Saxena, 2026). The project's reframe in [[sec:a8]] follows the same move, from why students do not eat to what holds the gap in place. Design principles are then the short tests that every concept must pass, derived from the leverage analysis ([[sec:a9]]).

## Testing Interventions without a Pilot {#sec-lit-testing}

An intervention that cannot be piloted has to be tested in some other way. Sterman (2000, 2006) argues that models should be judged by their usefulness for a purpose, since all models are wrong, and that pushing a system into extreme conditions reveals more about its structure than small adjustments do. The course gives the same instruction: scenario testing asks how the system might respond to each intervention and makes no attempt to predict the future (Saxena, 2026). The project replayed proposed kitchen rules over recorded days of data, which is a backtest, and applied assumed shocks, which is a stress test ([[sec:a10]]).

Measures need the same caution. Goodhart's law holds that a statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes, and Campbell's law states that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decisions, the more it is subject to corruption pressures and the more it distorts the processes it monitors (Goodhart, 1975; Campbell, 1979).

## Interviews, Observation and Question Design {#sec-lit-interviews}

The primary evidence of the project came from semi-structured interviews, visits and requests to see records. The textbook treatment distinguishes unstructured, structured and semi-structured interviews, and recommends the semi-structured form when topics are known and the answers are not (Sharp, Preece and Rogers, 2019). Nunnally and Farkas (2016) name four pitfalls in questions: leading, shallow, personally biased and unconsciously biased wording. They break a question into a setup, an area of inquiry, laddering follow-ups and a segue, and they advise cutting any question that does not serve a named objective.

Two limits from these sources shaped the claims. A person's account of what they do differs from what a record shows, so interview findings were checked against records where records existed. And a small interview sample can show that a mechanism exists and cannot support a claim about how common it is; prevalence needs a survey or a record (Hall, 2013).

## Key Gaps and the Project's Response {#sec-lit-gaps}

The frameworks above are general. Within the material available to the team, none addressed standing registration, billing at the moment of registration and kitchen planning in an institutional canteen, and none gave a standard of evidence for a student team working with partial records. The project adapted the generic methods in seven ways, set out in [[tab:lit-gaps]].

Table: Gaps in the available literature and the project's response. {#lit-gaps cols=40,60}
| Gap | Response in the project |
|---|---|
| Power-interest grids give one position per actor. | Authority and leverage were judged decision by decision, and power was separated from leverage ([[sec:a3]]). |
| Causal loop diagrams are often drawn from plausible links, with no rule for acceptance. | A loop was accepted only if every link was evidenced; the rest stayed as chains with a dashed closing link ([[sec:a6]]). |
| Archetype guidance assumes a trajectory over time, and the project had records for one month in each of two halls. | Archetypes were graded as present, candidate, risk or not established, and the absence of a time series was stated ([[sec:a7]], [[sec:a9]]). |
| Leverage scales rank depth and say little about what a small team can do, and two numbering conventions are in use. | Meadows' numbering was fixed, each lever was ranked by depth and then judged for feasibility, and levers waiting on missing facts were held ([[sec:a9]]). |
| Evaluation of interventions relies on a pilot or on a single weighted score. | Four pass-or-fail gates came before scoring, a concept expected to fail was kept in every test, and every result was labelled projected, simulated or planned ([[sec:a10]]). |
| Interview guidance assumes access to a large population. | Interviews were used for mechanisms and records for prevalence, and every claim carried a label of how sure the team was ([[ch:method]]). |
| Sources on default booking in institutional catering were not found in the material available. | The system was analysed from first-hand evidence, and conflicts between sources were kept open and not resolved ([[ch:framework]]). |
