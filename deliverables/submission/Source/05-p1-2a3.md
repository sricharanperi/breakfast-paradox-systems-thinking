## Activity 3: Analysing Power, Interests and Leverage {#sec-a3}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a3-approach}

The activity shows how authority, resources, precedent and bargaining power shape the system and who can and cannot influence its decisions. Power is the capacity to influence a decision; leverage is the capacity of a small action to produce a large effect. Power is issue-specific, so every claim names its domain.

Table: Key activities of Activity 3, the method used for each and where it is reported. {#a3-approach cols=27,53,20}

| Identify formal and informal power | Actors with standing against actors with influence but none | [[sec:a3-informal]] |
| Analyse incentives and motivations | Interest, reward and behaviour compared; incentive chains; dependency; precedent search | [[sec:a3-incentives]] |

The evidence was the governance documents, the Parliament roster, the facilities head's interview of 13 September 2026, operator accounts and visits, Kadamba's scans, Yuktāhār's registers and six student respondents; earlier episodes are press summaries (candidate).

The first draft (6 September 2026) held a taxonomy, the 26-domain matrix, five incentive chains, bargaining analysis, a precedent search and a resource table, with ten open questions logged and answered that day (sleeping collective leverage became exercised-but-unmeasured). Revisions followed on 27 September (twice) and 28 September.

### Decision-Making Authority {#sec-a3-authority}

#### Formal governance structure

The Mess Committee sets the menu about a month ahead with student input; its separateness from the Mess Office is confirmed, a faculty chair rests on a press summary (candidate), and authority beyond the menu is unconfirmed. The Mess Office runs the registration platform (closing about four days before service) and issues operational policy; its one action on record, the December 2024 tightening of cancellation rules, was reportedly its alone and motivated by staff workload (candidate). The cap of five cancellations per meal type per month, four days ahead, is confirmed.

Academic administration sets the class schedule, including the 8:30 start that overlaps breakfast. Its authority is total, it has no formal relationship with any mess-governance actor, and nobody has raised the overlap with it: inattention, not refusal. Exams, events and holidays reach Yuktāhār only if raised in the planning meeting (confirmed).

The Student Parliament keeps a Mess Secretary and Deputy Secretary for Yuktāhār, Kadamba and Palash (single-sourced) and meets a Mess Warden (title unconfirmed as the Warden above). The CDS team, the counterpart operators name, supplied Kadamba's April 2026 scans (confirmed); whether it is the Mess Office is unconfirmed. Whether quality control can penalise is unknown ([[sec:a4]]).

The Kadamba chain runs from CFS to CDS, the contractor Prism (which wins by tender) and, inside Prism, from operations manager to manager, supervisor and service staff (single-sourced). One account names a head of CDS and an earlier interview a different CDS Chair; whether these are two roles or one misnamed is open. [[fig:a3-command]] shows the chain and Yuktāhār's flat contrast.

![Kadamba's command chain, with the facility team's side channel and Yuktāhār's flat contrast.|Source: the Kadamba manager's account and the team's first-hand account (single-sourced).](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d23-kadamba-command-chain.png){w=88 #a3-command}

The "Mess Council" is most likely a naming artefact. Sources describe possibly two bodies called Mess Committee (one inside the Parliament, one faculty-led) and operators name a "student committee" that picks the menu (single-sourced); none is assumed to be another. For breakfast timing, the cut-off and contract terms beyond the tender no source says who proposes, approves or can veto: itself a finding.

#### Operator-side authority

The operators' accounts show a layered actor, not one box.

- Contract and price terms sit with owners or the managing director; Kadamba's manager does not know the plate rate (single-sourced).
- Quantity rules are the operator's, with no institutional approval evidenced ([[sec:a4]]). The in-service top-up sits with the supervisor at Kadamba, the daily meeting at Yuktāhār and the manager alone at Bakul, who phones a kitchen 8 km away.
- Re-cooking, reuse and supplier choice are the operator's: Yuktāhār's Wastage Book sets an impurity norm per raw item (confirmed); Kadamba cut quantities and bought outside during an LPG shortage (single-sourced).
- Menu content is where knowledge exceeds authority. The Bakul manager, with about 20 years in North Indian food, says students pick pairings that do not work (kachori belongs with chole;
- Yuktāhār is flat, with peers cross-verifying registers (confirmed); Kadamba's supervisor decides with no staff voice described.

The operator holds high local authority over quantity, timing and reuse, and none over the rules that shape how many students register.

### Formal and Informal Power {#sec-a3-informal}

Four informal actors lack standing yet influence. Roommates and friends change attendance slightly (six respondents) and eat in place of absent registrants, which Kadamba's manager calls routine (single-sourced). Mess Cell is a self-organised resale market.

Late diners hold a small routine power: scans after 9:30 recur on every April day, and 25 came after close on one biryani day (single-sourced). Kadamba also cooks about 30 portions for 30 to 50 people outside the registration, such as night-shift security, and about half its staff eat the same food (single-sourced), absorbing part of the gap unreported.

### Incentives and Motivations {#sec-a3-incentives}

#### Stated interest, what the system rewards and observed behaviour

Two operators assumed to share one billing structure take opposite stances ([[tab:a3-incentives]]): Yuktāhār lowers cost by learning from records, Kadamba applies a fixed ratio as an obligation. The difference tracks management culture, not the payment rule, making operator culture a candidate lever.

#### Incentive chains

1. Billing follows registration, not attendance, so nothing gives a student reason to register only when intending to attend; Kadamba registrations stay flat across the week while turnout varies (confirmed, records; [[sec:a5]]).
2. Skip Meal returns nothing and resale returns value, so a student with notice prefers resale: of six respondents none used Skip Meal for a return and four used Mess Cell (confirmed).
3. A walk-in price about 70 per cent above the registered rate (Rs 81 against Rs 48, vegetarian) gives a reason to register under uncertainty, inflating counts independently of no-shows; all six respondents register in advance. Yuktāhār's breakfast register shows none to twelve unregistered paying diners a day, usually four or fewer, twelve on the peak day of whom nine paid (confirmed).
4. The Mess Office's workload incentive produced tighter cancellation rules, which cut flexibility and plausibly strengthened the pattern targeted: a candidate fix that fails.
5. Academic administration feels none of the overlap's consequences, so the issue never reaches its agenda (confirmed).
6. The payment basis decides which side of the gap an operator sits on. Paid per registered plate, an operator is paid for the whole registration and cooks part of it (70 per cent at Kadamba, 70 to 80 at Bakul), so the gap is margin and accuracy has no financial appeal; paid per served plate, it bears every portion cooked and not eaten and wants accuracy. No operator has stated a position on the cut-off.

#### Bargaining power and dependency

Operators use registration only as a ceiling: their planning input is Yuktāhār's books, Kadamba's fixed ratio and Bakul's weekly ratio with a floor of about 70 per cent, none calibrated to recorded turnout. At Kadamba a 70 per cent first batch against 37.1 per cent turnout implies about 1.9 times over-preparation before top-up; by stream, about 757 daily vegetarian registrations against about 245 eaten gives about 2.2 times, and about 315 non-vegetarian against about 153 about 1.4 times (candidate).

Operators bargain with suppliers (Bakul switched paneer brands twice; Yuktāhār's impurity norm, rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months; single-sourced); no bargaining with the institution is evidenced, and none has objected to the four-day cut-off.

#### Historical precedent

- Bakul's creation, triggered by Kadamba's non-vegetarian capacity ceiling, is a confirmed structural response to a limit (who raised it is unknown).
- "Let Them Eat Frogs" (November 2024): a frog was reportedly found in Kadamba's chicken biryani and the Mess Office cancelled non-vegetarian meals without stating the reason (candidate).
- The December 2024 tightening was reportedly triggered by Mess Office workload and a Kadamba renovation, with no evidenced consultation. Ping's opinion form returned majority disappointment and Parliament's representatives reportedly met the Office twice before it took effect; it went ahead (candidate). Whether this is the organised effort heard of earlier is unconfirmed.
- Registration was made non-compulsory and the cap raised from five to ten during an LPG shortage and holidays, then reversed (single-sourced).
- Yuktāhār's waste-tracking change (August to December of its first year) is the one evidenced case of an actor cutting its own waste. Per-item recording met fear of blame ("they are catching us"), weighing was made blame-free and discussed daily, and by December staff reported waste falling (confirmed). It dropped a commercial ERP ("I want to remain close to the data") and stayed inside the kitchen.
- "Water Mess" (2023): drinking-water complaints went unaddressed for months, reportedly until 38 or more typhoid cases forced a response (secondhand; candidate).

No precedent was found for breakfast timing or class-timing coordination (unknown, not zero).

### Who Can and Cannot Influence Decisions {#sec-a3-influence}

#### Power asymmetries

Six asymmetries stand out. (1) The Mess Office can tighten registration and cancellation rules without a confirmed consultation step. (2) Operators decide how much is cooked and reused; students reach them only through the menu and the counter. (3) Operators adjust on the day while students are locked in four days ahead: Kadamba keeps raw material ready to cook in 10 to 15 minutes, Bakul phones a kitchen 20 to 40 minutes away, and Kadamba orders perishables a day ahead and dry goods a week ahead (single-sourced).

#### Consequence and control

Students carry high consequence (wasted food, lost money, energy crashes) with low power: beyond five cancellations a month the charge cannot be switched off, and Skip Meal tells the kitchen but does not remove it (confirmed).

Operators absorb consequence with no power over its cause. Kadamba's scans show one crowd still rising at 9:29 ([[sec:a5]]); its rule of thumb of about 150 diners in the last ten minutes matches, and the records put 78.8 scans a day (19.8 per cent) between 9:20 and 9:29 (confirmed, records). The Bakul manager places his rush at 8:00 to 8:30 with 10 to 15 in the last ten minutes, yet holds food for about 50 at 9:20, since a top-up from Hafizpet could not arrive in time (candidate; no Bakul scans). At Kadamba, 30 to 50 outside diners and half the staff are uncounted absorbers and the garbage contractor removes the rest; cooked food lasts three to four hours, so surplus cannot move to another day, and Yuktāhār reuses it as a new dish, curd or batter (confirmed).

#### Coalitions and conflicts

Students and operators may share an interest in a shorter cut-off, since the four-day lock serves no evidenced procurement need (asymmetry 3); no operator has stated a position, so the coalition is potential (candidate).

A second conflict runs over menu criteria: students choose for diet, protein and preference, the Bakul manager for tradition. Only his side is heard, so it is a conflict between formal selection power and consumption knowledge, not evidence of student preference.

#### Resource control

Students control none of these resources. The evidence of the pattern sits in the operators' records and the CDS scan records, and neither reaches the rule-setters.

#### Information flows: who holds what, and where it stops

Information breaks in four places, each at the operator. Upward, Yuktāhār's records end inside the kitchen, with no route to CDS, the Mess Office, any Committee, CFS or billing; the safety checks carry nothing about quantity back out, and the facilities head's awareness of low turnout never reached a billing discussion (confirmed). Downward, the calendar reaches Yuktāhār only by word of mouth. From student to kitchen, whether a Skip Meal declaration changes what Kadamba cooks is in conflict: earlier findings say it does, Kadamba's operator says it cooks on registrations only (single-sourced).

Yuktāhār's site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50 per cent (confirmed as belief); his September register shows 17.7 to 40.2 per cent, mean about 28 (confirmed, records), while his lunch figure above 70 per cent matches. The Bakul manager's estimate of at most about 60 per cent, below his own floor, is an upper bound if Bakul resembles the recorded halls at 28 to 37 per cent (single-sourced; no records), and he blames taste, menu and students not waking, untested with residents.

#### Leverage analysis

The Mess Office holds power and leverage over student flexibility rules (a small change with a system-wide effect in December 2024, candidate), a different lever from waste. The operator holds high leverage and local power over waste and no rule-making power, in two kinds. Parameter leverage (first-batch ratio, top-up threshold, batching) is fast and local: Prism's rule and the supervisor at Kadamba, the daily meeting at Yuktāhār, the manager above his floor at Bakul; tightening it alone hides the gap further upstream.

#### Sleeping stakeholders

Academic administration is the clearest case: total authority over class timing, no relationship to any mess actor and no incentive to engage. A faculty chair of the Mess Committee, if confirmed, may have informal access to it; no use is evidenced.

### Implications for System Behaviour {#sec-a3-implications}

This distribution of power plausibly sustains the Breakfast Paradox: billing without an attendance incentive removes pressure on the student, the one governance action on record may have tightened the pattern (candidate), and the actor best placed to resolve the overlap is disconnected from every governance actor.

The stabiliser is in the kitchen: each operator cooks a fraction of registrations, watches the counter and tops up (confirmed at Yuktāhār; single-sourced elsewhere). A run-out is visible and routes a complaint towards the managing director, while a surplus is silent (candidate). The one actor who measures the gap daily cannot change the rules that create it, and no channel from kitchen records to policy exists.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a3-decisions}

Decision: Power is assessed one decision at a time and each claim states whether it rests on formal authority or observed influence; a single score per actor was rejected because the same actor is decisive on one question and absent on the next.

Decision: Power and leverage are analysed separately; combining them, as the first version did, hid that the actor with most leverage over waste has no rule-making power.

Decision: Open questions are logged and left unanswered, with raw discovery notes kept apart from the polished brief, instead of guessing at ten unknowns.

Decision: Chart placements are shown as ordinal with a warning against false precision, since no measured coordinates exist for any actor.

Decision: Overlapping or unverified body names are kept apart and none is assumed to be another (Mess Office and CDS, the two bodies called Mess Committee, the operators' student committee, the two names for the head of CDS); the "Mess Council" is not treated as an actor.

Decision: The payment basis of operators (registered or served plates) is left unresolved and dependent conclusions are labelled candidate, because the only evidence is one Kadamba remark and one Bakul report, which pull apart.
