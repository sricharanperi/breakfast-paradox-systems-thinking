# Introduction {#ch-intro}

## Background and Motivation {#sec-background}

Every student at the International Institute of Information Technology, Hyderabad, who lives in a hostel is registered for a meal at each of the four dining halls on the campus. The registration is automatic and the charge follows it. The halls are run by caterers appointed by the institution, and each caterer cooks a quantity of food before the first diner arrives. Breakfast is the meal where this arrangement shows its strain most clearly. A large share of the students who are registered for breakfast do not eat it, yet the food is cooked, the meal is billed, and the surplus leaves the kitchen in ways that no office of the institution tracks.

The course in Systems Thinking and Design offered four campus puzzles, and the team chose the Breakfast Paradox. Its opening description presents a student whose alarm rings several times before 7:45, who stayed awake late the night before, who chooses sleep over breakfast, and who by 11:15 is hungry and unable to concentrate. The hall serves breakfast from 7:30 to 9:30, classes begin at 8:30, and only about a third of the hostel turns up. The remaining food is thrown away. The description names a single cause, the clash between the early class and the late night, and a chain of consequences that follows from it.

The project treated that description as a set of claims to be tested. Over about six weeks the team studied how breakfast registration, billing, cooking, serving and disposal fit together, who is able to change each part, and where a change would do the most good. This document records that work from the first interview to the final recommendation: the methods used at each step, the processes followed, the deliverables produced and the decisions that shaped them.

## Problem Statement {#sec-problem}

The puzzle as given asks why students skip a meal that they have registered for. The first phase of the study showed that this question is too narrow. Registration at the halls is a default that students did not choose, so non-attendance cannot be read as a decision about a meal they selected. The operators, for their part, do not cook to the registration count; each applies its own rule of thumb. The problem addressed in this document is therefore stated as follows.

> Breakfast registration at IIIT Hyderabad does not reflect who will eat, by roughly two thirds, while lunch under the same rules mostly does. Each kitchen estimates its own conversion from registrations to cooked food, the records that could calibrate that estimate are already kept, and none of those records reaches the people who set the registration and billing rules. The project asks why this arrangement persists and what a small group of students could propose to change it.

## Aim and Objectives {#sec-aim}

The aim of the project is to explain the persistence of the gap between breakfast registration and breakfast attendance as a property of the whole system, and to recommend an intervention that can be tested on the campus. The objectives are to:

1. define the boundary of the breakfast system and separate its visible symptoms from their underlying causes;
2. identify the people and bodies that take part in the system, and establish who holds authority and who holds leverage over each decision;
3. trace how a registration becomes a quantity of cooked food, and how the system behaves over a morning, a week and a month;
4. build a model of the system that distinguishes feedback loops which close from those which only appear to;
5. reframe the problem from the evidence and identify the places where intervention would be most effective;
6. generate, test on paper and evaluate alternative interventions, and recommend one with a sequence, a pilot and a plan for withdrawing the project team.

The research questions that operationalise these objectives, and the activities that answer each of them, are set out in [[ch:method]].

## Scope and Boundary {#sec-scope}

The study covers breakfast in the four dining halls (Kadamba, Palash, Bakul and Yuktāhār) as it is registered, billed, cooked, served and disposed of. Lunch and dinner are outside the boundary and serve as a comparison, since the same portal, rules and caterer apply to them. The class timetable is also outside the boundary but is kept on the maps because it shapes the morning. Quantitative evidence is available for two halls only: Kadamba, from a month of scan records, and Yuktāhār, from photographed attendance registers. Bakul is described from an interview with its manager and from no records, and Palash is recorded as a candidate kitchen that shares its off-site kitchen with Bakul. These limits are repeated where they affect a claim. The boundary itself is drawn and justified in [[sec:a1]].

## Structure of the Document {#sec-structure}

The course framework is arranged into four phases, ten activities and fifteen deliverables, and the order of the document follows it. [[ch:framework]] sets out the framework in full, with the plan and timeline for each activity and the standards of evidence. [[ch:litreview]] reviews the concepts and methods from the literature on which the analysis relies. [[ch:method]] states the research objectives and questions, the interview guides, the fieldwork and the data handling. [[ch:phase1]] to [[ch:phase4]] report the ten activities, each under the course framework's own key activities, with the methods, the process followed and the findings. [[ch:synthesis]] brings the findings together, [[ch:discussion]] discusses them, [[ch:limits]] sets out the limitations and ethical considerations, and [[ch:conclusion]] concludes with directions for further work.
