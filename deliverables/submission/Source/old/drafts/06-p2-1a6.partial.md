### Actors, Process, Resources and Constraints {#sec-a6-actors}

[[sec:a2]] describes the cast and [[sec:a3]] the authority structure. The map adds three features. The operator layer is drawn as three operators, not one box. A second demand stream exists: Kadamba cooks about 30 extra portions for 30 to 50 outsiders and about half its staff eat at the mess (single-sourced), planned demand and an uncounted sink for surplus (candidate); Yuktāhār's faculty and staff diners number zero to two a breakfast (confirmed). Boundary actors (suppliers, the garbage contractor, the site team relaying complaints) carry material without governing.

#### The operator layer in cross-section

[[sec:a4]] describes each kitchen. Against the records ([[fig:a6-batch]]), a 70 per cent batch at Kadamba is about 1.9 times the 37.1 per cent who eat, and 2.6 times on a Sunday (candidate arithmetic: a stated rule applied to a month whose batches were not recorded). Yuktāhār's site head believes breakfast runs at about 50 per cent where his register averages about 28.

The halls differ in learning, not data. Both on-site kitchens know on the day how many are eating, but only Yuktāhār records the outcome where it returns to the next decision about the same meal, and treats a smaller leftover as money saved. Bakul corrects against surplus by one person's judgement, down to a floor. Each operator won its hall by tender and is paid monthly as a lump sum on plates, with deductions (single-sourced); whether a plate is registered or served is the open question of [[sec:a3]]. Bakul and Palash share one approved off-site kitchen until a new shared kitchen opens, expected about December 2026.

#### Registration, procurement and service

[[sec:a1]] sets out the rules and [[sec:a5]] the counts. Four points belong to the map. First, the four-day lock applies to registration, not buying. Yuktāhār's vegetables arrive two to three times a week and its milk daily (confirmed); its stock register shows fortnightly groceries while the operator describes buying the week before use, unreconciled. Kadamba buys dry goods a week ahead, some vegetables two to three days ahead and perishables the day before (single-sourced), so only dry goods are committed before the close (candidate). What binds waste is the cooked first batch, a ratio of registrations: raw stock is recoverable, cooked food lasts three to four hours, and a batch can be topped up but never shrunk. 

Second, the institution's account, backed by a second source, is that Skip Meal declarations reach the kitchen; yet the toggle does not remove the charge, no operator names it as a planning input, and Kadamba's operator says it cooks on registrations alone (single-sourced). The conflict stays open; one reading fits both, that a declaration lowers the count before the operator reads it and the operator treats any count as the obligation (candidate). Third, counts arriving "in weekly blocks" may or may not be the students' automatic monthly booking. Fourth, exemptions exist for internships, doctoral students and long-term medical need, and when the scanner fails Kadamba's staff write ID and name by hand, so the scan record may miss some diners (single-sourced).

#### The Yuktāhār book system

Yuktāhār runs on paper books that feed each other. Every book was photographed (confirmed); the connections are the operator's account (single-sourced). The day's registrations go into the Grocery Book header. At the planning meeting the matching-week Wastage Book entry, the weekday register and the month's pattern become a per-dish list in kilograms from the store's ingredient book, which is the issue in the Grocery Book; the stock register balances it against receipts and it drives purchasing. After each meal the outcome goes into the Wastage Book (raw issued, cooked, left, percentage, run-out time, re-cook, plate waste plus leftover) and onto a wastage board whose target is a falling waste share; the page is read again when the menu day returns two weeks later. 

![How Yuktāhār's books feed each other, and where the chain stops.|Source: photographed books (confirmed); connections from the operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-book-network.png){w=100 #a6-book-network}

Two rules hold the books together: the store book's issue must match the physical book's cooked quantity (4.5 kg of toor dal issued, balance 49.45 kg, agreed on the day checked), and every book is kept by one person and cross-checked by another, "not to find fault". The books reconcile materials, not diners: for 3 September the Wastage Book and the attendance register, kept by different people, record 77 and 110 breakfasts (confirmed).

### Waste, Environment and the Routes of Surplus {#sec-a6-waste}

Kadamba's waste goes into a dustbin emptied by a garbage contractor, with no composting (single-sourced, plain and repeated). The facilities head had described an outside compost facility; the operator's account is first-hand and more recent, and the contractor's onward route is unverified. Yuktāhār's route was not stated. Suspected contamination goes to a laboratory, and Kadamba holds an external five-star Eat Right Campus rating. Yuktāhār weighs waste per item and meal and sets the next matching day's issue from it (confirmed); Kadamba's figure is a quoted percentage with no record seen. No route carries these measurements to the registration rules, billing rules or menu owner.

Surplus is absorbed before the rule-setters see it: reuse at Yuktāhār; outsiders, half the staff and reheating at Kadamba (candidate); the walk-in buffer; latecomers after the nominal close; finally the bin. Each does real good, so the problem persists without anyone ignoring it and no fix should work by removing one. Nobody has measured the cost of the 20,221 Kadamba breakfasts registered and never eaten in April, including the landfill and methane cost of food sent to general garbage.

### The Service Timeline and Blueprint {#sec-a6-timeline}

[[sec:a5]] reports the Kadamba crowd curve; here the question is how each operator's picture of the crest compares with it. The Kadamba operator's rule of thumb has the same shape (about 100 early, 100 through the middle, 150 in the last ten minutes), but the records show 19.8 per cent of scans, about 79 a day, in the last ten minutes against about 43 per cent in his example (candidate). Bakul's manager places his main rush at about 8:00 to 8:30, with 10 to 15 people in the last ten minutes (single-sourced), so curves may differ by hall (candidate).

The service blueprint ([[fig:a6-blueprint]]) separates what the student sees from what the kitchen does. Behind the line the kitchen reacts within a meal. At Kadamba the supervisor reads what is left of each item beside the scan count and tells the chef how much more to cook, and the counter asks diners to wait five minutes. Yuktāhār projects from its first half hour, checks every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close and opens a second counter in surges (checks and counter confirmed, projection single-sourced). Kadamba reopens its front door for five to ten minutes for latecomers (25 came after the close on one biryani day); after 9:30 latecomers use the back door and the mess cannot refuse them (confirmed, from a team member's experience as a diner and the operator's account).

![Service blueprint: what the student sees above the line, what the kitchen does below it, and the one signal that crosses it.|Source: authors' analysis; Bakul differences from the manager's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-blueprint.png){w=100 #a6-blueprint}

The response runs one way: it adds food, counters and minutes and cannot take back a cooked batch. No signal crosses the line of visibility from one meal to the next, so nothing tells the student or the rule-setters what was left over. One artefact crosses at every meal, a wrapped display plate showing the full menu (confirmed); it carries the menu, never quantity.

### Conflicts and Dependencies {#sec-a6-conflicts}

Students depend on the operator for food; no operator depends on any one student. The institution depends on students registering accurately although nothing in billing requires it, and on operators for the only item-level record of what is eaten and wasted, for which it has no channel. Operators depend on the institution for the menu, rules and exam and event calendar, which reaches them only informally; Bakul also depends on the road from Hafizpet. #### Food-safety checkpoints

Assurance is routine and runs before any laboratory step. The Kadamba sequence ([[sec:a4]]) is cooking temperature, a 72-hour sample, a wrapped display plate, serving temperature per item and a tasting by the facility team, with a weekly quality-control inspection and a daily reading of dissolved solids in water. Plate and tasting are confirmed by observation, the rest is single-sourced; whether faculty visitors to the store are the weekly inspection, and who the facility team is, are unknown. Yuktāhār has a tasting team and logs fridge temperatures three times a day, all within 1.4 to 3.5 °C in September (confirmed). None of this covers quantity. The batch must be cooked, sampled, displayed and tasted before service opens at 7:30, from cooking that starts at 5:30, so its size is committed before a diner arrives (candidate).

### Formal and Informal Structure {#sec-a6-structure}

CDS sits under CFS. Low-intensity feedback goes straight to the CFS Head; high-intensity feedback escalates through the Student Council to the Committee. Below CDS sits a contract relation that governance diagrams usually omit: CDS tenders each hall to an outsourced vendor. [[fig:a6-governance]] separates three routes. The complaint route works for same-incident fixes (at Kadamba an email to the director passes to the manager and into a messaging group the site team also reports into; at Bakul it runs from the CDS team to the manager, kitchen and managing director). The escalation route has produced one real result. The rule-change route does not exist. [[sec:a3]] draws Kadamba's command chain.

![CDS, CFS and the vendors: the complaint route that works for incidents, the escalation route that produced the menu rotation, and the rule-change route that does not exist.|Source: authors' analysis of interviews and posters.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-governance.png){w=100 #a6-governance}

Yuktāhār has no hierarchy and everyone does every job (confirmed); there is no leave policy, and colleagues absorb the load raised at the daily meeting. One project manager trained the staff at his farmhouse, and staff describe a family-like culture (single-sourced). 

The escalation route's one result is the clearest working feedback in the project: a long-standing complaint about a single fixed menu went through the Student Council to the Committee and came out as a menu that rotates every two weeks (operators confirm). The rotation makes Yuktāhār's matching-day lookup possible, so a governance loop enables a kitchen loop. The menu is chosen by students and the committee (single-sourced). Operators see which items waste each time they appear (uttapam, set dosa and upma at Kadamba, while puri, bonda, vada and idli are eaten) and have no route to the menu owner. 

Bakul's manager frames the issue as how a menu is formed (his view only, the students' side unheard). In his account students choose the combinations and he objects to pairings: kachori belongs with chole, and Raj Kachori is listed with chana where he would serve aloo curry. He sets students' reasoning (balanced diet, protein, preference) against his own (traditional and regional combinations, taste, what is eaten, after about 20 years in North Indian food) and mentioned a protein target of 30 to 40 g a meal set by someone unknown. He also sees that pav bhaji brings more students and muesli and Chocos "go easily". Whether residents share his view is untested. The menu owner lacks two kinds of knowledge the operator holds, what is eaten and what goes together, and neither has a route to it (candidate).

Outside the formal structure the resale market is a student messaging group, the student publication has no confirmed power to act, and at least one unofficial tool manages registration outside the portal. Academic administration has no formal or informal relationship to any part of mess governance, a confirmed absence ([[sec:a2]], [[sec:a3]]).

### The Class Schedule and Who Else Needs Breakfast {#sec-a6-class}

The project had treated 8:30 classes as an obstacle, which is probably unfair: the timing may suit harder lectures, let a faculty member teach once and keep the day free, or fit every programme into five days on shared halls. The map does not recommend moving class time; the 8:30 start is a fixed condition. The exam and event calendar is different: it reaches a kitchen only if someone raises it in the planning meeting, an absent information flow, not a scheduling clash.

Faculty and staff demand is small and counted at Yuktāhār and larger at Kadamba, where it helps absorb surplus. Whether it is a written entitlement or a norm, whether anyone counts or bills it, and whether grounds and housekeeping staff who start before seven eat at any mess in volume are unknown. A past organised email effort by students produced a change; today that energy goes into reselling registrations. Kadamba's manager independently describes students who register "even if you are in the village" while a friend eats in their place (single-sourced). Whether the resale group ever raised billing with the Committee or CDS is unconfirmed.

### The System Already Solved This Once {#sec-a6-precedent}

During a cooking-gas shortage the mess cut to one item at the same price and the cancellation limit rose from five to ten a month. During holidays and long weekends such as the campus festival, registration stopped being required: students could walk in and scan, or register a day or two ahead, at any of the four messes, and someone who neither registered nor came was not charged (single-sourced; the holiday part is disputed, since the team's own account is that students stay registered through holidays). If it holds, the institution has more than once run a different way of matching food to demand and has not kept it: "nobody has worked this out" becomes "this was worked out and switched off".

The usual reason, protecting the vendor's four-day lead time, is weakened: kitchens already absorb daily unpredictability by cooking a fraction of registrations and topping up, and Kadamba orders perishables one day ahead. The likelier reason is commercial: if operators are paid on registered plates, a walk-in model moves the cost of a no-show from student to operator (candidate, pending the plate basis). The exception also worked because it was rare and short and the live top-up was already doing most of the work (candidate).

### Bottlenecks and What Actually Closes the Loop {#sec-a6-bottlenecks}

The bottleneck is the cooked first batch, a ratio of a count that is itself a standing booking, which can be topped up but never shrunk, against a crowd that at Kadamba crests in the last fifteen minutes ([[fig:a6-bottleneck]]). At Bakul the arm is longer: the batch leaves Hafizpet before any turnout signal exists, and late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals. At Kadamba the safety sequence fixes the batch before 7:30, so the constraint costs quality as well as food. The 9:30 close is a precise cut-off against a crowd that peaks right before it, and in practice runs to 9:35 or 9:45. No formal channel carries the calendar to operators, and waste, measured in detail by one operator, signals nothing outside that kitchen.

### Culture, Health and What Is Not a Problem {#sec-a6-culture}

Breakfast menus lean toward nutrient-focused items such as dal, sprouts and plain rotis, away from the food people crave, a plausible reason for turnout below lunch alongside the billing incentive; Yuktāhār confirms that breakfast runs far below lunch on the same days under the same rules, so both can hold. Bakul's manager offers that students may dislike the taste or menu, or may not wake up or want to come (his view): the second matches the late sleep students most often cite, the first is his hypothesis, since the six student interviews do not support food quality as a primary reason and none of those students is known to live at Bakul.

A recent outbreak of hand, foot and mouth disease reportedly spread across both hostels, cause unconfirmed. Skipped breakfast did not cause it (the disease spreads by contact), though shared mess halls and washrooms would worsen an outbreak. Some students skip for reasons unrelated to the system: fasting, a belief that they are healthy enough, habits with no formal breakfast, dietary or religious practice, or discomfort eating certain foods such as egg in a shared, visible place. No fix should be judged by bringing turnout near 100 per cent; the measure is whether what is cooked tracks what is eaten.

### Key Findings of the System Map {#sec-a6-findings}

- The institution has run a working fix for the core problem and not kept it as the default; the reason looks like who pays for a no-show more than four days' notice ([[sec:a6-precedent]]).
- The gap is measured and acted on inside the kitchen every day, and no route carries it to the rule-setters; Yuktāhār is the most data-rich actor and one of the least powerful.
- The three halls run three planning rules; the two on-site kitchens under identical rules are the clearest comparison.
- Breakfast is the problem meal, with arrivals cresting in the last fifteen minutes, so shortage and waste share a morning.
- Absorbers (reuse, outsiders, bin, resale) hide the cost, and each does real good.
- The class schedule probably has good reasons of its own, and part of the paradox is legitimate variation in how people eat.

### The Closure Test and the Loop Dashboard {#sec-a6-loops}

A causal loop exists only if the last variable in a chain causes the first. A loop with an even number of negative links reinforces whatever starts it; one with an odd number pushes back toward a steady state. A chain that never closes is neither, so it is drawn with a dashed closing link. Each mechanism was asked three questions: is every link supported by evidence on its own, does the last link really cause the first, and what is the delay around the cycle. Eighteen mechanisms were tested, L1 to L17 plus L3b, a reinforcing variant of L3. An earlier analysis without operator-side evidence treated the kitchen as a black box; opening it showed that the best-evidenced feedback lives there. ### Loops That Close {#sec-a6-closed}

#### L11: Yuktāhār's waste-learning loop

Cooked quantity above what is eaten raises the leftover and run-out time written in the Wastage Book. A planning meeting held twice a day reads that record against the matching past day (menu weeks one and three are identical, as are two and four), the month's turnout pattern, events, and registrations counted as regular plus Jain plus cash meals, and sets the next day's issue. A larger leftover lowers the next issue, which lowers the surplus: one negative link, so the loop balances, and every link is evidenced by the photographed pages and the site head's account. Shown the 18.7 per cent attendance of Sunday 6 September, he said the quantity would be cut "not even next month, next week." Delays: the plan is fixed the day before, and learning lags one to two weeks.

![L11: Yuktāhār's Wastage Book loop, its two delays, and the preconditions that keep the record honest.|Source: Yuktāhār registers (confirmed); operator account.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l11.png){w=100 #a6-l11}

The loop exists because weighing, begun in August, was made blame-free after staff first feared blame ("they are catching us"); by December staff reported lower waste (self-report, no before-and-after figures). Peer cross-checking by staff who rotate across every job and the two-book rule keep the record honest, and it all runs on paper, a commercial ERP system having been dropped as too costly for data already in the books (single-sourced). Four limits apply. The site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50 per cent while his register shows 17.7 to 40.2 per cent. The loop adjusts quantity, never registration. It rests on one operator's account from one visit. And Kadamba keeps per-dish gram standards (four idlis from about 120 g of batter, 100 g of sambar) but no learning record: "We don't have that idea… it was wasted many times." Operator stance may explain why the loop is present at one hall, absent at another and bounded at the third (candidate). Bakul's manager sets the first batch at 70 to 80 per cent of registrations by item from weekly attendance history, so a surplus can lower next week's share, only to a floor of about 70 per cent; whether the review is written down is unknown.

#### L12: counter monitoring and top-up

Within a meal, food at the counter falls, a periodic check registers the risk of running out, the counter asks for more, and food rises again: one negative link, balancing, described by all three operators ([[fig:a6-l12]]). Kadamba's supervisor watches each item against its gram standard and asks the chef for portions, combining registrations with turnout so far; top-ups take 10 to 15 minutes (single-sourced). Yuktāhār staff check every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close, open a second counter at surges and re-cook by the run-out rule (the 9:00 re-cook on 3 September is on record, confirmed). Its projection from the first half hour (candidate at breakfast) samples the part of the morning least like the close: 15.3 per cent of Kadamba's April scans fall between 7:30 and 8:00, so a flat projection gives about 240 of the roughly 398 daily scans, about 40 per cent short (candidate arithmetic). Bakul's manager checks about every 30 minutes and phones Hafizpet: a call, a cook and a drive of 20 to 40 minutes (single-sourced).

![L12: top-up at three halls, and the delay that decides how well each works.|Source: operator accounts (single-sourced); Yuktāhār registers (confirmed).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l12.png){w=100 #a6-l12}

The delay decides the behaviour. On site, a top-up requested when the final surge shows lands at or after the 9:30 close, so the loop cannot track the crest and is pre-empted only by a buffer cooked in advance, which is L14's hedge. Off site no top-up can land late, so the only hedge is food already held: Bakul's manager keeps food for about 50 at 9:20 against the 10 to 15 he says arrive in the last ten minutes, a reserve that is, as a candidate reading, the structural consequence of the transport delay, an insurance premium paid in surplus. The loop is asymmetric: it corrects only upward and protects against shortage without reducing surplus, which is held and reheated at Kadamba (the open branch of L13) or held hot at Bakul at about 60 to 72 °C, where the late reserve is also the food held longest and likely the softest (candidate). Reducing surplus falls to the slower L11 where it exists, and otherwise to nothing.

#### L4: the menu rotation response

Every link of this governance loop traces to a direct account. A single fixed menu produced menu fatigue; the feedback reached the CDS Student Council, which escalated it to the Student Parliament, and the CDS Committee approved a biweekly rotation. Variety rose and, by the facilities head's account, fatigue complaints fell; operators' records confirm the two-week cycle. The escalation structure can close a loop when a complaint is loud enough, which makes the gaps elsewhere diagnostic: the registration gap produces no loud, immediate signal. A live test is under way at Kadamba, where a student complained that dropped dinner sambar was removed "while they are taking more money from us" (single-sourced); the change has not been reversed. Menu and registration governance have separate owners, so L4 shows the mechanism exists, not that it has been applied to the booking.

### Candidate Loops {#sec-a6-candidates}

Each of these would close if its last link held, but that link is assumed, so each is drawn dashed and not counted as a loop.

**L1, speculative registration and the resale safety net.** A student registers without firm intent, producing an unused registration that feeds the resale market. Two respondents show the first links ("Kadamba veg breakfast costs around Rs 48 and I book it every day and never avail it"; "I have both bought and sold registrations"), and Kadamba's manager describes the transfer from the kitchen side (single-sourced); [[sec:a5]] draws the loop. The first three links are confirmed. The closing link, that a lower net cost causes more speculation, is stated by no respondent, and the strongest student evidence points to securing a scarce slot ("I do register for breakfast for the whole month because Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full"). Registration also needs no motive, since it is automatic, so Kadamba's flat count of about 1,070 a day is what the default produces (L13): L1 is a behaviour on top of the default and does not drive registration.

**L9, waste absorption.** Surplus passes through five routes before the bin: reuse at Yuktāhār (idli becomes upma or poha for lunch, batter returns to the fridge, milk is set as curd; confirmed); outsiders and staff at Kadamba (single-sourced; whether their 30 portions sit inside or on top of the 70 per cent batch is unconfirmed); held and reheated food (the team's judgement); latecomers at the back door (L15); and the contractor's dustbin. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown. April averaged about 1,072 registered and 398 eaten a day (confirmed), so a 70 per cent first batch of about 750 leaves roughly 350 portions before any top-up (candidate arithmetic); by line the batch is about 2.2 times what was eaten for vegetarian (757 registered, 245 eaten) and 1.4 times for non-vegetarian (315 and 153). The links that would close the loop, that absorbed surplus suppresses pressure and that this permits more unused registration, have never been seen to fire; L17 is the better-grounded version.

**L17, kitchen competence shifts the burden.** Operators discounting registrations and topping up live (L11, L12) relieve the gap's visible consequences every morning: queues clear, shortages are re-cooked, surplus is reused, fed to outsiders and staff, held or binned. Because each morning is handled locally, no signal accumulates upstream, which plausibly reduces pressure for the fundamental fix, a booking that tracks the daily decision to eat ([[fig:a6-l17]]). The shift is partly benign: the surplus has beneficiaries, so cutting the first batch takes their meals unless it plans for them, and an operator that treats the count as an obligation it is paid to meet has no motive to raise a signal. Bakul's floor turns a risk of visible shortage into a silent surplus that it bears itself, acceptable if it is paid on plates served, as its manager reports (candidate). The symptomatic side is confirmed and the pressure-reduction link assumed, but it is better grounded than L9 because the correction routes are observed. It is the clearest statement of why the system is stable, not broken.

![L17: kitchen competence relieves the symptom each morning, so the fundamental fix never gets pressure.|Source: authors' analysis; the dashed link is assumed.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l17.png){w=100 #a6-l17}

**L3b, Kadamba demand lock-in.** Kadamba's reputation plausibly drives scarcity, and scarcity drives slot-locking for the respondent who registers all month because Kadamba fills up. April is consistent with that, but automatic registration predicts the same pattern (about 1,070 a day, 62.9 per cent unused, vegetarian least used at 32.4 per cent), and the Bakul operator's belief that Kadamba gets 90 to 95 per cent turnout is contradicted for breakfast by the records. The closing link, that locking reinforces the reputation, is the weakest, and the only direct testimony describes a crowded Kadamba as a negative experience. The loop is kept as a plausible reason Kadamba stays saturated while Bakul stays under-registered, unconfirmed.

**L15, the soft close.** At Kadamba, when the front door closes at 9:30 people come in by the back door and the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced). The operator's wording covers dinner as well as breakfast, so breakfast back-door entry is a candidate, supported by the records: 9.2 per cent of April scans came after 9:30, the latest at 10:07 (confirmed). If students learn that the close is soft they arrive later, which builds the late crest and brings more latecomers, a cycle with no negative link. Only the first link is evidenced.

### Chains That Do Not Close {#sec-a6-chains}

These are real, evidenced mechanisms, and none closes. For the first two that is the diagnostic point.

#### L13: from standing booking to fixed-ratio surplus

This is the core chain of the Breakfast Paradox. Registration is mandatory and automatic, running whether or not the student is on campus, and the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead (confirmed). The registered count therefore stays flat, 928 to 1,123 a day at Kadamba in April, while turnout swings with the weekday and breakfast at Yuktāhār runs far below lunch under the same booking ([[sec:a5]]). Whether the booking cycle is weekly, as Kadamba's operator describes it, or monthly, as students experience it, is unconfirmed. The kitchen converts the flat count into a first batch under one of three regimes ([[sec:a4]]), and a fixed ratio over-prepares most where turnout is lowest: 1.9 times what turnout needed overall, 2.6 on Sundays and 1.7 on Tuesdays, 2.2 for vegetarian and 1.4 for non-vegetarian (candidate); at Bakul the manager's own ceiling sits below both his batch and his floor (candidate). Portions drift too: Kadamba's biryani lunch is cooked at 100 per cent, and when some diners take a third piece (150 to 200 g each, unlimited rice) later ones go without; at breakfast bonda, puri, vada and idli "float" above the gram standard (single-sourced).

![L13: the core chain. A flat booking meets a fixed ratio; the quality branch is dashed because its closing link is not evidenced.|Source: authors' analysis of Kadamba scan records and operator accounts.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l13.png){w=100 #a6-l13}

Four delays separate the decision from the meal: the cancellation lead of four days, which decouples the daily decision to eat from the bill; quantity set the day before, against the procurement tiers above; the chef's arrival at 5:30 with live items from 7:00 (Bakul's labour-heavy items may start at Hafizpet around 3 AM for a 7 AM arrival); and the safety sequence, which finishes before any turnout signal exists. The first batch is thus fixed three and a half to four hours before the crest. No link runs from the surplus back to the booking or the rule: operators control quantities, nothing routes their registers to the rule owner, and Kadamba treats the count as the obligation. That is the structural reason the gap persists. L11 and L12 act on the kitchen's conversion, never on the booking, and the same structure gives 56 to 89 per cent at lunch, which is why the problem belongs to breakfast.

The surplus has an open branch at Kadamba. In the team's judgement (candidate) the main quality problem is food cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, one of them the display plate. If lower quality led to more skipping, the branch would close into a reinforcing loop, but that link is not evidenced, so it is dashed. Bakul has a similar branch driven by distance: food held hot softens, as hot parathas steam in the box (single-sourced, one item), and earlier dispatch or a bigger reserve lengthens the holding (candidate). Whether texture changes taste as students judge it, and whether taste moves turnout, is untested with any Bakul resident.

#### L14: from the closing rush to a just-in-case batch

Arrivals at Kadamba crest at the close ([[sec:a5]]): about 79 scans a day fell between 9:20 and 9:29, reaching about nine a minute at 9:29, and Yuktāhār's site head says the rush is always at closing time (both confirmed). Where the crest comes at the close, demand outruns what a top-up delivers in time, so one morning produces shortage and surplus together. Shortage shows as the 9:00 re-cook at Yuktāhār, back-door service at Kadamba and run-outs on the floating items; surplus shows as Bakul's batch and floor under its no-shortage rule and Kadamba's raw reserve held for a quick cook. Kadamba's extra portions are not part of the hedge, since they feed outsiders as a planned commitment.

![L14: shortage is loud and surplus is silent, so the ratio can ratchet upward. The dashed link is a candidate.|Source: authors' analysis; arrivals from Kadamba scan records.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l14.png){w=100 #a6-l14}

A candidate reinforcing closure sits on the chain. Shortages show as queues, complaints and back-door arrivals, while surplus is absorbed quietly, so an operator that raises its ratio after run-outs and never lowers it after surplus ratchets the ratio upward. No change of ratio in either direction is on record, so this is an asymmetry of intent, not an observed drift; Bakul states its premise as a rule (shortage outweighs waste, and the weekly review stops at a floor, single-sourced). The chain matters even without the ratchet: part of the over-preparation is a rational hedge against a delay at the close.

#### L2: Skip Meal

Skip Meal lets a student who knows they will not attend tell the kitchen to prepare less. Its first link, use by students with real non-attendance intent, is confirmed and close to zero: four of six respondents who addressed it had never used it, one because "it doesn't return the money." The kitchen link is in open conflict, as set out above, and even if it holds, nothing connects the resulting waste back to whether a student uses Skip Meal next time, so no self-correcting dynamic exists ([[fig:a6-l2]]).

![L2: Skip Meal. The student link is confirmed and near zero; the kitchen link is in open conflict.|Source: student interviews; Kadamba operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l2.png){w=100 #a6-l2}

#### L5, L6 and L7

**L5, menu transparency.** The rotation that closed L4 made the menu predictable, so a student can decide in advance not to come on a disliked day, a side effect the facilities head raised unprompted. Turnout does depend on the item: Yuktāhār's site head reports longer queues on aloo paratha and dosa days and 92 per cent for Sunday-night paneer, and Kadamba non-vegetarian breakfasts ran at 48.5 per cent against 32.4 for vegetarian (both confirmed); Bakul's manager says pav bhaji draws more students and muesli and Chocos go readily (single-sourced). Whether advance knowledge causes skipping, as against same-morning preference, is untested; if so, it feeds unused registrations on particular days into L13.

**L6, shock-adaptive relaxation.** In a gas shortage, and separately in some holidays and long weekends, the institution made registration non-compulsory and billed only for consumption ([[sec:a6-precedent]]). The trigger and the walk-in mechanism are confirmed; Kadamba's operator describes cutting quantities and buying some items outside during the shock (single-sourced); which holidays were relaxed is unconfirmed. A first reading counted a self-limiting balancing loop (relaxed billing raises procurement uncertainty and lowers institutional confidence in the model), but every link was assumed, and kitchens set quantity the day before and buy dry goods that keep. The simpler reading fits better: the model reverts because the shock or holiday ends, a calendar switch with no evidenced feedback. The candidate reasons it is never left on lie in whether per-plate payment counts registrations and in the convenience of an automatic default. Yuktāhār's June Wastage Book adds a candidate observation: two Saturday breakfasts with 37 and 34 registered record 33 and 31 diners, but registered students alone numbered 22 and 21, about 60 per cent, roughly double September's 28 per cent; the regime in force in June is not known.

**L7, late-night workload to skipping.** Staying up late is the best-evidenced single cause of skipping: "If I sleep around 3:00 or 4:00 a.m. or later, I don't tend to wake up. I prefer sleep over breakfast." The records match as a population pattern, since the export carries no student identifier: Sunday is Kadamba's lowest breakfast at 27.3 per cent and among Yuktāhār's lowest at 17.9 to 24.1 per cent, and Kadamba's median scan falls between 8:51 and 9:11, consistent with late waking. Yuktāhār's site head names hackathons and exams as reasons numbers fall. What follows a skipped breakfast (an energy crash, a compensating purchase, a change in that night's sleep) has never been asked, so the rest of the chain is untested.

### Absent, Disconfirmed and Static Mechanisms {#sec-a6-absent}

**L3, capacity redistribution (strongly contraindicated).** The plausible story, that scarcity at Kadamba pushes students to Bakul or Palash, runs against the evidence: scarcity makes students lock Kadamba down harder, as the respondent who registers all month says directly, and Bakul's low registration is better explained by a standing preference gap. The verdict rests on one quote corroborated by the aggregate pattern, and Bakul and Palash have no attendance records.

**L8, vendor quality discipline (unresolved).** Student billing tracks registration (confirmed), but the vendor's payment is a different link and the evidence conflicts. Bakul's manager says payment is a monthly lump sum with deductions, based on plates served, though "served" is the synthesis's wording of one remark and may mean plates billed; Kadamba's "they are getting paid for it" hints that payment follows registration. No contract has been seen. If vendors are paid per registered plate, falling attendance never touches vendor revenue and a fixed ratio turns part of the gap into margin; if per served plate, the vendor has a direct stake in turnout and the loop could close. Yuktāhār shows a third route: a vendor that ties waste reduction to profit gains from cutting waste whatever the plate basis. Operators already respond to item-level signals: Kadamba cut ragi idli and raised plain idli, Bakul switched paneer supplier twice after complaints, and Yuktāhār changes supplier when a grams-of-debris norm is exceeded (single-sourced). Pre-service quality control is a working balancing structure at Kadamba on taste and safety ([[sec:a4]]); nothing in it tests first-batch size.

**L16, operator waste to menu choice (absent).** Operators see which items waste, all three say students or the committee decide the menu, and Bakul's manager is asking for input; his knowledge of which items go and which pairings work goes unused, though whether Bakul residents share his view is untested. Decision rights sever the loop: operators hold the data and the committee holds the choice. L16 is the counterpart of L4, which closes because complaints are loud and have a route while waste is silent and has none.

**L10, the measured-but-unrouted gap (a static condition).** The gap is measured in at least three places: the dining office's scan export (62.9 per cent no-shows for Kadamba breakfast), Yuktāhār's register (roughly 72 per cent of registered breakfasts unused) and its other books (confirmed); at Bakul it is the manager's weekly review, possibly unwritten. The facilities head's 35 to 40 per cent breakfast figure is confirmed for Kadamba at 37.1 per cent. Predictable skipping is acted on in the quantity of food, never in the registration rule. The instrument exists, so the failure is a missing route, which is hard to build even where records exist because Yuktāhār's are on paper. The chain from CFS through CDS to the operator runs downward, and whether any operator data runs back up it is unknown. Operators are data-rich and authority-poor: even information they need reaches them informally, as the site head says of events and exams, "We don't come to know… until we sit in the meeting." The condition stays static because the kitchen loops absorb each morning's consequence locally and nothing tracks the gap's visibility to decision-makers over time, so there is no basis for calling it reinforcing.

### Structural Causes and the Loop Picture {#sec-a6-structural}

[[sec:a7]] analyses the three structural causes. For the loops they matter in three ways. Registration is an automatic, mandatory standing booking that cannot follow the morning decision to eat, which keeps the count flat in L13. Billing fires at the booking, not at consumption, which allows L1's cheap speculation, underlies L3b and L9, and is what L6 shows the institution can suspend. And the gap is measured but unrouted, because decision rights over menu, kitchen quantity, academic scheduling, registration and billing sit with different actors and none holds more than one or two; that is why L4 can close (the menu has a real owner and an escalation path) while L10 and L16 cannot.

Beneath the kitchen half, three kitchens convert registrations into a first batch under three regimes, and each can correct only upward within a meal. This mechanism sits inside authority the operator already holds; it explains why L13 produces surplus on low-turnout days, why L12 cannot remove it and why L14's hedge can ratchet. Raw surplus is largely recoverable and cooked surplus is not, so the binding delay lies between the cooked first batch and the late crest, not in the four-day cancellation lead. Yuktāhār's culture change took about four months. The remaining chains stop for narrower reasons: L2 because Skip Meal pays the student nothing, L5 because item-level turnout is noticed only in the kitchen, L7 because nobody has asked what follows a skip. The evidence runs out at three points: the plate basis that decides L8, the absence of records for Bakul, Palash and for lunch and dinner at Kadamba, and the base of Kadamba's 5 to 10 per cent waste figure.

### What This Means for the Reframe {#sec-a6-reframe}

Two kinds of closed loop are demonstrated: L4 shows governance closing a loop end to end when a complaint is loud enough, and L11 and L12 show the kitchen closing loops every day. Neither touches registration. L1 and L6 fit a stable equilibrium but neither closes on the evidence. The equilibrium reading rests on the absorption layer (resale and informal transfer, the operator's discount and live top-up, reuse, outsiders and staff, held food, back-door service, outside venues, the bin) and on the asymmetry of attention: shortages are loud and trigger fast correction, while surplus is silent, can ratchet the ratio upward (L14) and never reaches the menu owner (L16). Under the same booking rule, operator culture sets how much of the gap becomes surplus. A precedent for solving the core problem exists in L6, and two designed corrective mechanisms do not connect back to the behaviour they were built to correct: L2, and L8 if vendors are paid per registered plate. The evidence supports organisational inertia around a problem that is measured, locally managed and previously solved at least as strongly as an actively self-reinforcing spiral. [[sec:a7]] takes the argument down the iceberg and [[sec:a8]] turns it into the reframed problem.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a6-decisions}

Decision: Add a service blueprint to the system map as a new method. A Checkland-style rich picture was rejected (the course does not teach it; it would add notation without a finding), as was relabelling an unengaged mechanism as the fixes-that-fail archetype (nothing had been applied and made things worse). The blueprint stayed because it showed which artefacts cross the line of visibility.

Decision: Draw a mechanism as a loop only if its closing link has direct evidence, and re-test closure in every pass. Three previously named loops became open chains and one a calendar switch; calling every plausible cycle a loop would have given a richer picture that could not tell a reader what to trust.

Decision: Use one loop scheme for Phase 2, L1 to L17 plus L3b, with the loop analysis authoritative and a deliverable of its own; the first map's separate loops (R1 and B1 to B4) did not match it.

Decision: Name the cooked first batch, sized as an uncalibrated ratio of registrations, as the binding constraint, replacing the four-day procurement lock: the four-day close applies to registration, operators buy on their own schedules and set quantities the day before, and only dry goods are committed early.

Decision: Treat composting as unverified and remove it from the surplus absorbers, with the bin as the exit, because the facilities head's account could not be reconciled with the operator's first-hand one.

Decision: Describe the gap as measured twice but not routed to the rule owner, and drop the earlier claim that it is invisible: the scan records and the Yuktāhār books both measure it.

The first drafts treated the vendors as one actor cooking to a four-day forecast, showed Kadamba and Bakul as the same fixed-ratio kitchen, and placed Bakul's kitchen near the airport with a ratio raised from 70 to 80 per cent. The operator interviews of 26 and 27 September replaced the single operator with three regimes, withdrew the kilogram figures for Kadamba's waste and moved the surplus story from composting to outsiders, staff, reheating and the bin; the Bakul account of 28 September separated Bakul from Kadamba and added the distance-driven quality branches. Six loops once called closed were reduced to three, and the shock rule became a calendar switch. [[app:corrections]] lists the corrections and [[sec:open]] the open questions.
