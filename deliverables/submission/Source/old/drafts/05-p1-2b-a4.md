## Activity 4: Tracing the Existing Process {#sec-a4}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a4-approach}

The framework asks this activity to show how the system actually operates and not how it is supposed to, by tracing decisions and interactions, documenting rules, identifying escalation routes and tracking exceptions, workarounds and dependencies. The trace follows one breakfast from the student's registration through the operator's planning and cooking to the counter, the close, and what happens to the food nobody eats. Three timescales are kept apart. Registration is locked about four days ahead and, because every student is registered automatically, works as a standing month-long default and not as a choice. Cooked quantity is set the day before: as a share of registrations at Kadamba, from the mess's own past records at Yuktāhār, and at Bakul as an item-dependent share that the manager adjusts weekly. The decision to eat is made on the morning itself, and the operator responds live at the counter. At Yuktāhār the operator also re-forecasts the rest of the meal from the first half hour, so the morning holds an operator forecast as well as a reaction.

The first version, written on the evening of 6 September 2026 from the six student accounts and the portal rules, covered only the student half of the process and stopped at the kitchen door, because no source then showed how a registration became a cooked quantity. The operator half became visible in stages. The facilities head's interview and a walkthrough of the Kadamba kitchen on 13 September gave the governance flow, the entry cut-off and the equipment. The field visit of 26 September gave Yuktāhār's 26 photographed registers, the site head's walkthrough and the Kadamba manager's interview. The team's own first-hand account, written up on 27 September, added the Kadamba command chain and quantity rule and the Yuktāhār forecasting steps. The Bakul manager's account arrived on 28 September. Each batch led to a revision pass over the whole trace. The two best-evidenced operators run on different logics, so the trace follows each end to end, and Bakul, cooked off site, is traced on its manager's account alone, since no Bakul records exist. The deliverable is the Current-State Process Map and Process Trace.

The Bakul account was likewise first held as noisy audio from an unnamed mess and is now the manager's single-sourced statements, still his own estimates with no records. A live breakfast service has not been watched from start to finish. The 11,941 Kadamba scans answer what that observation was meant to capture, the shape and timing of the crowd, better than one morning of counting could. The team saw the wrapped display plate at Kadamba's entrance and the Yuktāhār books themselves. Waste disposal after close still needs direct observation. A transcription problem in the student material also had to be handled openly: the source lists a "daily eater 2" and a "daily eater 3" with only one transcript following both, so the five transcripts may represent as few as four distinct people, and the trace counts four to five.

### Decisions and Interactions {#sec-a4-decisions}

#### The student side

Registration is locked about four days ahead. The CDS handbook requires registration and cancellation at least four days in advance, and Kadamba's manager calls it weekly compulsory. Students say they register for a month, three of six explicitly. Both hold: the portal locks on a four-day weekly rhythm and students set a standing booking once. A team member's own experience as a Kadamba student adds that registration is automatic and mandatory, off campus and over holidays, and that the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month made four days ahead: "we don't have any opportunity to nullify it." The booking is a default. Kadamba's April records confirm the result, with registrations flat at about 1,070 a day on every weekday including Sunday (range 928 to 1,123). #### The operators' three logics

The three operators forecast in three ways ([[tab:a4-regimes]]). Kadamba cooks a fixed share of registrations and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond per-dish gram standards. Yuktāhār learns from its own books, looking up the matching past week before service and re-projecting from the first half hour during it. Bakul sits between: its manager varies the share by item and adjusts it weekly from past attendance by judgement, never below a floor that he keeps against shortage. An earlier reading, from a noisy recording that gave only the ratio, had Bakul resembling Kadamba.

Informal norms on the student side are unwritten and shared. Going early to avoid queues is a minority behaviour, since only a third of Kadamba scans arrive before 8:30. At Yuktāhār the culture is described as family-like, and one project manager trained all staff at his farmhouse on what good quality looks like and what responsibility to take (single-sourced).

Workarounds compensate for a gap in the formal system. Mess Cell resale, used by four of six, compensates for Skip Meal's lack of refund and is mirrored on the operator side by Kadamba's manager describing students who register while away while a friend eats in their place. Eating snacks or a later meal compensates for skipping without a cost-recovery path. Early registration at Kadamba compensates for a popular mess filling up, and students enter by the back door after close. ### Exceptions, Workarounds and Dependencies {#sec-a4-exceptions}

![Exception and workaround paths, student side and operator side.|Source: student interviews, operator accounts and Kadamba scan records.](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d9-exception-paths.png){w=86 #a4-exceptions}

#### Operator exceptions

[[fig:a4-exceptions]] sets out what goes wrong and what people do about it. On the operator side the exceptions are these.

- Scanner failure. When the QR system fails, Kadamba staff write down ID and name by hand. The April export shows no sign of bulk entry (never more than two scans in one second), so hand-logged diners may be missing from the records, a candidate undercount.
- Gas shortage. During the LPG shortage Kadamba cut quantities a little and bought some items outside. This is the first operator account of the shortage, and its cause is still single-sourced.
- Equipment failure. Kadamba's bread grill is broken and unreplaced, which removes an item from service regardless of demand.
- Events, exams and holidays. Nothing formal tells the kitchen. They reach Yuktāhār's meeting only if someone mentions them, and hackathons and exams lower turnout in ways the site head calls unavoidable.
- Supplier quality. At Yuktāhār, impurities above the norm are raised in the meeting and lead to a complaint or a new supplier. Bakul's paneer has moved between three brands after complaints.
- Biryani days. Kadamba cooks biryani lunch at 100 per cent and not 70, with a head chef brought in, so the ratio follows the operator's expectation of demand for a dish and is not a fixed rule. Portion leakage (three pieces for two) still causes late run-outs at full preparation.
- Off-site transport. Every Bakul top-up is a phone call, a cook at Hafizpet and a 20 to 40 minute drive against a fixed close, and the true lead time is unknown. The 9:20 reserve of about 50 is, as a candidate reading, the consequence: late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals.
- Latecomers. When Kadamba's front door closes at 9:30, latecomers use the back door and ask for food, and staff do not refuse. Last-minute registrations are called a pain. The speaker's wording covers both the breakfast and dinner closes, and scans after 9:30 on every April day support it at breakfast.
- Records that disagree. For breakfast on 3 September at Yuktāhār, the Wastage Book records 77 diners (28 per cent) and the attendance register, on a partly obscured row, records 110 (40 per cent). Which feeds planning is unknown. The two-book rule covers materials and not diner counts, and the two books have different keepers, which may be why the difference stands (candidate).

The operator workarounds beside these (leftover reuse, outside diners and staff eating from the same food, holding and reheating surplus, late-door service, raw stock held for a quick top-up) are absorption mechanisms. Each soaks up part of the gap before it becomes visible, and each is locally beneficial. The outside diners and the half of Kadamba's staff who eat at the mess are real people fed by the surplus, so any change to the first batch has to plan for them. Holding and reheating is the exception among absorbers: the team judged Kadamba's food to be cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, so it degrades fast. That links over-preparation to quality as an open chain, and whether poorer quality feeds skipping is not evidenced. Bakul's hot holding carries a similar cost for a different reason, since food is kept hot from an early start through transport and steam softens parathas on the way, and as a candidate reading the food kept back for late arrivals is also the food held longest. Yuktāhār tried and dropped a commercial ERP for grocery management and rush prediction, because paying "lakhs of rupees" for prediction made no sense when the data already sat in its books ("prediction will be based on our data only… I want to remain close to the data") and because staff are not digitally inclined and too few people exist to digitise, so the office digitises later.

#### Dependencies

Eating breakfast at the mess depends on waking with enough time and alertness, the most cited constraint. It depends on the desired item still being available (run-outs confirmed by at least three of six, directly or by reputation), on no clash with class-attendance pressure (explicit for one respondent), and on the mess still being open, since the 9:30 cut-off is the most requested change. The attendance-policy pressure depends on academic scheduling, which the power analysis found disconnected from mess governance.

Item availability depends in turn on a chain that the trace can now follow. The four-day lock applies to registration and not to cooking or buying. Yuktāhār buys groceries fortnightly by the stock register from many local shops and godowns, vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily. Kadamba buys in three tiers: dry goods a week ahead, perishables the day before and some vegetables two to three days ahead. Cooked quantity is set the day before and adjusted live through top-ups and re-cooks. At Bakul the first batch leaves Hafizpet before any sign of the morning's turnout, and a top-up can only add food, slowly. The binding constraint on waste is therefore cooked food prepared ahead of demand and not raw procurement: raw stock left over is issued again, while cooked food lasts only three to four hours. An earlier version had said that the Mess Office fixes each vendor's order four days out, so that nothing on the day could change what is cooked.

The operator side has its own dependencies. They include supplier cadence and quality, and gas, electricity and water, which Yuktāhār logs daily and Kadamba checks for water quality daily. They include equipment (Kadamba's roti line, multi-item cooker and tilting curry machine, and the broken grill) and one person running all of Kadamba's inventory, a single point of failure. They include the matching-week Wastage Book page, which depends on the two-week menu rotation, and the daily meeting as the only channel for event information and exceptions at Yuktāhār. Transport from Hafizpet, about 8 km and about 20 minutes early and 40 later, bounds every Bakul top-up. Yuktāhār chooses inputs for quality (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months, with visits to the mills), and staff numbers matter: Yuktāhār has 16 kitchen staff and two project managers with some site staff sharing the work, in separate tasting, cutting and cleaning teams without a hierarchy, and Kadamba has about ten chefs under a main veg chef with a head chef for biryani days.

Where uneaten food finally goes is only partly settled. The facilities head described production and plate waste going via a garbage collector to an outside compost facility. Kadamba's manager, asked about composting, said there is none and that the bin goes to the garbage contractor (single-sourced), which replaced the first assumption that composting absorbs the physical surplus. At Kadamba the surplus is absorbed by outside diners and staff, by holding and reheating, by latecomer top-ups and by the bin, and whether the contractor composts it off site is unverified. Yuktāhār's disposal route after reuse is not stated in any source. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown, with no onward route stated.

### Key Findings {#sec-a4-findings}

1. The registration-attendance gap has several mechanisms and not one. Habitual monthly over-registration made the default by automatic registration, scarcity-driven defensive registration at Kadamba, automatic allocation and simple forgetting all feed it, and the Kadamba records show their combined effect. Registration is an automatic standing booking, flat across the week while turnout varies by weekday, and not a daily demand signal.
2. Peer influence is disconfirmed as a decision-determining factor. Six respondents place it at most as a motivational add-on.
3. There are three forecasting logics, not one or two. Kadamba cooks a fixed share and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond gram standards. Bakul varies an item-dependent 70 to 80 per cent weekly from past attendance by the manager's judgement, never below about 70 per cent, so its correction closes against surplus only down to that floor, and whether it rests on any written record is unknown. Yuktāhār has no fixed ratio: it learns from its books by matching week and weekday and re-projects from the first half hour, though its head believes about 50 per cent where his register shows about 28. None of the fixed ratios is set against actual turnout. The gap is measured, locally and repeatedly, and what is missing is any route from that measurement into registration or billing rules. Even in the best-documented mess the fast correction samples the calm start of service and not the crest at the close.
4. Broken restock promises. Staff say an item will return before close and it does not, a trust and service finding first reported by students. The operator side offers a candidate mechanism: a re-cook cut-off that leaves late run-outs unfilled, top-ups of 10 to 15 minutes on site, an unmeasured 20 to 40 minute drive from an off-site kitchen against a close minutes away, equipment failures and items that float above their gram standard, together with a Kadamba chef's "wait 5 minutes" that matches what students describe hearing.
5. Under-provisioning is not the problem. Two respondents independently said that only about 30 per cent of registrants attend and the mess still struggles to serve even that smaller group, which early drafts read as under-provisioning. The attendance half is confirmed ([[sec:a5]]), and the under-provisioning reading does not hold. Every stated first-batch share sits well above turnout, at Bakul the share and weekend floor sit above even the manager's own estimate (candidate, no records), and Yuktāhār's book records leftovers. The shortage students feel is a timing and item-mix failure at the close, and the curve differs by hall, since the Bakul manager describes an earlier peak. The items students report running out (idli, puri, bhatura, fruit) include those that Kadamba says float above gram standard (puri, idli), while the ones it says waste (uttapam, set dosa, upma) are left over, and late run-outs are not re-cooked. Over-preparation and run-outs happen on the same morning.
6. The escalation-path absence is corroborated from the operator side. An operational complaint path exists and works for same-incident fixes, but no path for changing rules or timing works for students or operators.

One tension that the operator's own figures leave open should be stated. Kadamba's stated practice and stated waste do not yet add up. In April it averaged about 1,072 registrations and 398 scans a day (confirmed). A 70 per cent first batch is about 750 portions against about 400 eaten, a surplus of about 350 a day before any top-up (candidate arithmetic from the ratio and the records). The operator reports about 5 to 10 per cent daily wastage on no stated base (single-sourced). The two are compatible only if most of the surplus goes somewhere other than the bin: to the 30 to 50 outside diners and half the staff, to food held and reheated, to smaller top-ups than the ratio implies, or to a waste figure on a different base such as all meals or plate waste only. Which holds is not known, and the trace assumes none.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a4-decisions-log}

The first decision concerned what could honestly be claimed from the student material alone. The first version was split into a part established from real data and a part that was only a plan for the unobserved kitchen side, and it said so, in preference to describing the kitchens from assumption.

Decision: The student half of the trace was written first and the kitchen half was marked as unobserved until operator evidence existed, so that nothing was claimed about a process the team had not seen.

Decision: The transcript set with a labelling gap ("daily eater 2" and "daily eater 3" with one transcript) was flagged in the evidence and counted as four to five distinct people, and not silently relabelled, because neither explanation could be checked against the source. The reading of the five interviews was also compared with a teammate's independent synthesis of the same material, and two corrections were adopted because the independent reading was better supported: the registration cycle is monthly and not weekly, and an apparent two-peak crowd is one continuously building crowd cresting near the close.

Decision: Each caterer was traced end to end as its own system. One clean operator would have been easier to write, but the three differ in the way that matters, and merging them would have hidden the finding that there are three forecasting logics.

Decision: The Bakul manager's account was traced as his own statement only, with no number generalised to the other halls and every row marked as his, since no Bakul record exists. Where a Bakul statement stands in tension with Kadamba's, both are left in place and unresolved.

Decision: When field evidence contradicted a finding already written, the finding was changed in every place, and old and new versions were not blended. The trace was rewritten in three passes (27 September on the operators' interviews and scan records, 27 September again on the team's first-hand account, and 28 September on the Bakul account). Corrections include the replacement of a four-day procurement lock by a registration cut-off, the replacement of a single passive vendor by three operators with their own logics, the withdrawal of composting as an absorber, and the removal of an unattributed operator chain.

Decision: A live observation of a full breakfast service was not carried out, and the scan records were used in its place. This judged the 11,941 scans to describe the shape and timing of the crowd better than one morning of counting, and left disposal after close as an open item for direct observation.

The result of these choices was a picture in which the kitchens are not passive recipients of a count. They measure the gap, correct for it privately and absorb the cost, which made the next question, how large the gap really is, the subject of [[sec:a5]].
