## Activity 1: Understanding the System Context {#sec-a1}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a1-approach}

The framework gives the first activity one purpose: to understand the problem as part of a larger system, beyond a single operational fault. The activity therefore had to say where the system starts and stops, what it is meant to do, what it does in practice, and what the team could already see going wrong. Its deliverable is the System Context Brief (Deliverable 1).

The identification of primary, secondary and indirect stakeholders is listed under this activity in the framework but was carried out in [[sec:a2]], where the stakeholder evidence was assembled.

### System Boundary and Context {#sec-a1-boundary}

#### System boundary

The system studied is the breakfast meal as it is registered, billed, cooked and served in the four residential dining halls: Kadamba, Palash, Bakul and Yuktāhār. Three layers sit inside the boundary ([[fig:a1-boundary]]).

The registration and billing layer is the dining portal, where students book, cancel and pay for meals, together with the QR scan at the counter that records who ate. The kitchen layer is the outside caterer that runs each hall: Prism at Kadamba, ABC Hospitality Services at Yuktāhār, and Vijayalakshmi Caterers at Bakul, which cooks at its Hafizpet kitchen (single-sourced). It covers quantity planning, cooking, the food-safety checks before service, the counter, top-up during service and what happens to leftovers. Bakul and Palash receive their food from one approved off-site kitchen (confirmed), so for these halls the layer also includes the transport link and the hot holding of food at the hall. Whether Vijayalakshmi Caterers also supplies Palash is a candidate. The governance layer is the set of bodies that fix the menu, the registration and billing rules and the operator contracts.

Several parties sit on the boundary, exchanging food, money or information with the system without being run by it: the operators' suppliers, the garbage contractor, the institution's site team (which relays complaints) and facility team (weekly quality inspection), and the tender through which each operator is hired. People outside the registration also eat from the same pots. At Kadamba these are 30 to 50 outsiders such as night-shift security, together with about half of the operator's own staff (single-sourced). The academic class schedule sits outside the boundary but shapes it, since the two run on overlapping, independently set timings. Other places to eat (Vindhya Canteen, the juice canteens, delivery apps) lie at the outer edge, because a student who skips the mess eats from one of them.

Lunch and dinner at the same halls are outside the boundary and are the comparison that justifies it. The same portal, rules and operator produce very different turnout at the two meals, so the problem is specific to breakfast. The size of the difference is reported in [[sec:a5]].

#### Context

The published breakfast window is 7:30 to 9:30 AM. Kitchen staff start between 5:00 and 5:30. Batch items are ready by about 7:30, and at Kadamba live items such as dosa are made at the counter from 7:00. Bakul's food is cooked off site, so labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha may be started around 3 AM to reach the hall by about 7 (single-sourced). Service in practice ran later than the published window, which [[sec:a5]] documents from the scan records. Classes run from 8:30 AM to 6:40 PM with a break from 1:00 to 2:00 PM, and most students have few classes on a given day.

[[tab:a1-halls]] summarises the four halls as the portal showed them on one September day. Breakfast capacity totalled 2,755 registrations. The registered prices were Rs 48 (vegetarian) and Rs 66 (non-vegetarian) at Kadamba, Bakul and Palash, and Rs 53 at Yuktāhār; without prior registration the same breakfast cost Rs 81, Rs 111 and Rs 92, about 70 per cent more (confirmed, posted rates).

Registration is automatic. Every student is registered for every meal by default, on campus or not, holidays included. The only exit is a cancellation, allowed five times per meal type per month and at least four days ahead; beyond that a meal cannot be removed from the bill (confirmed). Billing is monthly and follows registration, so a student pays for every registered meal, eaten or not. A separate Skip Meal button tells the kitchen not to prepare a portion but leaves the charge standing. Registration is high and flat, and turnout is low and variable: about two thirds of breakfast registrations went unused at both halls with records ([[sec:a5]]). Operators describe counts reaching them in weekly blocks, while students describe registering monthly, and how the default is batched on the portal is unconfirmed (single-sourced).

The platform is shared, but the kitchens are not. Each operator buys its own ingredients on its own cadence, and the four halls are unified only at the registration, billing and access layer. Bakul's food travels about 8 km from Hafizpet, roughly 20 minutes early in the morning and 40 in normal traffic, in insulated boxes holding about 60 to 72 °C, and stands in a hot-water setup at the hall (single-sourced). The first submission had Bakul's vegetarian food cooked at Palash and carried over, which the later evidence corrected.

Five adjacent systems act on the mess without being controlled by it.

- Academic administration sets the timetable. No channel links the mess to the academic calendar, so exams, events and holidays reach a kitchen only if someone raises them in its planning meeting: "we don't come to know until we sit in the meeting," in the Yuktāhār site head's words (confirmed).
- Institutional governance divides between a Mess Committee (menu and policy, with student input about a month ahead), a Mess Office that runs the portal, and a Warden who holds vendor management and structural change. Operators speak of a "CDS team", and the Kadamba operator describes a line of authority from CFS to CDS to Prism (single-sourced). How these descriptions map onto each other is unconfirmed ([[sec:a2]]).
- The operators and their contracts: each firm is chosen by tender and paid monthly on plates with deductions (single-sourced). Whether a plate means a registered or a served plate decides who bears the cost of a no-show, and the evidence disagrees ([[sec:a3]]).
- Mess Cell is a student WhatsApp group in which people sell a breakfast registration they will not use, at a negotiated price, by passing on the personal QR credential. It runs outside the portal.
- The wider campus food ecosystem competes for the same students: Vindhya Canteen (Monday to Saturday from 10:00 AM), the juice canteens in the mornings and delivery apps (orders until 11:00 PM, riders kept outside the gate).

### Purpose and Intended Outcomes {#sec-a1-purpose}

#### Purpose

The system exists to give resident students reliable, adequate, good-quality food each morning at a predictable, affordable cost, covering vegetarian, non-vegetarian and Jain requirements, in support of health, energy and academic performance. The institution states its objective as to "provide nutritious, hygienic, and affordable meals while ensuring efficient operations and minimizing food wastage through advance meal planning." Advance registration is the mechanism it relies on to match food to demand.

#### Actual mechanism

Registration is a default that students do not choose. The booking therefore records the absence of an opt-out, it stays flat across the week while attendance does not ([[sec:a5]]), and billing follows it. The kitchen does not cook to the booking either: each operator reads the closed count the day before and applies its own conversion, traced step by step in [[sec:a4]]. Yuktāhār plans from its books and the first half hour of service (confirmed for the books). Kadamba cooks a fixed first batch of about 70 per cent, portioned by gram standards, and a supervisor calls for more food, ready in 10 to 15 minutes. No record carries one day's outcome into the next day's ratio, and the manager says of past attempts that "it was wasted many times". Bakul cooks 70 to 80 per cent depending on the item, reviewed weekly by its manager, with a floor of about 70 per cent even on low weekends, because his rule is that there should be no shortage even if some food is wasted (single-sourced for Kadamba and Bakul). The first submission had treated Bakul's ratio as fixed like Kadamba's.

Every one of these rules sits above breakfast turnout. Kadamba's first batch is about twice the share that arrives (candidate), and Bakul's batch and weekend floor both exceed its manager's own ceiling of about 60 per cent, by more if Bakul resembles the two recorded halls (candidate; no Bakul records exist). Yuktāhār's site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50 per cent while his own register shows about 28 (confirmed), so even the operator with the fullest books misjudges its number.

Surplus has four exits: reuse at Yuktāhār (idli into upma or poha, batter back to the fridge, milk set as curd), outside diners and operator staff at Kadamba, held and reheated food (candidate) and the bin. The earlier finding that composting absorbs the surplus is withdrawn, because Kadamba's waste goes to a garbage contractor with no composting (single-sourced), and the earlier 20 to 30 staff eating leftovers is replaced by the outside-diner figure given above. Kadamba's operator puts daily waste at 5 to 10 per cent without saying of what, which sits uneasily with a first batch near twice the turnout and is treated as an open tension in [[sec:a6]]. The operators also read the count with different aims: Kadamba as an obligation it is paid for, Yuktāhār as a cost to cut, and Bakul as a shortage to avoid at the price of some waste (single-sourced for all three).

#### Gap between purpose and mechanism

Advance planning works only if advance registration expresses an intention to eat. Under automatic registration it records who has not opted out. For breakfast the distortion is large: registrations overstate turnout about 2.7 times at Kadamba and about 3.5 times at Yuktāhār (confirmed, records). Every operator plans against a number it knows is inflated and discounts it by its own rule. The gap is already measured, in the institution's scan records and in the operators' registers, but nothing carries that measurement into the registration or billing rules. Inside the kitchens it has not produced a discount matched to breakfast turnout, and at Bakul it is judged weekly by one manager and may exist only in his head.

#### Institutions

Three governance actors hold different parts of mess governance: the Mess Committee (policy), the Mess Office (operations) and the Warden (vendors and structural change). The academic administration is independent of all of them. The tender and the monthly per-plate payment through which each operator is contracted complete the formal picture, with the plate basis unknown.

Two informal institutions matter as much. The first is the set of operators' record systems. Yuktāhār keeps paper books, all photographed (confirmed): a Wastage Book (per dish: issued, cooked, left, percentage, run-out time, re-cook quantity, registered and eaten counts, student plate waste), a weekday attendance register, a wastage board kept in view, a grocery issue book headed with the day's registrations, a store and ingredient book, a stock register, a vegetable book and indent with spoilage remarks, a milk, fruit, gas, electricity and water register, and fridge temperature and cleaning logs. The issue book must reconcile with what was cooked, and a mismatch is raised in that day's meeting (single-sourced); on the day checked the issue book and stock register agree (confirmed). The site head describes the cross-checking as "not to find fault", and weighing was made blame-free on purpose (single-sourced). Together these hold the system's most detailed demand data. The second is the food-safety regime: an external Eat Right Campus five-star certification and laboratory testing of suspect food, and at Kadamba a fixed pre-service sequence of checks described in [[sec:a4]]. QC inspects weekly and water TDS is read daily, with a failure triggering a full check (single-sourced; display plate and tasting confirmed by observation).

#### Resources

The portal and the scan-level record it produces of who ate and when are the first resource. The institution holds this record, which measures the registration-to-turnout gap for every meal it scans (confirmed for Kadamba breakfast; assumed for other halls and meals). The operators' own records are the second. Utility readings feed decisions too: a Wednesday electricity peak from grinding idli batter informs how long machines run (single-sourced). Yuktāhār tried a commercial ERP to simplify purchasing and predict the rush and dropped it, judging its books enough for the cost and its staff unsuited to digital tools, and office staff digitise the books later (single-sourced). Kadamba holds per-dish gram standards, a live count watched during service, a waste figure, retained 72-hour samples and a daily water-quality log (single-sourced). No written record of attendance, consumption or waste is known at Bakul. The registration-to-turnout gap is therefore already measured locally, by the people who cook.

The other resources are the four halls of differing capacity and origin (Kadamba purpose-built and largest, Bakul a converted warehouse), a production capacity split by cuisine (on-site kitchens at Kadamba and Yuktāhār, one off-site kitchen with a transport link for Bakul and Palash), Yuktāhār's quality-and-cost stance on ingredients (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months, single-sourced), kitchen equipment and storage including a cold room on first-in, first-out labels at Yuktāhār (confirmed), and the personal QR credential issued to each student. ### Initial Problem Framing {#sec-a1-framing}

Most breakfast registrations go unused. At Kadamba in April 2026 about 63 per cent of registered breakfasts were not eaten, and at Yuktāhār in September about 72 per cent, while at Yuktāhār lunch the same system is mostly used. Students are charged for every registered breakfast whether they eat it or not.

The kitchens know that registration overstates breakfast, so none cooks for every registration. Each cooks to its own discounted guess and tops up during service. Kadamba's first batch is still far above what arrives, yet arrivals pile into the last fifteen minutes and cause shortages at the close. Surplus is partly reused, partly eaten by outsiders and the operators' staff, at Kadamba possibly held and reheated into later service, and the rest is thrown away. Bakul shows the same pattern on its manager's own account (single-sourced; no Bakul records). He attributes low turnout partly to students not waking, which agrees with what students interviewed elsewhere most often say, and partly to taste and dish pairing, a candidate influence untested with Bakul residents.

Students do not choose to book breakfast; they are booked. They can cancel at most five times a month per meal and at least four days ahead, or mark a meal skipped, which returns nothing. A student who decides on the morning itself has no option, and students built their own workaround, reselling registrations, because it pays back more than the system's tools do.

> The problem: breakfast registration does not reflect who is going to eat, by a margin of roughly two thirds, while the same system works for lunch at Yuktāhār, the one hall with lunch records. Why not, and what in the system causes that gap? And why does each kitchen have to guess its own conversion from registrations to plates, when the data needed to calibrate it is already being recorded, and when one operator learns from its own books, another applies a fixed ratio, and a third adjusts its ratio by judgement?

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a1-decisions}

A study of the whole mess would give more to say, and a study of breakfast alone allows depth. The team chose breakfast and kept lunch and dinner outside the boundary as the comparison, since the same portal, rules and operator produce very different turnout at the two meals.

Decision: Scope the study to breakfast and keep lunch and dinner outside the boundary as a control, to test whether the cause is the meal or the system.

The opening story of the puzzle named a single cause, the clash between a late night and an early class. The team could have taken it as given and begun at once, or doubted it and begun more slowly. A neat story that proves wrong shapes every later question, so each link was written out as a claim and marked by whether evidence stood behind it. Only the first link, late nights leading to skipped mornings, had testimony behind it.

Decision: Treat the opening description as a set of hypotheses and record which have evidence, instead of starting from its single cause.

On 5 September the purpose section was split into the institution's stated purpose, the mechanism the rules actually set up, and the gap between them. The team judged that a registration mechanism which rewards staying booked over attending explained more of the paradox than the clash with the class schedule alone.

Decision: Report purpose, actual mechanism and the gap between them as three separate findings, so that the rules are examined before the students are.

The brief took the framework's section wording and order, states findings directly, and attaches one of the four evidence labels to every claim, so that a later reader can tell what was read from a record, what one person said, what was worked out by combining sources and what was assumed. A teammate's independent hypothesis, that Skip Meal and Mess Cell together make staying booked the dominant strategy for a student, was integrated, and the teammate's own text was kept in full beside it.

Decision: Follow the framework's section wording and order, state findings directly, and attach an evidence label to every claim.

Decision: Keep a contributor's original text on file next to any hypothesis taken from it, so that attribution survives the merge.

The three revisions changed the brief in this order. The records and recordings of 27 September replaced the 30 per cent working gap, introduced the three-layer boundary and the actual-mechanism subsection, and showed that no operator cooks to the full count. The first-hand account withdrew the kilogram waste figures, replaced the 20 to 30 staff with the outside-diner figure and turned the registration from a weekly standing booking into an automatic default with a narrow exit. The Bakul summary of 28 September named the third operator and the off-site kitchen, changed Bakul's ratio from fixed to weekly and added transport, hot holding and texture as constraints. The framing kept its shape and gained a second question, on why each kitchen guesses its own conversion.

Decision: When field evidence contradicts a statement in the brief, change the statement and mark the revision where it appears, without blending the old and new versions.

### Formal and Informal Roles {#sec-a2-roles}

Every institutional and commercial actor holds a formal, rule-defined or contract-defined position: students as registrants, the Mess Committee, the Mess Office, the CDS site team, the Warden, the three operators, serving and kitchen staff, suppliers, the garbage contractor, the canteens and the delivery platforms.

#### Formal roles inside the operators

Each operator runs its own organisation, which the institution does not see. The two on-site ones are opposites, and Bakul splits across two sites.

Kadamba sits at the end of a contract chain from CFS to CDS to Prism, and inside Prism from an operations manager to a manager, a supervisor and the service staff (single-sourced). A separate staff recording adds a director above the operations manager; reading that as Prism's ladder is a candidate. The supervisor is the in-service quantity decider, who watches the counters, tracks how much of each item is left at what time and tells the kitchen how much more to cook. The kitchen has about ten chefs with separate veg and non-veg cooks, a main vegetarian chef and a head chef who joins on biryani days, and up to fifty staff work at the mess (single-sourced). Every meal passes a fixed safety sequence before service, with the display plate and the tasting by a facility team and two Prism staff confirmed by observation ([[sec:a4]]). Who the facility team is (the CDS site team, CFS, Facilities and Estate, or faculty visitors) is not confirmed and is kept separate from each.

Yuktāhār has no hierarchy: everyone does every job and peers cross-check. ABC runs the mess through two project managers, with about three site staff sharing the work (single-sourced; revised from a five-person team). The mess in-charge keeps the accounts and the Wastage Book, and colleagues check those records. A cross-verifier writes and checks the grocery register, and their signature is on the photographed fridge cleaning log (confirmed). A coordinator keeps the registrations, the weekday attendance register and a wastage board with a falling waste-share target. The store function pulls, sorts and cleans raw material but is nobody's dedicated job. A tasting pair checks every breakfast, with cutting and cleaning teams alongside, and sixteen kitchen staff start cooking at 5:30 to be ready by 7:30. A site head has run the mess since the takeover and writes in the registers daily; whether they are one of the two project managers is unconfirmed. Each role attaches to a book, all photographed (confirmed), and each book is checked by someone other than its keeper. What was issued must match what was cooked, and a mismatch goes to the daily meeting; on 18 September the grocery book and stock register agreed on toor dal (confirmed).

Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) splits two sites: Hafizpet cooks, and the mess holds, serves and decides. The operator describes a line from staff to the manager to a managing director, and a complaint route from the CDS team to the manager, the kitchen and the managing director (single-sourced). The manager is the single quantity decider at the mess. This puts Bakul between the other two, with a weekly judgement in place of Kadamba's fixed share or Yuktāhār's books and no book of its own that any source has seen (candidate).

#### Informal roles

The informal roles belong to the Mess Cell community, Ping and the tool developers. None has official standing and each does a real job: a resale market, a public-pressure channel and an alternative registration interface. The operators add informal structures of their own. Yuktāhār's twice-daily meeting is the system's real forecasting institution, and no institutional document names it. It also carries Yuktāhār's informal organisation: there is no leave policy, so absences are raised there and colleagues agree to cover. One project manager took all staff to his farmhouse for training on what good quality looks like, and staff describe a family-like culture (single-sourced). That informal layer holds the formal books together. The WhatsApp group between the CDS site team and each operator carries formal business informally. At Kadamba, meals for outside diners and half the staff, the door reopened for 5 to 10 minutes after close and the back door that, in the operator's words, the mess "can't say no" to are accommodations that absorb surplus and lateness with no rule requiring them (single-sourced).

#### Roles that are not separate stakeholders

Attending regularly, skipping regularly, having an early class, selling on Mess Cell and using an unofficial tool are states one student moves through in a week. Peer influence is a relationship inside the Students node, so "friends" do not appear as a separate stakeholder. Guest diners have a different formal relationship (no registration, a higher spot rate) but too little volume for their own node. Students also act as menu selectors. In the Bakul manager's account, students choose combinations from balanced diet, protein, carbohydrate and preference, while he reasons from traditional pairings, regional habit and what is eaten (single-sourced; residents have not been asked). Whether residents differ in regional familiarity, diet and breakfast habit in ways that matter for menus is untested, so menu research at Bakul should sample along those lines (research implication, candidate).

#### Relationship network

The same twenty-four stakeholders fall into five clusters ([[fig:a2-network]]): core daily operation, operator interior, governance, supply and waste, and informal or competing actors. The operator interior holds two opposite organisations, Kadamba's ladder from CFS through CDS to Prism and the supervisor, and Yuktāhār's flat, peer-checked ring of books and keepers. Bakul adds a third arrangement: one manager at the mess, linked by phone and road to a kitchen 8 km away. The manager takes registration counts from the portal and sets the first batch from a weekly review of attendance history. He checks stock about every 30 minutes, phones Hafizpet for top-ups on a lead time nobody has stated, and holds a reserve for about 50 at 9:20. Complaints run from the CDS team to the manager, the kitchen and the operator's managing director. Money arrives as a monthly lump sum on plates "served", with deductions (basis flagged). The manager's turnout estimate, his view of which items go and his view of good pairings are held only by him and routed nowhere known (single-sourced; no Bakul records exist).

#### Dependencies

Students depend on operators for food quality and timing, while operators depend on no individual student, an asymmetry consistent with a walk-in price that needs no negotiation. Operators depend on the Mess Office for counts released four days ahead and on the contract for payment, but not on the count to procure food, which runs on their own cadence. What the count sets is how much cooked food is prepared, because every operator converts registrations into a cooked quantity and cooked food lasts only three to four hours. The Mess Office depends on students to register accurately, yet billing rewards no accuracy: a student pays the same whether or not they attend and is registered automatically unless one of the capped cancellations is used. The Mess Committee depends on the Office to execute policy, and the Office's one confirmed policy change, motivated by workload (candidate), did not visibly come from the Committee, which suggests that operational pressure may drive policy from below as often as governance drives it from above.

Operators depend on suppliers for clean raw stock, on the garbage contractor to remove what is not eaten and, at Bakul, on the road. The first version had a 40-minute leg from a kitchen near the airport and top-ups arriving within 30 minutes. The kitchen is in fact about 8 km away, 20 minutes by road early in the morning and 40 in normal traffic, and a top-up needs a phone call, cooking and that drive. The total lead time is unstated, so the 30-minute figure survives only as a noisy-audio reading that transit alone makes doubtful (single-sourced). A top-up called late in the window would miss the close, a candidate reason for the reserve of about 50 at 9:20, several times the 10 to 15 arrivals the manager describes in the last ten minutes. The same boxes keep food hot and soften it, so Bakul's quality depends on the transport link as well as the kitchen (aloo paratha only; other items untested). Kitchen staff depend on the QR scanner and portal for live counts, and operators depend on one another without planning to: Yuktāhār's site head blames weak Wednesday paneer turnout on another mess serving something more attractive that day.

At Kadamba the outside diners and about half the staff eat what the fixed first batch produces (single-sourced), so any cut to that batch takes food from people nobody has consulted unless their portions are planned explicitly. They are a latent opponent of waste reduction (candidate).

Vendors and students meet directly at the counter, which the first version denied when it said everything routes through the Mess Office or the portal. A student complained in person to Kadamba's manager about the dropped sambar and got a direct answer, operators absorb latecomers through a door and a back door they say they cannot refuse, and Yuktāhār weighs student plate waste. Only formal complaints route through institutional channels, from a director's email or the CDS team to the manager and the kitchen, and at Bakul on to the managing director (single-sourced). No source describes the Portal and IT owner having any direct relationship with the Mess Committee, although the Committee sets policy that the system presumably enforces.

One contract dependency matters more than any other. Vijayalakshmi Caterers says it won a tender and is paid monthly on plates with deductions, and that payment is on plates served (single-sourced). If plates mean registrations, an operator cooking for well under all of them keeps a margin on every no-show and has no financial interest in closing the gap; if plates mean meals served, the reverse holds. Bakul's "served" and Kadamba's "we cook on registrations because they are getting paid for it" pull opposite ways. The contracts may differ, "served" may be a normalised word for billed plates, or Kadamba's remark may describe a duty without describing a formula, so the question stays open for every operator until the contract wording or CDS confirms it ([[sec:a3]]). If Bakul is paid on plates served, it bears the cost of over-cooking and accepts it to avoid a shortage (candidate).

#### Influence

Influence splits by what each actor controls. Operators hold high local influence over how much is cooked, when a batch is topped up and whether a second counter opens, and they respond within 10 to 30 minutes. At Kadamba that minute-to-minute decision sits with the supervisor, at Yuktāhār with the kitchen team reading the first half hour. At Bakul the manager decides, but every top-up adds a 20 to 40 minute drive to the cooking time. Latecomers are absorbed without a decision, since students at the back door cannot be refused. Operators hold no influence over the registration cut-off, the billing rule or the service window, and their influence over the menu is contested. Bakul's manager is the clearest case: he holds pairing knowledge and sees what is eaten, yet in his account students choose the combinations, and which menu decisions he can override is unknown (single-sourced). The Mess Office holds the registration and billing rules and the only scan-level attendance data, and has no confirmed influence over how much any operator cooks. The Mess Committee holds menu authority and the final say on policy. Academic administration holds the class timetable, the variable most in conflict with breakfast hours, and exerts no influence on the mess at all. Students supply the input everyone else plans around and have no influence over any rule, except informally through Mess Cell and direct complaint at the counter.

### Key Findings {#sec-a2-findings}

1. Thirty-two candidates were narrowed to twenty-four on direct evidence; three lenses that might plausibly have produced a stakeholder (student health, senior-to-junior norms, a parental financial relationship) produced none.
2. The Mess Office is distinct from the Mess Committee, which separates who decides policy from who executes it. Every earlier account, including the team's first drafts, had collapsed the two. The Office owns the four-day registration rule, passes counts to operators, runs the platform and issued the one confirmed policy change on record, motivated by workload (candidate). It places no food orders: operators buy their own stock, and the count sets only how much cooked food they prepare.
3. The operators are the largest part the first map left out, and each runs a different planning regime ([[sec:a4]]). Yuktāhār's matching-week learning against Kadamba's fixed ratio is the cleanest comparison in the map, and Bakul's weekly judgement with a floor sits between them (candidate). At both halls with records the planning figures sit well above turnout ([[sec:a5]]).
4. The registration-to-attendance gap is measured twice and reaches nobody who sets the rules. The CDS team holds a timestamp for every Kadamba breakfast scan; Yuktāhār records registered against eaten by weekday and waste by item; Kadamba records nothing of what was eaten; at Bakul the measurement is a weekly review that may live in one head. None of these is confirmed to reach the owner of registration and billing policy, and they do not reach each other.
5. Academic administration holds the lever most likely to affect the core problem, the overlap between breakfast hours and the 8:30 AM class start, and has no relationship of any kind to the three mess-governance actors. It is the clearest sleeping stakeholder in the map. Exams, hackathons and events cut turnout and reach a kitchen only if someone raises them in a meeting ([[sec:a3]]).
6. Three student-side feedback channels (an in-app rating, a public email address and Ping) have no confirmed instance of changing a policy. The one working internal loop is Yuktāhār's Wastage Book, which took hold only once measurement was made blame-free (single-sourced). The fortnightly menu rotation is a confirmed policy change that came through the governance route from CDS to the Student Council to the Student Parliament (single-sourced, CFS Chair), through no student feedback channel.
7. The first version read registrations against capacity, markedly lower at every North Indian mess, as three independent messes pointing to cuisine. That no longer stands: Bakul is served from Hafizpet (single-sourced) and Palash from the same off-site kitchen (candidate), and food held hot for hours and softened in transit is a competing explanation (candidate). Inside a single mess, turnout differs by line, so cuisine is one factor among several.
8. The Mess Committee's chair, reported as a faculty member by a secondhand source that could not be verified, would be a second candidate sleeping stakeholder: a person with cross-institutional standing and a plausible bridge to academic administration, with no evidence that the bridge has been used. The Student Parliament's elected Mess Secretary and per-mess Deputy Secretaries (single-sourced, from its published records) are a real channel of student representation in the internal ring.
9. Surplus leaves the system through people and a bin, at least at Kadamba ([[sec:a1-purpose]]). The onward route of Kadamba's waste stays open, because the CFS Chair earlier said a collector takes it to an outside compost facility and the manager may mean only that nothing is composted on site.
10. The first version named the four-day procurement lead time as a hidden actor capping the system's response to a same-day signal. Kitchens respond within 10 to 30 minutes and buy perishables a day ahead, so that finding no longer holds. Three other hidden actors carry outsized influence: the Portal and IT owner, used constantly and named by no one; the four-day registration lock, which fixes the student's commitment long before they know whether they will eat; and each operator's first-batch share, which turns that commitment into cooked food lasting three to four hours.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a2-decisions}

A long list of candidates is generous and hard to reason about, and a short list is clear but may drop an actor who matters later. The team cut to twenty-four, recorded the five minor and three excluded candidates with the reason for each, and so left the cut open to review.

Decision: Cut the stakeholder list from thirty-two to twenty-four on direct evidence, and keep the minor and excluded candidates on record with their reasons.

The first map treated the vendors as one actor. When the team asked who decides how much food appears, it found three firms with three planning regimes, with managers, supervisors, kitchen teams and tasting staff inside each, and redrew the map with an operator cluster at its centre. Where two accounts described one body under different names (Mess Office and CDS, Mess Committee and CDS Committee, the Student Council and the Parliament's mess seat), the team recorded five unconfirmed identities and kept the bodies apart on the map and in the text.

Decision: Treat the operators as their own cluster, keep the three firms separate, and keep every unconfirmed governance identity separate until someone with authority confirms it.

On 5 September a diagram-first map and a separate stakeholder register were merged into one document in which each stakeholder carries its role, interest, need and expectation together, and the redundant file was dropped. Roles that are states of one student, such as skipping regularly or selling on Mess Cell, stayed inside the Students node, and peer influence became a relationship inside it.

Decision: Hold each stakeholder's role, interest, need and expectation in one place, and model peer influence as a relationship inside the Students node.

The deliverable follows the course's toolbox template, which covers the framework's required methods in one structure, and it was extended with a relationship network, a proximity map and a Bakul flow of information, food and complaints as the evidence arrived.

Decision: Follow the course's stakeholder-map template for the deliverable's structure and add maps only where they show something the template's map does not.

The revisions of 27 and 28 September changed the operator side most. The four-day lead time stopped being a procurement lock and became a registration lock. The vendors stopped being passive recipients of a forecast and became three planners with their own ratios. The staff eating leftovers became non-student diners, a stakeholder in their own right, and the cuisine reading of the North Indian messes was withdrawn. Bakul's third regime, its transport link, its shortage-first stance and its manager's menu knowledge entered the map on 28 September, with a research implication for Bakul residents, whose menu preferences nobody had yet asked.

Decision: When operator visits contradicted a first-version finding about vendors, lead times or ordering, replace the finding everywhere and mark the revision where it appears.
