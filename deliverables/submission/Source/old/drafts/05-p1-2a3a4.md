## Activity 3: Analysing Power, Interests and Leverage {#sec-a3}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a3-approach}

The framework asks this activity to show how differences in authority, resources, precedent and bargaining power shape the behaviour of the system, and who can and cannot influence its decisions. Two terms are kept apart throughout. Power is the capacity to influence a decision, and leverage is the capacity of a small action to produce a large effect on the system. Power is also treated as issue-specific: an actor may hold high authority over one decision and none over the next, so every claim names its domain and separates formal authority from observed influence.

The evidence came from the governance documents and the portal rules, the published roster of the Student Parliament, the interview with the facilities head on 13 September 2026, the operators' accounts (the Kadamba manager, the team's first-hand account of the Kadamba and Yuktāhār visits, the Yuktāhār site head and the Bakul manager), Kadamba's scan records, Yuktāhār's registers and the six student respondents. Accounts of earlier episodes, such as the December 2024 change to the cancellation rules, reached the team only as secondhand press summaries and are labelled candidate.

The first full draft was written on the afternoon of 6 September 2026. It rested on a power taxonomy, a 26-domain decision-rights matrix, five incentive chains, an analysis of bargaining and dependency, a search for precedent and a resource-control table. Ten open questions were written down and left unanswered. The team's answers to them were folded in the same day and changed two findings: a student representative on the mess committee softened the claim that students are consulted but cannot decide, and a prior organised student action aimed at governance replaced the idea of sleeping collective leverage with exercised-but-unmeasured leverage. A self-audit against the framework then added confidence labels where a claim had drifted (the faculty chair of the Mess Committee), warnings that chart placements are ordinal, a statement on the thinness of precedent and the table of authority by domain. Independent research the same day found that the Student Parliament elects a Mess Secretary and a Mess Deputy Secretary for each mess, an actor missing from the stakeholder map, and that the "Mess Council" which had surfaced was most likely a naming mix-up with a body at another institute. The activity was finalised that night. It was revised three times when field evidence arrived: on 27 September against the operators' interviews and the Kadamba scan records, again on 27 September against the team's first-hand field account, and on 28 September against the Bakul manager's account. The deliverable is the Stakeholder Power-Interest and Leverage Map with the Power Analysis Brief.

Two features of the result frame everything below. Power is divided among actors whose relationships are only partly established, and some of it is exercised by actors with no official standing. The two most consequential features are absences: nothing connects the one actor who controls the class schedule to the actors who control everything else about breakfast, and nothing carries the kitchens' own records of the gap to anyone who sets the rules that produce it.

### Decision-Making Authority {#sec-a3-authority}

#### Formal governance structure

Three institutional actors hold mess governance, and the first draft had collapsed who decides and who executes into one. The Mess Committee is a policy body that sets the menu about a month ahead with student input. Its separateness from the Mess Office is confirmed. That a faculty member chairs it rests on a secondhand press summary that could not be read in full (candidate), and its authority beyond menu content is unconfirmed. The Mess Office is the operational arm. It runs the registration platform, which closes about four days before service, and issues operational policy. The one policy action on record, the December 2024 tightening of the cancellation rules, was reported as issued by this office alone and motivated by staff workload (candidate). The present cap of five cancellations per meal type per month, each made at least four days ahead, is confirmed by the published rule and by students' experience. The Warden holds vendor management and structural change, though no source describes the Warden taking a specific action, so the authority is stated and not observed.

Academic administration sets the class schedule, including the 8:30 start that overlaps the breakfast window. Within that domain its authority is total, and it has no formal relationship with any mess-governance actor. Nobody has raised the overlap with it and, in the one direct account on the point, nobody has tried. The evidence favours inattention over refusal. The gap reaches the kitchen too: exams, events and holidays reach Yuktāhār only if somebody raises them in the planning meeting, "we don't come to know until we sit in the meeting" (confirmed), and no calendar feed reaches any operator.

The Student Parliament is a further actor. Elected by single transferable vote at roughly one representative for every 50 students, it keeps a Mess Secretary and a Mess Deputy Secretary for each mess, known for Yuktāhār, Kadamba and Palash from its published records (single-sourced). Student representation therefore sits in the Parliament and not in a seat that the Committee appoints. Parliament representatives also hold a standing meeting with a Mess Warden, a title not yet confirmed as the Warden above.

The CDS team is the institutional counterpart that operators actually name. At Bakul, complaints reach the manager from the CDS team and pass to the kitchen and on to the operator's managing director. At Kadamba they arrive by email from a director and the manager relays them into a messaging group that a site or facility team also reports into (both single-sourced). Kadamba's April 2026 scan records came from the CDS team (confirmed). Whether CDS is the Mess Office is unconfirmed and the two are kept separate. Oversight at Kadamba is routine and narrow. Every meal passes a fixed sequence of checks, set out in [[sec:a4]], and quality control inspects weekly, including the store, while water is read daily. Whether quality-control visits can penalise or only inspect, and whether the facility team that tastes the food is the CDS site team, is not known.

The Kadamba command chain runs from CFS to CDS, then to the contractor Prism, and inside Prism from an operations manager to a manager, a supervisor and the service staff (single-sourced). Prism is an outsourced vendor that wins by tender and runs all of Kadamba's operations. The team's site account names one person as head of CDS, while an earlier interview names a different CDS Chair. Whether these are two roles or one misnamed is open, and the names are not merged. A separate staff account of an operator's reporting chain matches the lower part of the chain, but it could not be tied to one mess, so reading it as Prism's is a candidate. [[fig:a3-command]] shows the chain with the side channel of the facility team and the flat structure of Yuktāhār as a contrast.

Two naming ambiguities remain. A body called the "Mess Council", raised earlier as the recipient of a public complaint email, is most likely a naming artefact, since a Mess Monitoring Council exists at a different institute and IIIT Hyderabad's own site never mentions one. A sharper ambiguity replaces it: secondhand sources describe what may be two bodies both called Mess Committee, a Parliament-internal committee of elected representatives that negotiates with the administration and the faculty-led policy body above it. The Kadamba and Bakul operators add a third name, a "student committee" that chooses the menu from options they supply (single-sourced). Whether that is either Committee or the CDS Committee is unconfirmed, and none is assumed to be another. For several decisions, including breakfast timing, the registration cut-off and contract terms beyond the tender, no source says who proposes, approves or can veto a change. That absence across every source is itself a finding.

#### Operator-side authority

The first version treated vendors as one box. The operators' accounts show a layered actor whose internal structure decides who holds which power.

- Contract and price terms sit with the operator's owners or managing director. Kadamba's manager does not know the plate rate: "our owners are directly dealing with them" (single-sourced).
- Quantity rules sit with the operator, and no institutional approval of them is evidenced. The standing rule at Kadamba is Prism's, the Bakul manager resets his own weekly, and Yuktāhār's is set by the daily meeting; their content is traced in [[sec:a4]].
- The in-service top-up decision sits with a different person in each mess: the supervisor at Kadamba, the daily team meeting at Yuktāhār, and at Bakul the manager alone, who phones a kitchen 8 km away (single-sourced except at Yuktāhār).
- Re-cooking, portioning, reuse and supplier choice also belong to the operator. Yuktāhār's Wastage Book works as a rule for re-cooking, it holds an impurity norm for each raw item and changes supplier when the norm is exceeded, and it visits the mills that supply its oil (confirmed). Bakul's operator switched paneer brands twice after complaints, and Kadamba's operator cut quantities and bought items outside during an LPG shortage on its own initiative (single-sourced). Who may replace broken equipment is unknown, since Kadamba's toaster was broken with no replacement.
- Menu content is the one kitchen domain where knowledge exceeds authority. The Bakul manager says students pick the combinations and some do not work as pairings: kachori belongs with chole, and Raj Kachori, listed with chana, would go better with aloo curry. He reasons from traditional and regional combinations, taste and what people eat, with about 20 years in North Indian food, and describes students as reasoning from balanced diet, protein, carbohydrate and preference. He mentioned a protein target of about 30 to 40 g a meal, of unknown origin, and sees which items move: pav bhaji draws students, and muesli and Chocos go readily. He holds consumption knowledge that the menu process does not use and has no formal power over it. Whether his view matches what Bakul residents prefer is untested, so it is not evidence of what students want (single-sourced).
- Internal structure is the sharpest contrast. Yuktāhār is flat: two project managers, no hierarchy, no storekeeper-only role, and peers checking one another's records. The mess in-charge keeps the accounts and the Wastage Register while a colleague cross-verifies the grocery register (the cross-verifier's signature on a photographed fridge log is confirmed). Leave is shared by agreement in the daily meeting, and the site head describes a proposal-and-review practice: "you follow this; if you find any problems come back to me" (confirmed). Kadamba runs the layered chain, in which the supervisor decides and no staff voice was described. Bakul's structure is known only through its complaint route and a manager who decides both the first batch and every top-up.

The operator therefore holds high local authority over the quantity, timing and reuse of food, and none over the rules that shape how many students register.

### Formal and Informal Power {#sec-a3-informal}

Formal power sits with the governance actors and academic administration, as above. Four informal actors exercise real influence without formal standing. Roommates and friends change individual attendance in a minor way, a secondary effect that six student respondents support, and friends also eat in place of registrants who stay away, which Kadamba's manager describes as routine (single-sourced). The Mess Cell community is a self-organised resale market that shows real peer-to-peer coordination, applied mostly to individual trades. Ping, the student publication, has covered mess issues at least four times according to a secondhand search summary: a November 2024 piece on a unilateral non-vegetarian cancellation, a December 2024 opinion survey, a January 2025 follow-up and a 2023 piece on an unrelated water-quality issue (candidate). It gives problems public visibility, and no source shows visibility alone producing a policy response. Finally, at least one public open-source tool lets a student manage registration through an AI agent and is described in its documentation as built for students of IIIT Hyderabad. Its existence is confirmed and its adoption and effect are not.

Late diners hold a small, routine informal power over the operator. Kadamba closes at 9:30, but when the front door closes students come in by the back door and ask for food, and the operator says it cannot refuse (single-sourced; 25 came after close on one biryani day). The records show that this is not occasional, since scans after 9:30 recur on every day of April. The operator, for its part, exercises informal power beyond its remit. Kadamba cooks about 30 portions for 30 to 50 people outside the student registration, such as night-shift security, and about half its own staff eat from the same food (single-sourced). Nobody grants or forbids these accommodations. Each absorbs part of the gap between registration and turnout without being reported, and the people fed have no voice in how much is cooked.

#### Incentive chains

Six chains connect these incentives.

1. Billing follows registration and not attendance, so nothing gives a student a reason to register only when intending to attend. The cause is structural and not a personal failing. At Kadamba registrations stay flat across the week, Sunday included, while turnout varies by weekday and by line (confirmed, records; see [[sec:a5]]).
2. Skip Meal returns nothing and resale returns value, so a student with advance notice has a rational reason to prefer resale over the system's own tool. Of six respondents, none used Skip Meal for a return of value and four used Mess Cell in some form (confirmed).
3. A walk-in price about 70 per cent above the registered rate (Rs 81 against Rs 48 for vegetarian) gives a standing reason to register under uncertainty, which inflates counts independently of any no-show. All six respondents register in advance and none day by day. Walk-in demand is real but small: Yuktāhār's breakfast register shows none to twelve unregistered, paying, faculty and staff diners a day, usually four or fewer, with twelve on the peak day of whom nine paid (confirmed).
4. The Mess Office's workload incentive produced tighter cancellation rules, which cut student flexibility and plausibly strengthened the pattern they targeted. This is a candidate case of a fix that fails, and no governance-side account of its effect confirms it.
5. Academic administration feels none of the consequences of the overlap, so the issue never reaches its agenda and persists with no actor able to resolve it alone (confirmed).
6. The payment basis decides which side of the gap an operator sits on. If operators are paid per registered plate, they are paid for the whole registration and cook only part of it (70 per cent at Kadamba, 70 to 80 per cent at Bakul), so the gap is margin and accurate registration has no financial appeal. If paid per served plate, the operator bears every portion cooked and not eaten and wants accuracy. No operator has stated a position on the cut-off. Kadamba's remark leans to the first reading. The Bakul manager says payment is on plates served (single-sourced, from a team member's normalised summary and not a transcript). The statements pull apart and are left unresolved: contracts may differ, "served" may mean plates billed, or Kadamba's remark may describe a duty. If Bakul pays for plates eaten, it bears the cost of food held against a shortage and accepts it, which would mean that it judges shortage costlier than waste, and a change that lets students leave the booking would not cut its revenue (candidate). Under per-registered-plate billing, Yuktāhār's profit goal and Kadamba's fixed ratio both gain from cooking less than is registered and differ only in pursuing the saving through records or through a rule. Both readings remain candidate. This is the most important unresolved incentive question in the analysis.

#### Bargaining power and dependency

Students depend on operators for food, while operators depend on no individual student, a strongly asymmetric relationship consistent with a walk-in price that needs no negotiation. Operators depend on registration only as a ceiling. Their real planning input is their own books at Yuktāhār, a fixed ratio at Kadamba and, at Bakul, a weekly-adjusted ratio kept at about 70 per cent or more. Neither Kadamba's ratio nor Bakul's floor is calibrated to recorded breakfast turnout. Bakul's manager expects at most about 60 per cent at breakfast, below both his first batch and his floor, so on his own numbers the batch exceeds turnout by design (candidate; no Bakul records). At Kadamba a 70 per cent first batch against 37.1 per cent turnout implies about 1.9 times over-preparation before any top-up (candidate, combining records with a single-sourced ratio). Because the ratio applies to each line separately, the gap differs by stream: 70 per cent of about 757 daily vegetarian registrations against about 245 eaten is about 2.2 times, and 70 per cent of about 315 non-vegetarian registrations against about 153 eaten is about 1.4 times (candidate). The operator reports only 5 to 10 per cent daily waste on an unstated base (single-sourced). That fits only if outside diners and staff eat most of the surplus, reheating carries it into later service, top-ups are smaller than the batch implies, or the base differs. Whichever absorber takes the surplus has a stake in the ratio staying where it is.

Operators do not depend on the Mess Office for a four-day forecast, since they procure on their own cycles and adjust cooking on the day. The Mess Office depends on students to register accurately, yet nothing in billing requires it. Academic administration and the mess depend on nothing in each other, which is a total absence of mutual dependency and not an imbalance. The Mess Cell market and the formal system are similarly independent, though the market may quietly absorb part of the formal system's waste problem without either side acknowledging it. Operators show clear bargaining power over suppliers: Bakul switched paneer brands after complaints, and Yuktāhār changes supplier when its impurity norm is exceeded and has used rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months (single-sourced). No instance of an operator bargaining with the institution is evidenced. No operator has objected to the four-day cut-off, and none is recorded raising it with a rule owner.

#### Historical precedent

The precedents are thin, and several are secondhand.

- Bakul's creation, triggered by Kadamba's non-vegetarian capacity reaching its ceiling, is a confirmed structural response to a limit. Who raised it and how is unknown.
- "Let Them Eat Frogs" (November 2024). A frog was reportedly found in Kadamba's chicken biryani, and the Mess Office cancelled non-vegetarian meals at Kadamba without stating the real reason, prompting debate across mail threads. This is secondhand and not verified in full text (candidate), but it is a clear example of unilateral and opaque decision-making.
- The December 2024 cancellation tightening was reportedly triggered by Mess Office workload and a Kadamba renovation, and came with no evidenced Committee involvement or student consultation. Pushback reportedly occurred: Ping ran a student opinion form that returned majority disappointment, and Parliament's mess representatives reportedly met the Mess Office twice before the policy took effect and then told Ping they were pushing back. The policy went ahead, which is a partial answer to whether organised student action worked (candidate). Whether this is the organised email effort that the team had otherwise only heard about is unconfirmed.
- Non-compulsory registration during shortages and holidays (date unknown). During an LPG shortage, and during holidays and fests, registration was made non-compulsory and the cancellation cap raised from five to ten, then returned (single-sourced). It is the one evidenced loosening of the rules, and it shows that the rule owner can change them quickly when a supply shock or predictable low attendance forces it.
- The Yuktāhār waste-tracking change (August to December of the operator's first year) is the one evidenced case of an actor deliberately changing its own practice to cut waste. Management introduced per-item recording, staff first resisted for fear of blame ("they are catching us"), and by December staff themselves reported waste falling (confirmed). The project managers made the weighing blame-free and discussed it daily in the team meeting (single-sourced), which is the condition under which measurement changed behaviour. The operator also tried and dropped a commercial ERP: "prediction will be based on our data only; I want to remain close to the data" (confirmed). The change stayed inside the kitchen and nothing shows it reaching any rule owner.
- "Water Mess" (2023). In a different domain at the same institution, complaints about drinking-water quality went unaddressed for months, reportedly until 38 or more typhoid cases forced a reactive response (secondhand; candidate). The shape of complaint, neglect, crisis and reactive fix recurs across the two domains.

For breakfast timing and for class-timing coordination no precedent was found, which is recorded as unknown and not as zero.

### Who Can and Cannot Influence Decisions {#sec-a3-influence}

#### Power asymmetries

Six asymmetries stand out. The Mess Office holds unilateral operational authority over students on registration and cancellation rules, so rules can be tightened without a confirmed consultation step. Operators decide how much is cooked, when to re-cook and what to reuse, while students reach them only through a weak informal channel of menu selection and counter complaints. Operators adjust on the day while students are locked in four days ahead: Kadamba keeps raw material ready to cook in 10 to 15 minutes, Bakul phones a kitchen 20 to 40 minutes away, and Kadamba orders perishables a day ahead and dry goods a week ahead (single-sourced), so the flexibility runs against the party who knows least about the morning. Academic administration holds power over every mess-governance actor combined on class start time, the variable most likely to resolve the core overlap, because its authority is total and independent and mess-side actors have no channel to it. Students hold power over every formal actor on the accuracy of registration data, because they are its only source and nothing requires it to be accurate: Yuktāhār replaces the data with its records, Kadamba discounts it by a fixed ratio and Bakul by a weekly ratio with a floor, and since the correction stays private the inaccuracy shows up in the kitchen and nowhere else. Finally, the institution is present in the kitchen at every meal, but only on safety and taste. Temperature checks, the retained sample, the display plate, the tasting, weekly inspection and daily water readings all ask whether food is safe and good, and nothing asks how much is cooked or wasted.

#### Consequence and control

Students carry high consequence with low power. They bear the most visible cost of the central problem (wasted food, lost money, energy crashes) while holding no confirmed authority over billing structure, cancellation rules or class timing. Registration is mandatory and automatic, even off campus or on holiday, and beyond five cancellations a month made four days ahead the charge cannot be switched off (confirmed). Skip Meal tells the kitchen but does not remove the charge, and the student's influence over the menu runs through a committee of unconfirmed identity.

Operators absorb consequence downstream and hold no power over its cause. Kadamba's scan records show one continuously building crowd that is still rising at 9:29 ([[sec:a5]]), and the Kadamba and Yuktāhār operators describe the closing rush as the moment they re-cook or hold reserve (confirmed, records). Kadamba's rule of thumb, about 150 diners in the last ten minutes of any meal, matches the crest, and the records put 78.8 scans a day, 19.8 per cent of the total, between 9:20 and 9:29. The Bakul manager places his main rush earlier, at 8:00 to 8:30, with 10 to 15 arriving in the last ten minutes, yet aims to have food for about 50 at 9:20 (single-sourced; no Bakul scans). A top-up from Hafizpet could not arrive before close late in the window, so held food is Bakul's only hedge, and the reserve is several times the late tail he describes (candidate).

Whether over-preparation costs the operator depends on the unconfirmed payment basis. At Kadamba, 30 to 50 outside diners and about half the staff eat from the same food, an uncounted absorber with no voice in how much is cooked, and the garbage contractor removes the rest with no voice at all. Cooked food lasts only three to four hours, so cooked surplus cannot move to another day, while Yuktāhār reuses what it can as a new dish, curd or batter (confirmed). Within a meal Kadamba's surplus is not simply discarded. In the team's own observation food is cooked ahead, reheated and moved between vessels, and it degrades fast, so diners bear part of the cost of over-preparation as lower quality (candidate; no link to skipping is evidenced). Bakul's diners bear a different cost. Labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha may start around 3 AM at Hafizpet for a 7 AM arrival, and insulated boxes holding about 60 to 72 °C keep food hot but, in the manager's account, let steam soften the parathas, so the food held longest for late diners is likely the softest (single-sourced; candidate).

Academic administration is the opposite case, with high power and low consequence. It holds total authority over the variable most likely to resolve the overlap, experiences none of the consequences of leaving it, and nothing in its mandate connects it to the mess. That is the structural reason the overlap can persist indefinitely.

Between operators and students the relationship is thin and not absent. #### Coalitions and conflicts

Students and operators may share an interest in a shorter registration cut-off. At Kadamba registration closes four days ahead while perishables are ordered the day before and dry goods a week ahead (single-sourced). The perishable tier falls after the cut-off and the dry-goods tier before it, so the four-day lock serves no evidenced procurement need, while students, for whom it is the source of most unwanted registrations, stand to gain from a shorter one. No operator has stated a position and neither side has a channel to the rule owner, so this is a potential coalition (candidate). If operators are paid per registered plate their interest in accurate registration may be weak, a counter-reading that is also candidate. Bakul may be the exception: if it is paid on plates eaten, fewer unwanted registrations would not cut its revenue and its view on a shorter cut-off would likely be neutral or favourable (candidate, resting on the unresolved payment basis).

A second conflict runs over menu criteria. In the Bakul manager's account students choose for balanced diet, protein and preference, while he would choose for traditional pairing, regional familiarity and what is eaten. Only his side has been heard, and whether Bakul residents share his view is untested, so it is recorded as a conflict between the holder of formal selection power and the holder of consumption knowledge and not as evidence of what students prefer. Convenience for students against reduced workload for the Mess Office is not a symmetric negotiation, because the Office has shown unilateral authority to settle it in its own favour. Billing certainty against attendance uncertainty is structurally misaligned and cannot be negotiated away: no rule change removes the underlying uncertainty, and a rule only shifts who bears its cost.

#### Leverage analysis

An earlier version said that the Mess Office holds both power and leverage. The Mess Office holds power and leverage over student flexibility rules, shown by a small operational change producing a system-wide behavioural effect in December 2024 (candidate), but that is a different lever from waste. The operator holds high leverage and high local power over waste and no rule-making power, and two kinds of leverage need keeping apart. Parameter leverage, meaning the first-batch ratio, the top-up threshold and the batching of each item, belongs to the operator: at Kadamba the ratio is a standing Prism rule and the fastest lever, the in-service top-up, belongs to the supervisor; at Yuktāhār both sit with the daily meeting; at Bakul both sit with the manager, bounded below by his no-shortage floor. This leverage is fast, local and needs no rule change, but tightening it alone makes the gap even less visible upstream, and cutting the first batch would take food from the outside diners and staff who eat from it. Information-flow leverage, meaning the routing of operators' records and of the CDS scan records to whoever owns the cut-off, the cancellation rules and billing, belongs to nobody. The data exist and the flow does not. The institution's per-meal tasting and weekly inspection already put it in the kitchen every day, on a route that carries safety and taste only. Operator culture is a further candidate lever: under one billing structure a management that ties waste to profit built a record system and a blame-free measurement habit, and one that treats registration as an obligation did not.

Academic administration holds power without leverage over this problem. Students individually hold neither, but the Mess Cell market shows that students collectively have real organising capacity, which has been aimed at governance at least once. That is better described as exercised-but-unmeasured leverage than as sleeping leverage.

#### Sleeping stakeholders

Academic administration remains the clearest case: total authority over class timing, no relationship to any mess-governance actor and no incentive pulling it toward engagement. The chair of the Mess Committee, if the report of a faculty chair holds, is subtler, a person with plausible informal access to academic-side actors that no student channel has and no evidence that the access has been used to raise the overlap. The operators' records are a sleeping resource and not a sleeping stakeholder. At Yuktāhār they are kept per day and per item and reconciled book against book, though the books do not always agree (the Wastage Book showed 77 breakfast diners and the attendance register 110 on 3 September), and nobody outside the kitchen has asked for them. Students collectively are ambiguous. Mess Cell proves peer-level organising capacity, and an organised effort has been aimed at governance at least once, but what it targeted and whether it changed anything are unknown, so the open question has narrowed from whether the capacity has ever been aimed at governance to what happened the one time it was.

### Implications for System Behaviour {#sec-a3-implications}

This distribution of power plausibly produces and sustains the Breakfast Paradox. Billing without an attendance incentive removes pressure on the one actor, the student, whose behaviour most directly decides the outcome. The one governance action on record served an internal operational goal and not the paradox, and may have tightened the underlying pattern (candidate). The actor best placed to resolve the second driver, the schedule overlap, is disconnected from every governance actor who might raise it.

The most concrete stabiliser sits in the kitchen. Each operator runs its own balancing loop: cook a fraction of registrations, watch the counter, top up when it runs low. This is confirmed at Yuktāhār and single-sourced at Kadamba and Bakul. It keeps the counter from running empty and keeps the gap from surfacing as a crisis. The Bakul manager states its goal outright, "there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs", and keeps food for about 70 per cent of registrations even on very low weekends. A run-out is visible and routes a complaint from the CDS team through the manager towards the managing director, while a surplus is silent, so the floor reads as the kitchen's answer to a system that reacts only to what is loud (candidate). At Kadamba the surplus is further absorbed by outside diners, staff and reheating within service, which may explain why the waste the operator reports is small, though that is not established. Because the records never travel upward, the rule-setters receive no signal that anything needs changing. The system holds itself in place because the one actor who measures the gap daily cannot change the rules that create it, and no route carries the measurement to those who can.

The channel that could generate collective pressure, the students, has been aimed at governance at least once, in an effort whose subject and outcome remain to be confirmed. The paradox may be sustained less by missing channels than by channels whose effectiveness nobody has measured, alongside one channel, from kitchen records to policy, that does not exist.

### Key Findings {#sec-a3-findings}

1. Registration without an attendance incentive. Billing follows registration, so nothing pushes students to register only when they mean to attend. This is the best-evidenced structure in the activity and is sized in [[sec:a5]].
2. Unilateral operational authority in the Mess Office. It made a real policy change in December 2024 with no evidenced Committee or student involvement beforehand (candidate; press-derived). Whether this is a standing pattern needs governance-source confirmation.
3. Total independence of academic administration from mess governance. No mandate connects the two in either direction, through inattention and not refusal, and the gap reaches the kitchen, which learns of exams and holidays by word of mouth.
4. An input that nothing requires to be accurate. Students control registration and bear no cost for its inaccuracy. Operators correct it privately: Yuktāhār with its own records, Kadamba with a fixed share as an obligation and Bakul with a weekly share and a floor on low weekends (single-sourced). The scan records measure the inaccuracy, no rule responds and the institution never sees the corrections.
5. Skip Meal as a dominated strategy. It returns nothing while resale returns partial or full value, so resale wins whenever there is advance notice (confirmed, interviews).
6. Exercised-but-unmeasured collective leverage. Mess Cell shows real self-organising capacity, and an organised effort has been aimed at governance at least once. The December 2024 Ping-survey-and-Parliament episode is a strong but unconfirmed candidate match.
7. A confirmed feedback input with an unconfirmed output. Three student channels (in-app rating, email, Ping) carry input and complaints demonstrably reach the operator's manager, but none has a confirmed instance of producing a rule change.
8. A data-rich, authority-poor operator. Yuktāhār measures the gap daily, per item and weekday, in books it reconciles, and it cannot change the cut-off, cancellation, billing or menu rules that create it. No route carries its measurement to those who can.
9. A flexibility asymmetry against the student. The cut-off binds students four days out while operators top up within the meal, and beyond five cancellations a month the booking cannot be released at all (confirmed).
10. Oversight pointed at the wrong variable. The institution is in the Kadamba kitchen every meal and weekly through quality control, and every check covers safety and taste. None covers how much is cooked or wasted.
11. Two operators, one billing rule, opposite stances. Yuktāhār, with a profit and waste goal, a flat team and blame-free measurement, learns from its books. Kadamba, run by Prism through a layered chain, cooks a fixed ratio because it is paid for registrations. That the difference follows culture and not the payment rule is a candidate, and it makes operator culture a candidate lever. Bakul adds shortage first with waste accepted, and its manager reports payment on plates served, in tension with Kadamba's remark. If that is confirmed, the operators do not share one billing rule (unresolved).
12. Menu expertise without menu authority. The Bakul manager holds consumption knowledge by item and about 20 years in North Indian food, disputes the pairings that students choose and has no formal say (single-sourced). His view of what residents would eat is untested. The finding is the mismatch between who knows what is eaten and who decides what is served.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a3-decisions}

The central methodological choice was the unit of analysis. A single score for each actor is simple but wrong, because the same actor can hold strong authority over one decision and none over the next, and a single score makes actors look powerful or powerless in general. The team judged power decision by decision and named the domain in every claim.

Decision: Power is assessed one decision at a time, and every claim states whether it rests on formal authority or on observed influence. A single power score per actor was rejected because the same actor is decisive on one question and absent on the next.

Decision: Power and leverage are analysed separately, so that the Mess Office's leverage over student flexibility rules is not confused with the operator's leverage over waste. Combining them, as the first version did, hid that the actor with the most leverage over waste has no rule-making power.

Decision: Open questions are logged and left unanswered, and raw discovery notes are kept apart from the polished brief, instead of guessing at ten unknowns to finish the analysis. Every later deliverable closes with a list of outstanding evidence for the same reason.

Decision: Chart placements are shown as ordinal and carry a warning against false precision, since no measured coordinates exist for any actor.

Decision: Overlapping or unverified body names are kept apart and none is assumed to be another: the Mess Office and CDS, the two bodies called Mess Committee, the student committee that operators name, and the two names on record for the head of CDS. The "Mess Council" is not treated as an actor.

Decision: The payment basis of operators (registered or served plates) is left unresolved for every operator, and conclusions that depend on it are labelled candidate, because the only evidence is one remark from Kadamba and one report from the Bakul manager, which pull apart.

Three groups of first-version findings changed when field evidence arrived. The Mess Office had been presumed to hold the system's evidence base, but the data sit with the operators and, separately, with the CDS team's scan records. Vendors had been placed at moderate power and low interest, passive and producing to a forecast fixed four days ahead, and that was replaced by a data-rich, authority-poor operator with high interest, since no operator cooks the full count and the four-day lead is the students' cut-off and not a procurement lock. Students had been said to have no channel to vendors, yet a weak one runs through menu selection and the counter. The second and third passes added the Kadamba command chain, the opposite stances of the two operators, the finding that oversight covers safety and taste and never quantity, and, from the Bakul manager, the payment-basis tension, the item-level share with a floor, and the menu-expertise finding. Corrections of this kind are collected in the synthesis ([[ch:synthesis]]).

## Activity 4: Tracing the Existing Process {#sec-a4}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a4-approach}

#### The operators' three logics

The three operators forecast in three ways ([[tab:a4-regimes]]). Kadamba cooks a fixed share of registrations and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond per-dish gram standards. Yuktāhār learns from its own books, looking up the matching past week before service and re-projecting from the first half hour during it. Bakul sits between: its manager varies the share by item and adjusts it weekly from past attendance by judgement, never below a floor that he keeps against shortage. Informal norms on the student side are unwritten and shared. Students register for the whole month and not day by day, which looks like the path of least resistance and which automatic registration makes the default, and one respondent habitually checks the menu first. Going early to avoid queues is a minority behaviour, since only a third of Kadamba scans arrive before 8:30. At Yuktāhār the culture is described as family-like, and one project manager trained all staff at his farmhouse on what good quality looks like and what responsibility to take (single-sourced).

Workarounds compensate for a gap in the formal system. Mess Cell resale, used by four of six, compensates for Skip Meal's lack of refund and is mirrored on the operator side by Kadamba's manager describing students who register while away while a friend eats in their place. Eating snacks or a later meal compensates for skipping without a cost-recovery path. Early registration at Kadamba compensates for a popular mess filling up, and students enter by the back door after close. On the operator side the workarounds are a fixed ratio in place of a learned forecast at Kadamba, a weekly judgement with a shortage floor and a held reserve at Bakul, raw stock held ready for a quick top-up, holding and reheating each batch's surplus at Kadamba, leftover reuse into the next meal at Yuktāhār, and extra portions for people outside the registration.

| Skip Meal | Designed to reduce kitchen preparation | Unused by any of the six. The institution's account says declarations reach the kitchen, while Kadamba says it cooks on registrations regardless; the conflict is unresolved |
| Cancellation | The correct tool for planned non-attendance | Used inconsistently; one respondent forgets |
| Automatic allocation | A fallback for students who do not register | For one respondent it is their actual registration mechanism, and it applies over holidays |
| Closing time | Entry closes at 9:30 | Scans continue past 9:30 every day, occasionally to about 10:00; refills run to 10:00; at Kadamba latecomers use the back door |

### Negotiation and Escalation Mechanisms {#sec-a4-escalation}

From the student side the first impression still holds for rules and timing: asked whether they or anyone they know tried to change a mess rule or timing, all five new respondents said no. The operator interviews change the picture in one respect. An operational complaint path exists and works for same-incident fixes and supplier changes, and the operators route it differently: Kadamba through a command chain, Yuktāhār through its daily meeting with no hierarchy ([[tab:a4-routes]]). The first version had said that no escalation path was visible.

The first version's internal operator chain, from staff through supervisor and manager to operations manager and director, came from a cut-off clip that could not be tied to one mess. The Kadamba chain above is the operator's own account, heard first-hand. One identity in it is unresolved. The team account names one person as CDS head and the facilities head's interview names another as CDS Chair, which may be two roles or one wrong name, and they are not merged. The facility team that tastes Kadamba's food with two Prism staff is likewise unidentified and may or may not be the CDS site team.

A student complaint at Kadamba shows the boundary in action. Dinner sambar was dropped "while they are taking more money from us", and the operator answered that the committee decides the menu. The complaint reached the operator and stopped there, because the operator holds no menu authority. Operators sit on the same side of the missing path as students. They have high power over quantities and none over the registration deadline, the 9:30 close, the cancellation cap or billing. At Kadamba perishables are ordered the day before and dry goods a week ahead against a lock four days out, and the operator has no channel through which to question that cut-off with anyone who sets it. The absence found in the power analysis, with feedback inputs but no outputs, is now corroborated from three sides: the governance structure, the student experience and the operators.

### Exceptions, Workarounds and Dependencies {#sec-a4-exceptions}

![Exception and workaround paths, student side and operator side.|Source: student interviews, operator accounts and Kadamba scan records.](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d9-exception-paths.png){w=86 #a4-exceptions}

#### Operator exceptions

[[fig:a4-exceptions]] sets out what goes wrong and what people do about it. On the operator side the exceptions are these.

- Scanner failure. When the QR system fails, Kadamba staff write down ID and name by hand. The April export shows no sign of bulk entry (never more than two scans in one second), so hand-logged diners may be missing from the records, a candidate undercount.
- Gas shortage. During the LPG shortage Kadamba cut quantities a little and bought some items outside. This is the first operator account of the shortage, and its cause is still single-sourced.
- Equipment failure. Kadamba's bread grill is broken and unreplaced, which removes an item from service regardless of demand.
- Events, exams and holidays. Nothing formal tells the kitchen. They reach Yuktāhār's meeting only if someone mentions them, and hackathons and exams lower turnout in ways the site head calls unavoidable.
- Supplier quality. At Yuktāhār, impurities above the norm are raised in the meeting and lead to a complaint or a new supplier. Bakul's paneer has moved between three brands after complaints.
- Biryani days. Kadamba cooks biryani lunch at 100 per cent and not 70, with a head chef brought in, so the ratio follows the operator's expectation of demand for a dish and is not a fixed rule. Portion leakage (three pieces for two) still causes late run-outs at full preparation.
- Off-site transport. Every Bakul top-up is a phone call, a cook at Hafizpet and a 20 to 40 minute drive against a fixed close, and the true lead time is unknown. The 9:20 reserve of about 50 is, as a candidate reading, the consequence: late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals.
- Latecomers. When Kadamba's front door closes at 9:30, latecomers use the back door and ask for food, and staff do not refuse. Last-minute registrations are called a pain. The speaker's wording covers both the breakfast and dinner closes, and scans after 9:30 on every April day support it at breakfast.
- Records that disagree. For breakfast on 3 September at Yuktāhār, the Wastage Book records 77 diners (28 per cent) and the attendance register, on a partly obscured row, records 110 (40 per cent). Which feeds planning is unknown. The two-book rule covers materials and not diner counts, and the two books have different keepers, which may be why the difference stands (candidate).

The operator workarounds beside these (leftover reuse, outside diners and staff eating from the same food, holding and reheating surplus, late-door service, raw stock held for a quick top-up) are absorption mechanisms. Each soaks up part of the gap before it becomes visible, and each is locally beneficial. The outside diners and the half of Kadamba's staff who eat at the mess are real people fed by the surplus, so any change to the first batch has to plan for them. Holding and reheating is the exception among absorbers: the team judged Kadamba's food to be cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, so it degrades fast. That links over-preparation to quality as an open chain, and whether poorer quality feeds skipping is not evidenced. Bakul's hot holding carries a similar cost for a different reason, since food is kept hot from an early start through transport and steam softens parathas on the way, and as a candidate reading the food kept back for late arrivals is also the food held longest. Yuktāhār tried and dropped a commercial ERP for grocery management and rush prediction, because paying "lakhs of rupees" for prediction made no sense when the data already sat in its books ("prediction will be based on our data only… I want to remain close to the data") and because staff are not digitally inclined and too few people exist to digitise, so the office digitises later.

#### Dependencies

Eating breakfast at the mess depends on waking with enough time and alertness, the most cited constraint. It depends on the desired item still being available (run-outs confirmed by at least three of six, directly or by reputation), on no clash with class-attendance pressure (explicit for one respondent), and on the mess still being open, since the 9:30 cut-off is the most requested change. The attendance-policy pressure depends on academic scheduling, which the power analysis found disconnected from mess governance.

Item availability depends in turn on a chain that the trace can now follow. The four-day lock applies to registration and not to cooking or buying. Yuktāhār buys groceries fortnightly by the stock register from many local shops and godowns, vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily. Kadamba buys in three tiers: dry goods a week ahead, perishables the day before and some vegetables two to three days ahead. Cooked quantity is set the day before and adjusted live through top-ups and re-cooks. At Bakul the first batch leaves Hafizpet before any sign of the morning's turnout, and a top-up can only add food, slowly. The binding constraint on waste is therefore cooked food prepared ahead of demand and not raw procurement: raw stock left over is issued again, while cooked food lasts only three to four hours. An earlier version had said that the Mess Office fixes each vendor's order four days out, so that nothing on the day could change what is cooked.

The operator side has its own dependencies. They include supplier cadence and quality, and gas, electricity and water, which Yuktāhār logs daily and Kadamba checks for water quality daily. They include equipment (Kadamba's roti line, multi-item cooker and tilting curry machine, and the broken grill) and one person running all of Kadamba's inventory, a single point of failure. They include the matching-week Wastage Book page, which depends on the two-week menu rotation, and the daily meeting as the only channel for event information and exceptions at Yuktāhār. Transport from Hafizpet, about 8 km and about 20 minutes early and 40 later, bounds every Bakul top-up. Yuktāhār chooses inputs for quality (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months, with visits to the mills), and staff numbers matter: Yuktāhār has 16 kitchen staff and two project managers with some site staff sharing the work, in separate tasting, cutting and cleaning teams without a hierarchy, and Kadamba has about ten chefs under a main veg chef with a head chef for biryani days.

Where uneaten food finally goes is only partly settled. The facilities head described production and plate waste going via a garbage collector to an outside compost facility. Kadamba's manager, asked about composting, said there is none and that the bin goes to the garbage contractor (single-sourced), which replaced the first assumption that composting absorbs the physical surplus. At Kadamba the surplus is absorbed by outside diners and staff, by holding and reheating, by latecomer top-ups and by the bin, and whether the contractor composts it off site is unverified. Yuktāhār's disposal route after reuse is not stated in any source. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown, with no onward route stated.

### Key Findings {#sec-a4-findings}

1. The registration-attendance gap has several mechanisms and not one. Habitual monthly over-registration made the default by automatic registration, scarcity-driven defensive registration at Kadamba, automatic allocation and simple forgetting all feed it, and the Kadamba records show their combined effect. Registration is an automatic standing booking, flat across the week while turnout varies by weekday, and not a daily demand signal.
2. Peer influence is disconfirmed as a decision-determining factor. Six respondents place it at most as a motivational add-on.
3. There are three forecasting logics, not one or two. Kadamba cooks a fixed share and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond gram standards. Bakul varies an item-dependent 70 to 80 per cent weekly from past attendance by the manager's judgement, never below about 70 per cent, so its correction closes against surplus only down to that floor, and whether it rests on any written record is unknown. Yuktāhār has no fixed ratio: it learns from its books by matching week and weekday and re-projects from the first half hour, though its head believes about 50 per cent where his register shows about 28. None of the fixed ratios is set against actual turnout. The gap is measured, locally and repeatedly, and what is missing is any route from that measurement into registration or billing rules. Even in the best-documented mess the fast correction samples the calm start of service and not the crest at the close.
4. Broken restock promises. Staff say an item will return before close and it does not, a trust and service finding first reported by students. The operator side offers a candidate mechanism: a re-cook cut-off that leaves late run-outs unfilled, top-ups of 10 to 15 minutes on site, an unmeasured 20 to 40 minute drive from an off-site kitchen against a close minutes away, equipment failures and items that float above their gram standard, together with a Kadamba chef's "wait 5 minutes" that matches what students describe hearing.
5. Under-provisioning is not the problem. Two respondents independently said that only about 30 per cent of registrants attend and the mess still struggles to serve even that smaller group, which early drafts read as under-provisioning. The attendance half is confirmed ([[sec:a5]]), and the under-provisioning reading does not hold. Every stated first-batch share sits well above turnout, at Bakul the share and weekend floor sit above even the manager's own estimate (candidate, no records), and Yuktāhār's book records leftovers. The shortage students feel is a timing and item-mix failure at the close, and the curve differs by hall, since the Bakul manager describes an earlier peak. The items students report running out (idli, puri, bhatura, fruit) include those that Kadamba says float above gram standard (puri, idli), while the ones it says waste (uttapam, set dosa, upma) are left over, and late run-outs are not re-cooked. Over-preparation and run-outs happen on the same morning.
6. The escalation-path absence is corroborated from the operator side. An operational complaint path exists and works for same-incident fixes, but no path for changing rules or timing works for students or operators.

One tension that the operator's own figures leave open should be stated. Kadamba's stated practice and stated waste do not yet add up. In April it averaged about 1,072 registrations and 398 scans a day (confirmed). A 70 per cent first batch is about 750 portions against about 400 eaten, a surplus of about 350 a day before any top-up (candidate arithmetic from the ratio and the records). The operator reports about 5 to 10 per cent daily wastage on no stated base (single-sourced). The two are compatible only if most of the surplus goes somewhere other than the bin: to the 30 to 50 outside diners and half the staff, to food held and reheated, to smaller top-ups than the ratio implies, or to a waste figure on a different base such as all meals or plate waste only. Which holds is not known, and the trace assumes none.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a4-decisions-log}

The first decision concerned what could honestly be claimed from the student material alone. The first version was split into a part established from real data and a part that was only a plan for the unobserved kitchen side, and it said so, in preference to describing the kitchens from assumption.

Decision: The student half of the trace was written first and the kitchen half was marked as unobserved until operator evidence existed, so that nothing was claimed about a process the team had not seen.

Decision: The transcript set with a labelling gap ("daily eater 2" and "daily eater 3" with one transcript) was flagged in the evidence and counted as four to five distinct people, and not silently relabelled, because neither explanation could be checked against the source. The reading of the five interviews was also compared with a teammate's independent synthesis of the same material, and two corrections were adopted because the independent reading was better supported: the registration cycle is monthly and not weekly, and an apparent two-peak crowd is one continuously building crowd cresting near the close.

Decision: Each caterer was traced end to end as its own system. One clean operator would have been easier to write, but the three differ in the way that matters, and merging them would have hidden the finding that there are three forecasting logics.

Decision: The Bakul manager's account was traced as his own statement only, with no number generalised to the other halls and every row marked as his, since no Bakul record exists. Where a Bakul statement stands in tension with Kadamba's, both are left in place and unresolved.

Decision: When field evidence contradicted a finding already written, the finding was changed in every place, and old and new versions were not blended. The trace was rewritten in three passes (27 September on the operators' interviews and scan records, 27 September again on the team's first-hand account, and 28 September on the Bakul account). Corrections include the replacement of a four-day procurement lock by a registration cut-off, the replacement of a single passive vendor by three operators with their own logics, the withdrawal of composting as an absorber, and the removal of an unattributed operator chain.

Decision: A live observation of a full breakfast service was not carried out, and the scan records were used in its place. This judged the 11,941 scans to describe the shape and timing of the crowd better than one morning of counting, and left disposal after close as an open item for direct observation.

The result of these choices was a picture in which the kitchens are not passive recipients of a count. They measure the gap, correct for it privately and absorb the cost, which made the next question, how large the gap really is, the subject of [[sec:a5]].
