## Activity 6: Developing the System Map and Causal Loop Analysis {#sec-a6}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a6-approach}

The framework asks Activity 6 for a holistic representation of the system: actors, processes, information, resources and constraints placed together, conflicts and dependencies marked, formal and informal structures shown, and bottlenecks, tensions and feedback loops made visible. The project answered with two deliverables, the System Map and the Causal Loop and Feedback Analysis. [[tab:a6-methods]] sets the framework's key activities against the methods used.

### Actors, Process, Resources and Constraints {#sec-a6-actors}

[[sec:a2]] describes the cast and [[sec:a3]] the authority structure. The map adds three features: the operator layer, drawn as three operators and not one box; a second demand stream, since Kadamba cooks about 30 extra portions for 30 to 50 people from outside the student registration and about half its own staff eat at the mess (both single-sourced), planned demand and an uncounted sink for surplus (candidate), while Yuktāhār's faculty and staff diners number zero to two a breakfast (confirmed); and boundary actors that carry material through the system without governing it, namely suppliers, the garbage contractor and the site team that relays complaints into each operator's messaging group.

#### The operator layer in cross-section

[[sec:a4]] describes how each kitchen works. Set against the records ([[fig:a6-batch]]), a 70 per cent batch at Kadamba is about 1.9 times the 37.1 per cent who eat, and about 2.6 times on a Sunday (candidate arithmetic: a stated rule applied to a month whose batches were not recorded). The operator puts waste at 5 to 10 per cent a day without saying of what (single-sourced); that fits only if outside diners, staff and reheating absorb most of the difference, or top-ups run smaller than the batch implies, or the base differs, and the evidence does not say which. On Bakul's own numbers its batch and weekend floor both exceed the highest turnout its manager expects, so 60 per cent is an upper bound if Bakul resembles the recorded halls (candidate). Yuktāhār's site head believes breakfast runs at about 50 per cent where his register averages about 28 per cent, so even the operator with the fullest records misjudges its own level.

![First batch against turnout at each hall. Only Kadamba's turnout and Yuktāhār's register are records.|Source: Kadamba scan records, April 2026; Yuktāhār registers, September 2026; operator statements (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-batch-vs-turnout.png){w=88 #a6-batch}

The halls differ in learning and not in data. Both on-site kitchens know on the day how many are eating, but only Yuktāhār writes the outcome down in a form that returns to the next decision about the same meal and treats a smaller leftover as money saved. Bakul corrects against surplus by one person's judgement, down to a floor held against shortage. Each operator won its hall by tender and is paid monthly as a lump sum on plates, with deductions (single-sourced); whether a plate is registered or served is the open question of [[sec:a3]]. Bakul and Palash share one approved off-site kitchen until a new shared kitchen opens, expected about December 2026, so part of the system is mid-transition.

#### Registration, procurement and service

[[sec:a1]] sets out the registration rules and [[sec:a5]] the counts. Four points belong to the map. First, the four-day lock applies to registration and not to buying. Yuktāhār's vegetables arrive two to three times a week and its milk daily (confirmed); its stock register shows groceries bought fortnightly while the operator describes buying the week before use, and the two are not reconciled. Kadamba buys in three tiers, dry goods a week ahead, some vegetables two to three days ahead and perishables the day before (single-sourced), so only dry goods are committed before the close (candidate). What binds waste is the cooked first batch, sized as a ratio of registrations: raw stock is recoverable, cooked food lasts three to four hours, and a batch can be topped up but never shrunk. At Kadamba the team judged that unserved food is held, reheated and moved between vessels, so some of the cost of surplus returns as poorer food (candidate).

Second, the institution's account, backed by a second source, is that Skip Meal declarations reach the kitchen and change how much is cooked. The toggle does not remove the charge, no operator names it as a planning input, and Kadamba's operator says it cooks on registrations alone (single-sourced). The conflict stays open; one reading fits both, that a declaration lowers the count before the operator reads it and the operator treats whatever count it receives as the obligation (candidate). Third, an operator's description of counts arriving "in weekly blocks" leaves open whether that and the students' automatic monthly booking are one mechanism. Fourth, exemptions exist for internships, doctoral students and long-term medical need (the monthly infrastructure fee still applies), and when the scanner fails Kadamba's staff write ID and name by hand, so the scan record may miss some diners (single-sourced).

#### The Yuktāhār book system

Yuktāhār runs on paper books that feed each other. Every book was photographed (confirmed); the connections are the operator's account (single-sourced). The day's registrations go into the header of the Grocery Book. At the planning meeting the matching-week Wastage Book entry, the weekday register and the month's pattern become a per-dish list in kilograms from the store's ingredient book, which is the issue in the Grocery Book; the stock register balances it against receipts and it drives purchasing. After each meal the outcome goes into the Wastage Book (raw issued, cooked, left, percentage, run-out time, re-cook, plate waste plus leftover) and onto a wastage board whose target is a falling waste share, and the page is read again when the menu day returns two weeks later. An item that runs out at 1:30 is re-cooked, and one that runs out at 2:20 is not.

![How Yuktāhār's books feed each other, and where the chain stops.|Source: photographed books (confirmed); connections from the operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-book-network.png){w=100 #a6-book-network}

Two rules hold the books together: what the store book says was issued must match what the physical book says was cooked, with a mismatch going to the daily meeting (the two agreed on the day checked, 4.5 kg of toor dal issued and a balance of 49.45 kg), and every book is kept by one person and cross-checked by another, "not to find fault". The books reconcile materials and not diners: for 3 September the Wastage Book and the attendance register, kept by different people, record 77 and 110 breakfasts (confirmed).

### Waste, Environment and the Routes of Surplus {#sec-a6-waste}

Kadamba's waste goes into a dustbin emptied by a garbage contractor, with no composting (single-sourced, but plain and repeated). The facilities head had described an outside compost facility; the accounts are unreconciled, the operator's is first-hand and more recent, and the contractor's onward route is unverified. Yuktāhār's route was not stated. Suspected contamination still goes to a laboratory, and Kadamba holds an external five-star Eat Right Campus rating. Yuktāhār weighs waste per item and meal and sets the next matching day's issue from it (confirmed); Kadamba's figure is a quoted percentage with no record seen. No route carries these measurements to the registration rules, the billing rules or the menu owner, and nothing suggests the institution knows that operators hold the data.

Surplus is absorbed before anyone who sets rules sees it: reuse at Yuktāhār; outside diners and half the staff at Kadamba, and reheating (candidate); the walk-in buffer; latecomers served after the nominal close; and finally the bin. Each does real good for real people, which is why the problem persists without anyone ignoring it and why no fix should work by removing one: cutting Kadamba's first batch would take food from the outside diners and staff unless it planned for them. A contractor's bin is less visible than a compost stream and mirrors the resale market on the demand side, a sensible local mechanism that quietly removes the evidence of the gap. Two smaller notes: Bakul has been described as untidy, with insects near food, which has nothing to do with registration; and Kadamba's plates serve all three meals and are washed on a staff-run conveyor, so some students distrust them despite a strong safety rating, which is a trust problem. Nobody has measured the cost of the 20,221 Kadamba breakfasts registered and never eaten in April, including the landfill and methane cost of food sent to general garbage.

### The Service Timeline and Blueprint {#sec-a6-timeline}

[[sec:a5]] reports the Kadamba crowd curve, a continuous build to a crest just before the close. For the map the point is how each operator's picture of the crest compares with the record. The Kadamba operator's rule of thumb has the same shape, about 100 early, 100 through the middle and 150 in the last ten minutes, but the records show 19.8 per cent of scans, about 79 a day, in the last ten minutes against about 43 per cent in his example, so the loudest minutes loom largest in the kitchen's memory (candidate). Yuktāhār's site head describes the same closing rush. Bakul's manager places his main rush at about 8:00 to 8:30, with 10 to 15 people in the last ten minutes, and keeps food for about 50 at 9:20 (single-sourced), so arrival curves may differ by hall (candidate).

The service blueprint ([[fig:a6-blueprint]]) separates what the student sees from what the kitchen does. Behind the line the kitchen reacts to the counter within a meal. At Kadamba the supervisor reads what is left of each item beside the scan count and tells the chef how much more to cook, and the counter asks diners to wait five minutes. Yuktāhār projects from its first half hour, checks every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close and opens a second counter in surges (checks and counter confirmed, projection single-sourced). Bakul's manager checks and phones Hafizpet. Kadamba reopens its front door for five to ten minutes for latecomers (25 students came after the close on one biryani day), and after 9:30 latecomers use the back door and the mess cannot refuse them (confirmed, from a team member's experience as a diner and the operator's account).

![Service blueprint: what the student sees above the line, what the kitchen does below it, and the one signal that crosses it.|Source: authors' analysis; Bakul differences from the manager's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-blueprint.png){w=100 #a6-blueprint}

The response runs in one direction: it adds food, counters and minutes and cannot take back a cooked batch. No signal crosses the line of visibility from one meal to the next, so nothing tells the student, or whoever sets the rules, what was left over. One artefact crosses it at every meal, a wrapped display plate at the entrance showing the full menu (confirmed), and it carries the menu and never quantity. Otherwise the student crosses only by entering a cancellation or Skip Meal on the portal or by leaving a rating. Every decision that matters to the student happens on the visible side.

### Conflicts and Dependencies {#sec-a6-conflicts}

Students depend on the operator for food, and no operator depends on any one student. Operators receive no forecast: they receive a count into which students are placed by default, which they know to be inflated, and each converts it privately into a first batch. The institution depends on students registering accurately although nothing in billing requires it, and on operators for the only item-level record of what is eaten and wasted, for which it has no channel. Operators depend on the institution for the menu, the rules and the exam and event calendar, and receive the calendar only informally; Bakul also depends on the road from Hafizpet. #### Food-safety checkpoints

Assurance is routine and runs before any laboratory step. [[sec:a4]] describes the Kadamba sequence: cooking temperature, a 72-hour sample, a wrapped display plate, serving temperature per item and a tasting by the facility team, with a weekly quality-control inspection and a daily reading of dissolved solids in cooking and drinking water. The plate and tasting are confirmed by the team's observation and the rest is single-sourced; whether faculty visitors to the store are the weekly inspection, and who the facility team is, are unknown. Yuktāhār has a tasting team and logs fridge temperatures three times a day, all within 1.4 to 3.5 °C in September (confirmed). All of this covers safety and taste and never quantity. The batch must be cooked, sampled, displayed and tasted before service opens at 7:30, from cooking that starts at 5:30, so its size is committed before a diner arrives (candidate).

### Formal and Informal Structure {#sec-a6-structure}

CDS sits under CFS. Low-intensity feedback goes straight to the CFS Head, and high-intensity feedback escalates through the Student Council to the Committee. Below CDS is a contract relation that governance diagrams usually omit: CDS tenders each hall to an outsourced vendor, which runs the kitchen. [[fig:a6-governance]] separates three routes. The complaint route exists and works for same-incident fixes (at Kadamba an email to the director passes to the manager and into a messaging group the site team also reports into; at Bakul it runs from the CDS team to the manager, the kitchen and the managing director). The escalation route has produced one real result. The rule-change route does not exist. [[sec:a3]] draws Kadamba's layered command chain.

![CDS, CFS and the vendors: the complaint route that works for incidents, the escalation route that produced the menu rotation, and the rule-change route that does not exist.|Source: authors' analysis of interviews and posters.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-governance.png){w=100 #a6-governance}

Yuktāhār has no hierarchy and everyone does every job (confirmed), and its informal structure makes the books work. There is no leave policy: anyone who needs leave raises it at the daily meeting and colleagues absorb the load. One project manager took the staff to his farmhouse for training on what good quality looks like, and staff describe a family-like culture (single-sourced).

The escalation route's one result is the clearest working feedback in the project. A long-standing complaint about a single fixed menu for the semester went through the Student Council to the Committee and came out as a menu that rotates every two weeks, which the operators confirm; the rotation makes Yuktāhār's matching-day lookup possible, so a governance loop enables a kitchen loop. The menu is chosen by students and the committee (single-sourced). Operators see which items waste every time they appear (uttapam, set dosa and upma at Kadamba, while puri, bonda, vada and idli are eaten) and have no route to the menu owner. Kadamba dropped dinner sambar and a student complained that it was removed while charges rose; Bakul's manager asked the interviewers for menu input.

Bakul's manager frames this as a question of how a menu is formed (his view only, with the students' side unheard). In his account students choose the combinations, and his objection is to pairings: kachori belongs with chole, and Raj Kachori is listed with chana where he would serve aloo curry. He describes students reasoning from balanced diet, protein and preference, and himself from traditional and regional combinations, taste and what is eaten, with about 20 years in North Indian food, and he mentioned a protein target of 30 to 40 g a meal set by someone unknown. He also sees consumption the menu owner does not: pav bhaji brings more students and muesli and Chocos "go easily". Whether residents share his view is untested. The menu owner lacks two kinds of knowledge the operator holds, what is eaten and what goes together, and neither has a route to it (candidate).

Outside the formal structure the resale market is a student messaging group, the student publication has no confirmed power to act, and at least one unofficial tool manages registration outside the portal. Academic administration has no formal or informal relationship to any part of mess governance, a confirmed absence ([[sec:a2]], [[sec:a3]]).

### The Class Schedule and Who Else Needs Breakfast {#sec-a6-class}

The project had treated 8:30 classes as an obstacle, which is probably unfair to the academic side: the timing may put harder lectures when students are freshest, let a faculty member teach once and keep the day free, or fit every programme's hours into five days on shared halls. The map does not recommend moving class time, which would treat the academic system as if its only job were to ease breakfast. The 8:30 start is a fixed condition the mess works around. The exam and event calendar is different: it reaches a kitchen only if someone raises it in the planning meeting, an absent information flow and not a scheduling clash.

Faculty and staff demand is small and counted at Yuktāhār and larger at Kadamba, where it forms part of how surplus is absorbed. Whether that is a written entitlement or a norm, whether anyone counts or bills it, and whether grounds and housekeeping staff who start before seven eat at any mess in volume are unknown. The resale group shows that students can organise around a shared problem, and a past organised email effort produced a change; today that energy goes into reselling registrations. Kadamba's manager independently describes students who register "even if you are in the village" while a friend eats in their place (single-sourced). Whether the resale group ever raised billing with the Committee or CDS is unconfirmed.

### The System Already Solved This Once {#sec-a6-precedent}

During a cooking-gas shortage the mess cut to one item at the same price and the cancellation limit rose from five to ten a month. During holidays and long weekends such as the campus festival, registration stopped being required: students could walk in and scan or register a day or two ahead at any of the four messes, and someone who neither registered nor came was not charged (single-sourced; the holiday part is disputed, since the team's own account is that students stay registered through holidays). If it holds, the institution has already run a different way of matching food to demand, aimed at this project's exact gap, more than once, and has not kept it. That turns "nobody has worked this out" into "this was worked out and switched off", and sharpens the point that responsibility is scattered across offices.

The usual reason for switching it off, protecting the vendor's four-day lead time, is weakened, because kitchens already absorb daily unpredictability by cooking a fraction of registrations and topping up, and Kadamba orders perishables one day ahead. The likelier reason is commercial: if operators are paid on registered plates, a walk-in model moves the cost of a no-show from student to operator (candidate, pending the plate basis). The exception also worked partly because it was rare and short, and partly because the live top-up was already doing most of the work (candidate).

### Bottlenecks and What Actually Closes the Loop {#sec-a6-bottlenecks}

The bottleneck is the cooked first batch, sized as a ratio of a count that is itself a standing booking, which can be topped up but never shrunk, against a crowd that at Kadamba crests in the last fifteen minutes ([[fig:a6-bottleneck]]). At Bakul the arm is longer: the batch leaves Hafizpet before any turnout signal exists, and late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals. At Kadamba the safety sequence fixes the batch before 7:30, so the constraint costs quality as well as food. The 9:30 close is a precisely timed cut-off against a crowd that peaks right before it, and in practice runs to 9:35 or 9:45. No formal channel carries the calendar to operators, and waste, measured in detail by one operator, is a signal for nothing outside that kitchen. Bakul's manager adds that a shortage is visible and routes a complaint to the managing director while a surplus is silent, so his floor reads as the kitchen-level form of a system that reacts only to what is loud (candidate).

### Culture, Health and What Is Not a Problem {#sec-a6-culture}

Breakfast menus lean toward nutrient-focused items such as dal, sprouts and plain rotis, away from the food people crave, a plausible separate reason that turnout is lower than at lunch alongside the billing incentive; Yuktāhār confirms that breakfast runs far below lunch on the same days under the same rules, so both explanations can hold. Bakul's manager offers an operator's version, that students may dislike the taste or menu or may not wake up or want to come (his view). The second agrees with what interviewed students say most often, late sleep. The first is his hypothesis, since the six student interviews do not support food quality as a primary reason and none of those students is known to live at Bakul.

A recent outbreak of hand, foot and mouth disease reportedly spread across both hostels, cause unconfirmed. Skipped breakfast did not cause it, since the disease spreads by contact, though irregular eating and shared spaces such as mess halls and washrooms would worsen an outbreak once started. Some students also skip for reasons that have nothing to do with the system: intermittent fasting, a belief that they are healthy enough, family or regional habits with no formal breakfast, dietary or religious practice, or discomfort eating certain foods such as egg somewhere visible and shared. None of that is dysfunction, and a meaningful share of low turnout is simply how a diverse group eats. It does not weaken the billing and governance findings, but no fix should be judged by bringing turnout near 100 per cent. The measure that matters is whether what is cooked tracks what is eaten.

### Key Findings of the System Map {#sec-a6-findings}

The institution has already built and run a working fix for the core problem, and it is not the standing default; the reason now looks less like the kitchens needing four days' notice and more like who pays for a no-show. The gap between registration and turnout is measured and acted on inside the kitchen every day, and no route carries it to the people who set the rules. Yuktāhār is the most data-rich actor and one of the least powerful. The three halls run three planning rules, so a finding from one should not be assumed for the others, and the two on-site kitchens under identical rules are the clearest comparison available. Breakfast is the problem meal, and at Kadamba and, by description, Yuktāhār arrivals crest in the last fifteen minutes, which is why shortage and waste share a morning. Reuse, the outside diners and staff, the bin and the resale market all absorb a cost quietly enough that nobody is forced to fix its cause, and each does real good while doing it. The class schedule probably has good reasons of its own, and a real share of the paradox is legitimate variation in how people eat.

### The Closure Test and the Loop Dashboard {#sec-a6-loops}

A causal loop exists only if the last variable in a chain causes the first. A loop with an even number of negative links reinforces whatever starts it, and one with an odd number pushes back toward a steady state. A chain that never closes is neither, so it is drawn as a chain with a dashed closing link. Each candidate mechanism was therefore asked three questions: is every link supported by evidence on its own, does the last link really cause the first, and what is the delay around the cycle. Eighteen mechanisms were tested, numbered L1 to L17 plus L3b, a reinforcing variant of L3. An earlier analysis had no operator-side evidence and treated the kitchen as a black box; opening it showed that the best-evidenced feedback in the system lives there. ### Loops That Close {#sec-a6-closed}

#### L11: Yuktāhār's waste-learning loop

Cooked quantity above what is eaten raises the leftover and run-out time written in the Wastage Book. A planning meeting held twice a day reads that record against the matching past day (the menu repeats with weeks one and three identical and weeks two and four identical, so a week-three Thursday is planned from the week-one Thursday), the month's turnout pattern, any events, and registrations counted as regular plus Jain plus cash meals, and produces the next day's issue list. A larger leftover lowers the next issue and a lower issue lowers the surplus: one negative link, so the loop balances, and every link is evidenced by the photographed pages and the site head's account. Shown the 18.7 per cent attendance of Sunday 6 September, he said the quantity would be cut "not even next month, next week." The coordinator works to push the waste share down and the operator names lower cost among its aims, so the loop has a goal. Two delays apply, the plan fixed the day before and a learning lag of one to two weeks.

![L11: Yuktāhār's Wastage Book loop, its two delays, and the preconditions that keep the record honest.|Source: Yuktāhār registers (confirmed); operator account.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l11.png){w=100 #a6-l11}

A slower delay sits behind the loop's existence: weighing began in August, staff first feared blame ("they are catching us"), the managers made it blame-free and discussed it daily, and by December staff reported lower waste (self-report; no before-and-after figures seen). Three preconditions keep the record honest: blame-free weighing, peer cross-checking by staff who rotate across every job, and the two-book rule. It all runs on paper, since the operator tried a commercial ERP system and dropped it as too costly for data already in its books (single-sourced). Four limits apply. The site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50 per cent while his register shows 17.7 to 40.2 per cent, so the loop corrects item by item while anchored on a wrong overall figure. It adjusts quantity only, never registration. It rests on one operator's account from one visit. And Kadamba keeps per-dish gram standards (four idlis from about 120 g of batter, 100 g of sambar) but no record that learns from turnout: "We don't have that idea… it was wasted many times." Operator stance may explain why the loop is present at one hall, absent at another and bounded at the third (candidate; no contract term has been seen). Bakul is a weaker second instance: its manager sets the first batch at 70 to 80 per cent of registrations by item from weekly attendance history, so a surplus can lower next week's share, but only down to a floor of about 70 per cent, and whether the review is written down is unknown.

#### L12: counter monitoring and top-up

Within a meal, food at the counter falls, a periodic check registers the risk of running out, the counter asks for more, and food rises again: one negative link, balancing, described by all three operators ([[fig:a6-l12]]). At Kadamba a supervisor watches each item against its gram standard and asks the chef for a number of portions, combining registrations with turnout so far; top-ups are ready in 10 to 15 minutes (single-sourced). At Yuktāhār staff check every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close, open a second counter at surges and re-cook by the run-out rule, and the 9:00 re-cook on 3 September is on record (confirmed). Yuktāhār also projects from the first half hour of service (use at breakfast is a candidate), but that sample comes from the part of the morning least like the close: on Kadamba's April curve 15.3 per cent of scans fall between 7:30 and 8:00, so a flat projection gives about 240 of the roughly 398 daily scans, about 40 per cent short (candidate arithmetic). At Bakul the manager checks about every 30 minutes and phones Hafizpet, a call, a cook and a drive of 20 to 40 minutes, total lead time unstated (single-sourced).

![L12: top-up at three halls, and the delay that decides how well each works.|Source: operator accounts (single-sourced); Yuktāhār registers (confirmed).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l12.png){w=100 #a6-l12}

The delay decides the behaviour. On site, a top-up requested when the final surge becomes visible lands at or after the 9:30 close, so the loop cannot track the crest and can only be pre-empted by a buffer cooked in advance, which is L14's hedge. Off site no top-up can land late in the window, so the only hedge is food already held, which is why Bakul's manager aims at food for about 50 at 9:20 against the 10 to 15 he says arrive in the last ten minutes; as a candidate reading, the reserve is the structural consequence of the transport delay, an insurance premium paid in surplus. The loop is also asymmetric. Cooked food cannot be un-cooked, so L12 corrects only upward and protects against shortage without reducing surplus. Surplus is held and reheated at Kadamba (the open branch of L13), or held hot at Bakul in insulated boxes at about 60 to 72 °C, where the reserve for the latest diners is also the food held longest and likely the softest (candidate). Reducing surplus falls to the slower L11 where it exists, and otherwise to nothing.

#### L4: the menu rotation response

This is the governance loop in which every link traces to a direct account. A single fixed menu for the semester produced menu fatigue; the feedback reached the CDS Student Council, which escalated it to the Student Parliament, and the CDS Committee approved a biweekly rotation. Variety rose and, by the facilities head's account, fatigue complaints fell; the operators' records confirm the two-week cycle. The loop shows that the escalation structure can close a feedback loop when a complaint is loud enough, which makes the gaps elsewhere diagnostic: the registration gap never produces the loud, immediate signal this route answers. A live test is under way at Kadamba, where dinner sambar was dropped and a student complained that it was removed "while they are taking more money from us" (single-sourced); the change has not been reversed. Menu and registration governance have separate owners, so L4 shows that the mechanism exists and not that it has ever been applied to the booking.

![L4: the menu-rotation loop, closed end to end by a loud complaint with a route.|Source: facilities head's account and operators' records.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l4.png){w=86 #a6-l4}

### Candidate Loops {#sec-a6-candidates}

Each of these would close if its last link held, but that link is assumed, so each is drawn dashed and not counted as a loop.

**L1, speculative registration and the resale safety net.** A student registers without firm intent, producing an unused registration that feeds the resale market. Two respondents show the first links ("Kadamba veg breakfast costs around Rs 48 and I book it every day and never avail it"; "I have both bought and sold registrations"), and Kadamba's manager describes the transfer from the kitchen side (single-sourced). [[sec:a5]] draws the loop. The first three links are confirmed. The closing link, that a lower net cost causes more speculation, is stated by no respondent, and the strongest student evidence points to securing a scarce slot: "I do register for breakfast for the whole month because Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full." Registration also needs no motive, since it is automatic, so Kadamba's flat count of about 1,070 a day is what the default produces whatever anyone intends (L13). L1 is a behaviour on top of the default and does not drive registration.

**L9, waste absorption.** Surplus passes through five routes before the bin: reuse at Yuktāhār (idli becomes upma or poha for lunch, batter returns to the fridge, milk is set as curd; confirmed); the outside diners and staff (single-sourced; whether their 30 portions sit inside or on top of the 70 per cent batch is unconfirmed); held and reheated food (the team's judgement); latecomers at the back door (L15); and a dustbin emptied by a contractor. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown. April averaged about 1,072 registered and 398 eaten a day (confirmed), so a 70 per cent first batch of about 750 leaves roughly 350 portions before any top-up (candidate arithmetic). By line, vegetarian averaged 757 registered and 245 eaten, so a 70 per cent batch is about 2.2 times what was eaten, and non-vegetarian 315 and 153, about 1.4 times. The operator's 5 to 10 per cent waste figure fits only if outsiders, staff and held food absorb most of this, if the batch is smaller in practice than the ratio implies, or if the figure uses another base. The two links that would close the loop, that absorbed surplus suppresses pressure and that suppressed pressure permits more unused registration, have never been seen to fire. L17 is the better-grounded version.

**L17, kitchen competence shifts the burden.** Operators discounting registrations and topping up live (L11, L12) relieve the gap's visible consequences every morning: queues clear, shortages are re-cooked, and surplus is reused, fed to outsiders and staff, held or binned. Because each morning is handled locally, no signal accumulates upstream, which plausibly reduces pressure for the fundamental fix, a booking that tracks the daily decision to eat ([[fig:a6-l17]]). The shift is partly benign for those inside it. The surplus has beneficiaries, so cutting the first batch takes their meals unless it plans for them, and an operator that treats the count as an obligation it is paid to meet has no motive to raise a signal upstream. Bakul's floor turns each low morning's risk of a visible shortage into a silent surplus, which it bears itself and has accepted if it is paid on plates served, as its manager reports (candidate). The symptomatic side is confirmed and the pressure-reduction link assumed, but it is better grounded than L9's version because the correction routes are observed. It is the clearest statement of why the system is stable and not broken.

![L17: kitchen competence relieves the symptom each morning, so the fundamental fix never gets pressure.|Source: authors' analysis; the dashed link is assumed.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l17.png){w=100 #a6-l17}

**L3b, Kadamba demand lock-in.** Kadamba's reputation plausibly drives scarcity, and scarcity drives slot-locking for the respondent who registers all month because Kadamba fills up. April is consistent with that, but automatic registration predicts the same pattern (about 1,070 a day, 62.9 per cent unused, vegetarian least used at 32.4 per cent), so the aggregate supports the candidate less than it first appeared to, and the Bakul operator's belief that Kadamba gets 90 to 95 per cent turnout is contradicted for breakfast by the records. The closing link, that locking reinforces the reputation, is the weakest, and the only direct testimony describes a crowded Kadamba as a negative experience. It is kept as a plausible reason Kadamba stays saturated while Bakul stays under-registered, and is unconfirmed.

**L15, the soft close.** At Kadamba, when the front door closes at 9:30 people come in by the back door and the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced). The operator's wording covers dinner as well as breakfast, so breakfast back-door entry is a candidate, supported by the records: 9.2 per cent of April scans came after 9:30, the latest at 10:07 (confirmed). If students learn that the close is soft they arrive later, which builds the late crest and brings more latecomers, a cycle with no negative link. Only the first link is evidenced.

### Chains That Do Not Close {#sec-a6-chains}

These are real, evidenced mechanisms, and none closes. For the first two that is the diagnostic point.

#### L13: from standing booking to fixed-ratio surplus

This is the core chain of the Breakfast Paradox. Registration is mandatory and automatic, running whether or not the student is on campus, and the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead (confirmed). The registered count therefore stays flat, 928 to 1,123 a day at Kadamba in April, while turnout swings with the weekday and breakfast at Yuktāhār runs far below lunch under the same booking ([[sec:a5]] gives the figures). Whether the booking cycle is weekly, as Kadamba's operator describes it, or monthly, as students experience it, is unconfirmed. The kitchen converts the flat count into a first batch under one of three regimes ([[sec:a4]]), and on the arithmetic above a fixed ratio over-prepares most on the days and the line where turnout is lowest: 1.9 times what turnout needed overall, 2.6 on Sundays and 1.7 on Tuesdays, 2.2 for vegetarian and 1.4 for non-vegetarian (candidate). At Bakul the manager's own ceiling sits below both his first batch and his floor, so over-preparation is built in on his numbers (candidate). Even full preparation runs short when portions drift: Kadamba's biryani lunch is cooked at 100 per cent, two pieces of 150 to 200 g with unlimited rice, and when some diners take three, later ones go without. At breakfast bonda, puri, vada and idli "float" above the gram standard (single-sourced).

![L13: the core chain. A flat booking meets a fixed ratio; the quality branch is dashed because its closing link is not evidenced.|Source: authors' analysis of Kadamba scan records and operator accounts.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l13.png){w=100 #a6-l13}

Four delays separate the decision from the meal. A student who wants out must cancel at least four days ahead, which decouples the daily decision to eat from the bill. Quantity is set the day before, against the procurement tiers described above. The chef arrives at 5:30 and live items start at 7:00, while Bakul's labour-heavy items may start at Hafizpet around 3 AM for a 7 AM arrival. The first batch is thus fixed three and a half to four hours before the crest, after a safety sequence that must finish before any turnout signal exists. No link runs from the surplus back to the booking or the rule: operators control quantities, nothing routes their registers to the rule owner, and Kadamba treats the count as the obligation, so nothing runs back to the ratio either. That is the structural reason the gap persists. The only loops that act on it, L11 and L12, act on the kitchen's conversion and never on the booking, and the same structure gives 56 to 89 per cent at lunch, which is why the problem belongs to breakfast.

The surplus has one more consequence at Kadamba, an open branch. In the team's judgement (candidate) the main quality problem is food cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, one of them the display plate. If lower quality led to more skipping, lower turnout would raise the surplus and the branch would close into a reinforcing loop, but that link is not evidenced, so it is dashed. Bakul has a similar branch driven by distance: food cooked hours ahead and held hot softens, as hot parathas steam in the box (single-sourced, one item, operator only), and earlier dispatch or a bigger reserve lengthens the holding (candidate). Whether texture changes taste as students judge it, and whether taste moves turnout, is untested with any Bakul resident.

#### L14: from the closing rush to a just-in-case batch

Arrivals at Kadamba crest at the close ([[sec:a5]]); about 79 scans a day fell between 9:20 and 9:29 and the rate reached about nine a minute at 9:29 (confirmed), and Yuktāhār's site head says the rush is always at closing time (confirmed). Where the crest comes at the close, demand outruns what a top-up delivers in time, so one morning produces shortage and surplus together. Shortage shows as the 9:00 re-cook at Yuktāhār, back-door service at Kadamba and run-outs on the floating items. Surplus shows as Bakul's first batch and floor under its no-shortage rule, and Kadamba's raw reserve held for a quick cook. Kadamba's extra portions are not part of the hedge, since they feed outsiders as a planned commitment.

![L14: shortage is loud and surplus is silent, so the ratio can ratchet upward. The dashed link is a candidate.|Source: authors' analysis; arrivals from Kadamba scan records.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l14.png){w=100 #a6-l14}

A candidate reinforcing closure sits on the chain. Shortages show as queues, complaints and back-door arrivals, while surplus is absorbed quietly, so an operator that raises its ratio after run-outs and never lowers it after surplus ratchets the ratio upward. No change of ratio in either direction is on record, so this is an asymmetry of intent and not an observed drift, and Bakul states its premise as a rule: shortage outweighs waste, and the weekly review stops at a floor (single-sourced). Kadamba's operator also perceives the crest as larger than measured, about 43 per cent of diners in the last ten minutes against about 20 per cent on the records, a candidate reason for setting a hedge high. The chain matters even without the ratchet, because part of the over-preparation is a rational hedge against a delay at the close.

#### L2: Skip Meal

Skip Meal lets a student who knows they will not attend tell the kitchen to prepare less. Its first link, use by students with real non-attendance intent, is confirmed and close to zero: four of six respondents who addressed it had never used it, one because "it doesn't return the money." The kitchen link is in open conflict, as set out above, and even if it holds, nothing connects the resulting waste back to whether a student uses Skip Meal next time, so no self-correcting dynamic exists to call a loop ([[fig:a6-l2]]).

![L2: Skip Meal. The student link is confirmed and near zero; the kitchen link is in open conflict.|Source: student interviews; Kadamba operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l2.png){w=100 #a6-l2}

#### L5, L6 and L7

**L5, menu transparency.** The rotation that closed L4 made the menu predictable, so a student can decide in advance not to come on a day they will dislike, a side effect the facilities head raised unprompted. Turnout does depend on the item: Yuktāhār's site head reports longer queues on aloo paratha and dosa days and 92 per cent for Sunday-night paneer, Kadamba non-vegetarian breakfasts ran at 48.5 per cent against 32.4 for vegetarian (both confirmed), and Bakul's manager says pav bhaji draws more students and muesli and Chocos go readily (single-sourced). Whether advance knowledge causes skipping, as against same-morning preference, is untested. If it does, it feeds unused registrations on particular days into L13.

**L6, shock-adaptive relaxation.** During a gas shortage, and separately during some holidays and long weekends, the institution made registration non-compulsory and billed only for what students consumed. The trigger and the walk-in mechanism are confirmed, and Kadamba's operator describes cutting quantities and buying some items outside during the shock (single-sourced). The relaxation was episodic, and which holidays were relaxed is unconfirmed. A first reading counted this as a self-limiting balancing loop in which relaxed billing raises procurement uncertainty and lowers institutional confidence in the model, but every link was assumed, and kitchens set cooked quantity the day before and buy dry goods that keep. The simpler reading fits better: the model reverts because the shortage or holiday ends, a calendar switch with no evidenced feedback. The candidate reasons it is never left on lie outside the kitchen, in whether per-plate payment counts registrations and in the convenience of an automatic default. Either way an attendance-tracked alternative has been built and has worked more than once. Yuktāhār's June Wastage Book pages add a candidate observation: two Saturday breakfasts with 37 and 34 registered record 33 and 31 diners, but those totals include unregistered and paying diners, and registered students alone numbered 22 and 21, about 60 per cent, roughly double September's 28 per cent. The regime in force in June is not known.

**L7, late-night workload to skipping.** Staying up late is the best-evidenced single cause of skipping in the project: "If I sleep around 3:00 or 4:00 a.m. or later, I don't tend to wake up. I prefer sleep over breakfast." The records match as a population pattern, since the export carries no student identifier: Sunday is Kadamba's lowest breakfast at 27.3 per cent and among Yuktāhār's lowest at 17.9 to 24.1 per cent, and Kadamba's median scan falls between 8:51 and 9:11 with the crest at the close, consistent with late waking. Yuktāhār's site head names hackathons and exams as reasons numbers fall. Bakul's manager gives two reasons, not waking and taste or menu; the first agrees with students and the second is his hypothesis, which the six interviews rarely support. What follows a skipped breakfast, an energy crash, a compensating purchase or a change in that night's sleep, has never been asked, so the rest of the chain is untested.

### Absent, Disconfirmed and Static Mechanisms {#sec-a6-absent}

A missing or disconfirmed loop is as much a finding as a working one.

**L3, capacity redistribution (strongly contraindicated).** The plausible story, that scarcity at Kadamba pushes students to Bakul or Palash, runs against the evidence: scarcity makes students lock Kadamba down harder, as the respondent who registers all month says directly. Bakul's low registration is better explained by a standing preference gap. This rests on one quote corroborated by the aggregate pattern, hence "strongly contraindicated" and not disconfirmed, and Bakul and Palash have no attendance records.

**L8, vendor quality discipline (unresolved).** Student billing tracks registration (confirmed). The vendor's payment is a different link, and the evidence on it conflicts. Bakul's manager says payment is a monthly lump sum with deductions, based on plates served, but "served" is the synthesis's wording of one remark and may mean plates billed; Kadamba's "they are getting paid for it" hints that payment follows registration without confirming it. Contracts may differ by operator and none has been seen. If vendors are paid per registered plate, falling attendance never touches vendor revenue and a fixed ratio turns part of the gap into margin; if per served plate, the vendor has a direct stake in turnout and the loop could close. Yuktāhār shows a third route: a vendor that ties waste reduction to profit gains from cutting waste whatever the plate basis. Operators already respond to item-level signals: Kadamba cut ragi idli and raised plain idli, Bakul switched paneer supplier twice after complaints, and Yuktāhār changes supplier when a grams-of-debris norm is exceeded (all single-sourced). Quality control before service is a working balancing structure at Kadamba on taste and safety ([[sec:a4]]); nothing in the sequence tests first-batch size.

**L16, operator waste to menu choice (absent).** Operators see which items waste, all three say students or the committee decide the menu, and Bakul's manager is asking for input. He holds knowledge the menu process does not use, which items go and which pairings work, though that is his view of the students' reasoning and whether Bakul residents share it is untested. Decision rights sever the loop, since operators hold the data and the committee holds the choice. L16 is the counterpart of L4, which closes because complaints are loud and have a route while waste is silent and has none.

**L10, the measured-but-unrouted gap (a static condition).** The gap is measured in at least three places: the dining office's scan export (62.9 per cent no-shows for Kadamba breakfast), Yuktāhār's register (roughly 72 per cent of registered breakfasts unused) and Yuktāhār's other books (confirmed). At Bakul the measurement is the manager's weekly review and may be unwritten. The facilities head's 35 to 40 per cent breakfast figure is now confirmed for Kadamba at 37.1 per cent. Predictable skipping is noticed and acted on in the quantity of food and never in the registration rule. The instrument exists, so the failure is a missing route, and the route is hard to build even where records exist because Yuktāhār's are on paper, digitised later by office staff. The chain from CFS through CDS to the operator runs downward, and whether any operator data runs back up it is unknown. Operators are data-rich and authority-poor: even information they need reaches them informally, as the site head says of events and exams, "We don't come to know… until we sit in the meeting." The condition stays static because the kitchen loops absorb each morning's consequence locally and nothing tracks the gap's visibility to decision-makers over time, so there is no basis for calling it reinforcing.

### Structural Causes and the Loop Picture {#sec-a6-structural}

[[sec:a7]] analyses the three structural causes and the conditions that keep them in place. For the loops they matter in three ways. Registration is an automatic, mandatory standing booking that cannot follow the morning decision to eat, which keeps the count flat in L13 whatever the motive behind each booking. Billing fires at the booking and not at consumption, which allows L1's cheap speculation, underlies L3b and L9, and is what L6 shows the institution can suspend. And the gap is measured but unrouted, because decision rights over menu, kitchen quantity, academic scheduling, registration and billing sit with different actors and none holds more than one or two; that fragmentation is why L4 can close, since the menu has a real owner and an escalation path, while L10 and L16 cannot.

Beneath the kitchen half, three kitchens convert registrations into a first batch under three regimes, and every kitchen can correct only upward within a meal. This intermediate mechanism sits downstream of the three causes and inside authority the operator already holds; it explains why L13 produces surplus on low-turnout days, why L12 cannot remove it and why L14's hedge can ratchet. Raw surplus is largely recoverable, since Yuktāhār buys groceries fortnightly, vegetables two or three times a week and milk daily (confirmed), while cooked surplus is not, so the binding delay lies between the cooked first batch and the late crest and not in the four-day cancellation lead. Yuktāhār's learning loop runs on a lag of one to two weeks and its culture change took about four months. The remaining chains stop for narrower reasons: L2 because Skip Meal pays the student nothing, L5 because item-level turnout is noticed only in the kitchen, and L7 because nobody has asked what follows a skip. The evidence runs out at three points: the plate basis that decides L8, the absence of records for Bakul, Palash and for lunch and dinner at Kadamba, and the base of Kadamba's 5 to 10 per cent waste figure.

### What This Means for the Reframe {#sec-a6-reframe}

Two kinds of closed loop are now demonstrated. L4 shows governance closing a loop end to end when a complaint is loud enough, and L11 and L12 show the kitchen closing loops every day, within the meal and across weeks. Neither touches registration. L1 and L6 fit a stable equilibrium but neither closes on the evidence, since L1's closing link is assumed and L6 is a calendar switch. Support for the equilibrium reading sits in two places. The first is the absorption layer: resale and informal transfer, the operator's discount and live top-up, reuse at Yuktāhār, outside diners and staff at Kadamba, held and reheated food (candidate), back-door service, outside venues and finally the bin. The second is the asymmetry of attention: shortages are loud and trigger fast correction, while surplus is silent, can ratchet the ratio upward (L14) and never reaches the menu owner (L16). Under the same booking rule, operator culture sets how much of the gap becomes surplus. A precedent for solving the core problem exists in L6, and two designed corrective mechanisms do not connect back to the behaviour they were built to correct: L2, and L8 if vendors are paid per registered plate. Read together, the evidence supports organisational inertia around a problem that is measured, locally managed and previously solved at least as strongly as it supports an actively self-reinforcing spiral. [[sec:a7]] takes the argument down the iceberg and [[sec:a8]] turns it into the reframed problem.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a6-decisions}

Decision: Add a service blueprint to the system map as a new method. A Checkland-style rich picture was considered and rejected because the course does not teach it and it would add notation without a finding, and relabelling an unengaged mechanism as the fixes-that-fail archetype was rejected because nothing had been applied and made things worse. The blueprint stayed because it showed which artefacts cross the line of visibility.

Decision: Draw a mechanism as a loop only if its closing link has direct evidence, and re-test closure against primary evidence in every pass. Three previously named loops became open chains on this rule and one became a calendar switch. Calling every plausible cycle a loop would have produced a richer picture that could not tell a reader what to trust.

Decision: Use one loop scheme for the whole of Phase 2, L1 to L17 plus L3b, with the loop analysis authoritative and kept as a deliverable of its own. The first system map had carried a separate set of loops (R1 and B1 to B4) that did not match the analysis.

Decision: Name the cooked first batch, sized as an uncalibrated ratio of the registration count, as the binding constraint, replacing the four-day procurement lock. Field evidence showed that the four-day close applies to registration, that operators buy on their own schedules and set quantities the day before, and that only dry goods are committed early.

Decision: Treat composting as unverified and remove it from the surplus absorbers, with the bin as the exit. Kadamba's waste goes to a contractor with no composting, and the facilities head's earlier account could not be reconciled with the operator's first-hand one.

Decision: Describe the gap as measured twice but not routed to the rule owner, and drop the earlier claim that it is invisible. The scan records and the Yuktāhār books both measure it, so what is missing is a route.

The map and the loop analysis changed under later evidence. The first drafts treated the vendors as one actor cooking to a four-day forecast, showed Kadamba and Bakul as the same fixed-ratio kitchen, and placed Bakul's kitchen near the airport with a ratio raised from 70 to 80 per cent. The operator interviews of 26 and 27 September replaced the single operator with three regimes, withdrew the kilogram figures for Kadamba's waste and moved the surplus story from composting to outside diners, staff, reheating and the bin. The Bakul account of 28 September separated Bakul from Kadamba (a floor by item and day, an off-site kitchen about 8 km away, a phoned top-up, a reserve at 9:20), brought the payment-basis question into focus and added the quality branches that depend on distance. In the loop analysis six loops once called closed were reduced to three, and the shock rule moved from a balancing loop to a calendar switch.
