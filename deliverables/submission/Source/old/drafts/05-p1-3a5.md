## Activity 5: Analysing System Behaviour over Time {#sec-a5}

### Purpose, Approach and Timeline {#sec-a5-approach}

The fifth activity asks how individual decisions accumulate into outcomes at the level of the system. The question that guided it was what pattern appears when the process repeats, across people and across time. The earlier activities described a morning that was assumed to be typical. This one replaced that assumption with measurements wherever records existed, and said plainly where only recall was available.

The evidence is of four kinds. Six student respondents described their own habits. Two sets of records cover thirty days of Kadamba breakfast registrations and timestamped scans (April 2026) and twenty days of Yuktāhār breakfast and lunch attendance registers (September 2026), photographed with the rest of Yuktāhār's daily books. The operators of three halls (Yuktāhār, Kadamba, and Bakul, run by Vijayalakshmi Caterers) were interviewed, and a team member who was present at the Kadamba and Yuktāhār conversations gave a first-hand account. Numbers read off records are confirmed, and so is practice the team saw on site. Kadamba's operator figures are single-sourced statements about his own practice. The Bakul figures come from a structured synthesis of recordings and are the manager's own statements, since no Bakul record of any kind exists. Neither a Bakul figure nor Kadamba's waste percentage, whose base is unstated, serves as a headline number.

Bakul runs a longer morning because its breakfast is cooked at Hafizpet, about 8 km away, and driven in. Every step is the manager's description (single-sourced). Labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha start at about 3 AM and leave in insulated boxes at about 60 to 72 °C, a journey of about 20 minutes early in the morning and 40 normally. They arrive at about 7 AM and wait in a hot-water setup. Service peaks at about 8:00 to 8:30. About every 30 minutes the manager compares food left with registrations and expected demand and, if short, phones Hafizpet, which cooks and sends more (cooking plus 20 to 40 minutes on the road; lead time not stated). Around 9:20 he keeps food for about 50 people, of whom about 10 to 15 arrive in the last ten minutes. For labour-heavy items the timeline begins about four hours before the food reaches the hall, and a top-up crosses the same road as the first batch. Hot parathas steam in the box and soften, so the food keeps its temperature and loses texture the longer it is held (other items unknown).

The records add two features that recall lacked. The curve opens with a burst: the first fifteen minutes after 7:30 carry more diners than any later slot until 9:00. It then runs as a flat plateau from 7:45 to 9:00 before climbing steeply, to about nine scans a minute in the final minute before 9:30. In total 40 per cent of diners arrive in the last half hour, and the first half hour carries about 15 per cent. Roughly one scan in eleven falls after the nominal close, 1,100 across the month, most before 9:35 and the latest at 10:07. The operator says that latecomers come in by the back door once the front door closes and that the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced), which the records match. The effective close is 9:35 to 9:45, set informally at the door. No bulk marking appears: never more than two scans share a second.

Kadamba's rule of thumb is about 100 diners in the opening phase, about 100 through the middle and about 150 in the last ten minutes, because "for any meal the highest flow is in the last 10 minutes" (single-sourced). The shape agrees with the records, which put the last ten minutes before 9:30 at about 79 scans a day, of about 398 a day in all. The rule slightly overstates the final surge and understates the total, and the records stay the measurement.

The closing crest is recorded at Kadamba and described at Yuktāhār, where the site head says the rush always comes at closing and staff check the counter every thirty minutes from about forty-five minutes before (single-sourced). It is not described at every hall. Bakul's manager places the main rush at about 8:00 to 8:30, with a small late tail, and holds food for about 50 around 9:20 to cover it (single-sourced; his description, not a measured curve). Arrival curves therefore differ by hall, and the Kadamba profile cannot be carried to another hall without that hall's own scans (candidate). The two timelines also meet badly at one point. Yuktāhār projects the rest of the meal from its first half hour, and on the Kadamba curve that window holds only about 15 per cent of the diners. If Yuktāhār's curve has the same shape, a straight projection reads low and pushes top-ups late, toward the crest (candidate; Yuktāhār keeps no arrival timestamps).

#### Aggregate stability despite individual divergence

The aggregate does not move the way individuals do. Each seven-day block of Kadamba's April sits between 35 and 39 per cent with no drift across the month, and the Sunday trough recurs week after week. Individual respondents are rising, falling or at zero, and the records cannot see individuals because they carry no student identity. Both hold together: heterogeneous individual trajectories sum to a stable aggregate with a strong weekly rhythm (confirmed at the aggregate). The three-to-six-week decline that one respondent reports cannot be tested against records from another month that carry no identities, and April itself shows no within-month decline.

### Examine Feedback Loops and Recurring Patterns {#sec-a5-loops}

#### R1: shifting the burden through Mess Cell

R1 is an open chain, held as a candidate reinforcing loop that sustains the dysfunction and does not grow it. Its central mechanism is confirmed. Zero of the six respondents who addressed Skip Meal have used it, and the respondent who explained why did so in the terms that the incentive analysis predicts: it does not return the money. Four of six have used Mess Cell in some form. The informal resale market works as a relief valve. It makes the underlying problem, billing that does not track attendance, tolerable enough that nobody is forced to fix the cause ([[fig:a5-r1]]). Every link is directly evidenced except the last, that relief reduces the pressure to fix billing, which is an inference that no respondent stated. Until it is evidenced, R1 is an open chain with a loop-shaped hypothesis.

![R1: Mess Cell as a shifting-the-burden loop. Dashed links are inferences that no respondent stated.|Source: student interviews; authors' analysis.](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d10-loop-r1.png){w=72 #a5-r1}

#### B1: kitchen waste control

The first reading was that, because no respondent uses Skip Meal, the kitchen-side balancing loop might not close at all. It does close, but the operator closes it, and each hall runs its own balancing loop with a different delay ([[fig:a5-b1]]).

Yuktāhār runs two balancing channels. The first sizes each item from registrations, the Wastage Book page for the matching day two weeks earlier, the weekday register and the month's turnout, in a meeting held twice a day. Having seen 18.7 per cent breakfast attendance on Sunday 6 September, the site head said the quantity would be cut "not next month, next week" (confirmed). Its delay is two weeks and it acts on surplus as well as shortage. The second channel works within the meal: the first half hour projects the rest (single-sourced), and items are re-cooked or not by a written run-out-time rule (confirmed). Its delay is minutes and it acts upward only.

Kadamba cooks 70 per cent of registrations, vegetarian and non-vegetarian separately, by gram standard. The supervisor watches the counters and orders top-ups in ten to fifteen minutes (single-sourced). The delay is minutes, and nothing recorded shrinks the next first batch.

Bakul also runs two channels. The manager's weekly review of attendance history sets 70 to 80 per cent by item and day, but on his account never below about 70 per cent, which he keeps even on low weekends under his rule that "there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs" (single-sourced). Its delay is about a week and it acts on surplus only down to that floor. The second channel is the 30-minute check and phone top-up, upward only, with cooking plus a 20 to 40 minute drive in its delay.

![B1: balancing channels in the kitchen. The link from Skip Meal to the kitchen is unresolved (dashed amber).|Source: operator interviews and Yuktāhār's books.](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d11-loop-b1.png){w=76 #a5-b1}

Kadamba's loop closes against shortage only. It adds food when the counter runs low and has no route that shrinks the first batch when it proves too large. Yuktāhār's first channel closes against surplus through the Wastage Book and weekday register, with a two-week delay. Bakul's weekly review also closes against surplus, by one person's judgement with no known record and only down to its floor, so it sits between Kadamba's fixed ratio and Yuktāhār's books. None of the three is calibrated to actual breakfast turnout. Kadamba's ratio sits well above it, the Yuktāhār site head's working figure for breakfast is about 50 per cent against about 28 per cent in his own register, and at Bakul the first batch and the weekend floor sit above even the highest turnout the manager expects, about 60 per cent (candidate; no Bakul records). Late in the window a Hafizpet top-up is unlikely to arrive before close, so Bakul's only hedge against late arrivals is food already held, and its reserve of about 50 at 9:20 is several times the 10 to 15 late arrivals (candidate reading). Skip Meal's zero student-side use is a separate fact.

Whether Skip Meal reaches Kadamba's kitchen at all is an open conflict. An earlier finding was that declarations reach the kitchen for same-week preparation. The operator's account is that the kitchen cooks for the number registered, "because it is a responsibility and they are getting paid for it" (single-sourced). The two fit together only if Skip Meal lowers the registration count that the kitchen sees, which is unconfirmed.

#### B2: capacity redistribution

B2 is weak or broken. One respondent registers for the whole month specifically because "Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full". That is direct evidence of scarcity-driven concentration of demand on the popular hall, and no evidence of organic redistribution to Bakul or Palash.

#### Same meal, two failures

The first candidate was that preparation might be calibrated to the low attendance figure, leaving real attendees under-served. That does not hold at the aggregate. Kadamba's first batch is 70 per cent of registrations, about 750 portions a day in April, while 37.1 per cent of registrants ate, about 398 a day, so the first batch alone is roughly double what is eaten before any top-up (the 70 per cent is single-sourced and the doubling a candidate inference against the confirmed 37.1 per cent). Yuktāhār's head believes about 50 per cent where his register averages about 28 per cent. What survives is that real attendees do meet run-outs, driven by timing and not by volume. A quarter of Kadamba's diners arrive in the last fifteen minutes, Yuktāhār cooks mostly in one batch, specific items draw longer queues, and the kitchen responds by re-cooking or serving latecomers at the back door. At Bakul the first batch exceeds turnout by design on the manager's own numbers, and the gap is larger if Bakul resembles the two recorded halls (candidate).

Kadamba's operator puts waste at about 5 to 10 per cent a day, without saying whether per meal or per day, by weight, or plate waste or kitchen leftover (single-sourced). That sits in tension with a first batch about twice what is eaten. Either the surplus is absorbed before it is counted, by 30 to 50 outside diners, about half the staff, reheating into later service and smaller top-ups, or the percentage uses a different base. The tension is recorded and not resolved. Over-preparation in total and shortage at the crest both come from fixed-ratio batch cooking against a curve that crests at close. No evidence links run-outs back to how students register, and none links reheated food to students skipping, so this is an open chain and not a loop.

#### Recurring patterns

The weekly rhythm is the clearest. Turnout falls every Sunday at both halls with records (Kadamba 25 to 33 per cent, Yuktāhār 18 to 24 per cent) while registrations stay put, so the system cycles weekly and registration does not cycle with it (confirmed). Bakul's manager describes weekend breakfast as very low too, a third hall showing the pattern on his description alone, and he still keeps food for about 70 per cent of registrations on those days (single-sourced).

Three kitchens use three forecasting regimes: a fixed ratio with counter reaction at Kadamba, learning from books across a two-week cycle with a within-meal projection at Yuktāhār, and a weekly judgement bounded by a floor at Bakul. Facing the same automatic registration, they state three stances. Kadamba cooks for registrations because that is what it is paid for, Yuktāhār names lower cost and higher profit as goals alongside quality (candidate reading of the difference), and Bakul's manager says a shortage is the failure to avoid and some waste is its price (single-sourced). He also says that payment is on plates served, in tension with Kadamba's remark about being paid to cook for registrations. Contracts may differ, and the basis is flagged here and not resolved (see [[sec:a3]]). Because Bakul's manager describes an earlier rush and not a closing crest, its late exposure is carried by the held reserve and not by top-ups (candidate).

Operator beliefs drift from operator records. At Kadamba the belief about timing is close to the records, and the gap lies in the level of turnout. Yuktāhār's site head believes breakfast turnout is at most about 50 per cent beside his own register's 18 to 40 per cent. The Bakul operator says that Kadamba sees 90 to 95 per cent (single-sourced) against 37 per cent in Kadamba's April records. Bakul's manager estimates his own turnout at most about 60 per cent (single-sourced), and if Bakul resembles the two recorded halls at 28 to 37 per cent, that is an upper bound and a further instance of the pattern (candidate). The numbers are confirmed from records. The narrower pattern, that operators misjudge the level of breakfast turnout more than its timing, is a candidate.

### Key Findings {#sec-a5-findings}

1. No single trend exists across individuals. At the aggregate, Kadamba turnout was stable at 35 to 39 per cent in every week of April with a Sunday trough that recurs weekly, so any claim that the paradox is getting worse or better needs multi-month records and not recall.
2. The within-morning crowd is measured and not inferred, and it differs by hall (Bakul's manager describes an earlier main rush, single-sourced).
3. Skip Meal's student-side use is zero, yet kitchen waste is balanced by operator rules with delays from minutes to two weeks. None of the three loops is calibrated to actual turnout.
4. The same breakfast fails in two directions at once: too much food in total (candidate) and item-level run-outs at the closing crest. Both follow from a fixed first batch meeting a curve that crests at close.
5. Registration does not track the daily decision to eat, and Sunday is the lowest and latest day at both halls with records.
6. Breakfast is the structurally worst meal. The "about 30 per cent" gap that two students cited is a lunch-sized figure, and the breakfast gap is 63 to 72 per cent.
7. Every recent change sits on the operator side, apart from the temporary non-compulsory registration during the LPG shortage. The one reported waste reduction came from a paper routine made blame-free and discussed daily over about four months, alongside team training. Software was tried and dropped because it cost more than the data it would add.
8. The two best-evidenced kitchens run opposite forecasting philosophies, a fixed ratio with reactive top-up and learning from books with a within-meal projection, and both are exposed to the closing crest. Bakul sits between them with a weekly judgement bounded by a shortage floor.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a5-decisions}

The record-level evidence arrived late, in batches, and each batch could have changed the findings again. Waiting for more records would have been safer and would never have ended.

Decision: Freeze the evidence at a stated date, write up what exists, and list what is missing (Bakul records, any month other than April and September, student identity, exam windows) instead of waiting for completeness.

Where recall and records disagreed, the choice was between respecting the respondents and respecting the data. The two students who said that only 30 per cent of students turn up had been read as giving an unsupported impression, and the records showed the reverse of what that reading implied.

Decision: Trust the records over recall, and use lunch at the same hall as the control for breakfast, so that the cause could be tested as the meal against the system.

The crowd curve is the clearest case of revision. The first version drew a curve from recall and reconciled two conflicting peak claims by reasoning. The scan records then confirmed the reconciled shape and added the opening burst and the late tail. Kadamba's "300 to 400 by 8:30", heard in a noisy recording, was superseded by the operator's clearer rule of about 100, 100 and 150, which has the shape of the records. A first reading of the Bakul peak at 8:30 to 9:00 was replaced by 8:00 to 8:30, and a dated change in Bakul's first-batch rule from 70 to 80 per cent was withdrawn when the clearer account showed a share that varies by item and day. The first version read the system as not in the middle of changing, and this was corrected to the stable rules and changing kitchens described above. The first loop reading of Bakul as a fixed ratio gave way to a third regime, and the candidate that preparation is calibrated to low attendance was dropped.

Decision: Treat each aggregate claim and each individual trajectory separately, and claim no semester-level trend, because the respondents move in different directions and no multi-month record exists.

Decision: Keep a loop as an open chain whenever one link is only inferred (R1, same meal two failures), so that nothing is called a loop that has not been evidenced.

## Synthesis of Phase 1 {#sec-p1-synthesis}

The five activities describe one system from five sides. Activity 1 drew the boundary and found that the stated purpose, minimising wastage through advance planning, and the mechanism that serves it are different things ([[sec:a1]]). Activity 2 found twenty-four stakeholders and an operator cluster at the centre of the map ([[sec:a2]]). Activity 3 found authority split into pieces that do not touch ([[sec:a3]]). Activity 4 followed the food and found that no kitchen cooks to the count ([[sec:a4]]), and this activity measured what the others could only describe. The nine findings that follow across the five deliverables are stated here once, with a pointer to the activity that holds the evidence.

1. Registration is a default and not a demand signal. The rules (automatic registration, a narrow early exit, billing at registration, a Skip Meal that refunds nothing, a walk-in premium) reward staying booked and ignore attendance ([[sec:a1]], [[sec:a5]]).
2. Every kitchen guesses its own conversion and every guess is too high. The three guesses differ in method, and none matches recorded turnout ([[sec:a4]], [[sec:a5]]).
3. The gap is measured twice and goes nowhere. The scan records and the operators' books both measure it, and neither reaches the owner of the registration and billing rules or the other ([[sec:a3]]).
4. The binding constraint is the cooked first batch and not procurement. Nothing shrinks a batch once it is cooked and approved ([[sec:a4]]).
5. Shortage and waste happen on the same morning, because a late crest meets a fixed first batch ([[sec:a5]]).
6. Academic administration is the clearest sleeping stakeholder. It holds the class start time, has no relationship with mess governance, and reaches the kitchen only by word of mouth ([[sec:a2]], [[sec:a3]]).
7. Oversight and feedback change kitchens and not rules. Institutional checks cover safety and taste and never quantity, and no confirmed instance exists of student input changing registration, billing or hours ([[sec:a3]]).
8. Surplus is absorbed out of sight. Resale absorbs the student's loss, and reuse, outside diners and staff, reheating and the bin absorb the food. Each absorber is locally useful and some feed real people, so any cut to the first batch must plan for them ([[sec:a4]]).
9. The most requested fix among respondents was a longer close, with a pay-as-you-go alternative to fixed registration proposed as a structural option ([[sec:a4]]).

Phase 1 is complete for the student half and the kitchen half. Disposal after close remains unobserved, and semester-level trends are unmeasured. These gaps and the corrections made during Phase 1 are collected in [[ch:synthesis]], and the next phase turns the findings into a model of the system ([[ch:phase2]]).
