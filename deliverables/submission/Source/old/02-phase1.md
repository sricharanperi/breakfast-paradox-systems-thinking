# Phase 1: Understanding the System {#ch-phase1}

Phase 1 asked the team to understand the problem as part of a larger system, beyond the operations of a single kitchen. It ran from 3 to about 6 September 2026 in its first form and was revised twice as the field evidence arrived. The five activities are reported in the order in which they were done. Each began from the output of the one before, and each was revised later, so the findings given here are the revised ones; the changes are listed in [[sec:corrections]].

## Activity 1: Understanding the System Context {#sec-a1}

#### Purpose

The purpose of the first activity was to set the boundary of the system, to describe what the system is meant to do and what it does in practice, and to separate the visible symptoms from the underlying issues before any explanation was attempted.

#### Method

Boundary framing was used. Every element that the student meets in the process of getting a breakfast was listed, and each was then asked what it depends on. Elements were sorted into those inside the boundary, those on its edge, which exchange food, money or information with the system but are not run by it, and those outside. The stated purpose of the system, taken from the posters in the dining halls, was compared with the mechanism that the published rules actually set up.

#### Process

The team began with the portal screens, the published rules and its own first interview, held on 3 September with a team member who lives in a hostel and often skips breakfast. The opening description of the puzzle was written out as a chain of causes, and each link was marked according to whether any evidence supported it. Only the first link, that late nights lead to skipped mornings, had testimony behind it. The link from the energy crash to a second purchase at the canteen had none, and the team set it aside as a hypothesis. The boundary was drawn in three layers, shown in [[fig:boundary]]. These are the registration and billing layer (the portal, the cancellation function, the monthly bill and the scan at the counter), the kitchen layer (each caterer's planning, cooking, top-up, reuse and disposal) and the governance layer (the bodies that set the menu, the rules and the contracts).

![The breakfast system: three layers inside the boundary, actors on its edge, and the elements outside.|Source: authors' analysis of the portal, published rules and interviews.](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/diagram1-system-boundary.png){w=100 #boundary}

#### Deliverable

The System Context Brief (Deliverable 1) contains the boundary, the purpose and intended outcomes, the visible symptoms and underlying issues, the key actors, institutions, processes, resources and constraints, and an initial statement of the problem.

#### Findings

The institution's stated method is minimising food wastage through advance meal planning. The mechanism does something else. A student is registered automatically for every meal, on campus or away, and the only way out is five cancellations per meal type per month, each made at least four days in advance (confirmed). The charge follows the registration whether or not the plate is eaten, and the portal's Skip Meal button tells the kitchen but returns no money (confirmed). A student who knows in advance therefore has a better option than the official one, which is to resell the registration on a student messaging group. The registered breakfast is priced at Rs 48 for the vegetarian option and Rs 66 for the non-vegetarian option, with Rs 53 at Yuktāhār, and a walk-in costs about seventy per cent more, according to the portal.

The visible symptom is that about two thirds of breakfast registrations go unused, a figure that the records of Activity 5 later made exact. The underlying issues are that registration is a default that carries no signal of demand, that each kitchen guesses its own conversion from registrations to food, and that items which always waste are seen by the operator and have no route to the menu.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

The team could have taken the opening description at face value and started on a plan at once, or could have doubted it and started more slowly. A neat story that proves wrong shapes every later question, so the team chose to doubt it (Decision 1). The second choice was between studying the whole mess and studying breakfast only. The whole mess would give more to say, while breakfast alone allowed depth. Breakfast was chosen, with lunch and dinner kept as a control, since the same portal, rules and caterer apply to them (Decision 2). The third decision was to state how sure the team was of every fact, using the labels of [[tab:labels]], and to carry them through all later documents (Decision 3). After this activity the guiding question widened from why students sleep through breakfast to what arrangement produces a booking that nobody acts on.

## Activity 2: Identifying and Mapping Stakeholders {#sec-a2}

#### Purpose

The second activity aimed to find out who takes part in the system, who benefits from it and who is affected by it, together with what each of them needs and how they are connected.

#### Method

Candidates were generated by looking at the breakfast through seventeen lenses, among them students, money, technology, waste, governance, the academic calendar, sleep and rival sources of food. Each candidate was then tested for a real role, a real stake, or only a place in the team's imagination. The survivors were arranged in tiers (primary, secondary and indirect), related to each other in a matrix of dependencies, influence and conflict, and placed on rings according to their proximity to the student's morning.

#### Process

The team started with thirty-two candidates and ended with twenty-four. Three lenses did not survive the evidence, since nothing connected a campus health office, senior students passing on habits, or parents paying per meal to the skipping of breakfast. They were kept on record so that the decision could be reviewed. The first map treated the caterers as one grey box labelled vendors. When the team asked who decides how much food appears, it found three firms with three ways of working, together with managers, supervisors, kitchen teams and tasting staff inside each. The map was redrawn with an operator cluster at its centre, and a group that the team had not imagined was added: people outside the student registration who eat from the same pots. [[fig:stakeholders]] shows the revised map and [[fig:onion]] the proximity rings.

![Stakeholder map: twenty-four stakeholders in four groups, with the student at the lower centre.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/diagram3-stakeholder-map.png){w=100 #stakeholders}

![Proximity of stakeholders to the student's morning.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/diagram5-onion-proximity.png){w=78 #onion}

#### Deliverable

The Stakeholder Map (Deliverable 2) holds the use case, the primary, secondary and indirect stakeholders, their interests and expectations, their formal and informal roles, the network and proximity maps, and the relationships, dependencies and conflicts among them.

#### Findings

Seven stakeholders are primary: the students, the three operators, the operators' site management, serving staff and kitchen staff. Five are secondary, among them the Mess Committee, the Mess Office, the site team of the dining services body, the Warden and the Student Parliament's mess seats. The other twelve are indirect, external or informal. The most important finding about the cast was an absence. The academic administration, which controls the class timetable, has no relationship of any kind with the people who run the mess, and nobody has raised the clash or tried to. The team read this as inattention, which is cheaper to correct than refusal. The map also showed an informal layer that works alongside the official one: the resale group, a student-run publication that comments on the mess, and unofficial tools built by students.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

Keeping every candidate gives a generous list that is hard to reason about, while cutting to those with evidence gives a clear list that may lose someone who matters later. The team cut to twenty-four and kept the three dropped lenses on record. It also decided to treat the operators as their own cluster, with the three firms kept separate (Decision 4).

## Activity 3: Analysing Power, Interests and Leverage {#sec-a3}

#### Purpose

The third activity sought to understand how differences in authority, resources and bargaining position shape the behaviour of the system, and which stakeholders can and cannot influence the outcome.

#### Method

The analysis went decision by decision. For each decision in the system (the registration rule, the cancellation cut-off, the menu, the first batch of food, the class start and the contract) the team asked who proposes it, who approves it and who bears its consequences. For each stakeholder it then compared the interest they state, what the system rewards, and what they are seen to do, which gave an incentive chain. Two quantities were marked separately on every stakeholder: power, the formal ability to decide, and leverage, the effect a small push from that stakeholder would have on the outcome. Information flows were traced to see where each record stops.

#### Process

The team went through the governance documents, the account given by the facilities head and the descriptions from the operators, and recorded for each decision whether authority was formal or observed influence. The result was placed on a power and interest grid ([[fig:grid]]) and on a map of the flows of authority and information ([[fig:flow]]). The placement on the grid is ordinal. Where a stakeholder sits matters more than the exact position.

![Power and interest of the stakeholders, placed by judgement.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d13-power-interest-v2.png){w=88 #grid}

![Power and information flow: where channels exist and where they stop.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d14-power-flow-network.png){w=100 #flow}

#### Deliverable

The Stakeholder Power-Interest and Leverage Map and Power Analysis Brief (Deliverable 3) contains the authority analysis, formal and informal power, incentives and motivations, the influence analysis, the analysis of unequal access to resources and information, the maps, and the implications for system behaviour.

#### Findings

Authority is divided into pieces that do not touch. A committee sets the menu, an office runs the portal and its rules, the Warden holds the vendor contracts, and the academic side holds the timetable. Each holds one piece and none holds the whole outcome of booked, cooked and eaten. Students bear the cost and hold the least power. The operators hold the most knowledge and the least authority: each decides how much to cook, but none of them can change the registration rule, the cut-off, the bill or the menu. The measurement of the gap is held in two places, by the dining office through its scans and by the kitchens through their books, and neither reaches the owner of the rules. The three operators read the count with different aims. Kadamba treats it as an obligation that it is paid for, and Yuktāhār treats it as a cost to cut. Whether an operator's monthly payment follows registered plates or served plates is unconfirmed for every operator, and [[sec:a8]] returns to it because so much turns on the answer.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

A single score for each actor is simple and wrong, since the same actor can have strong authority over one decision and none over the next, and a single score makes people look powerful or powerless in general. The team judged power one decision at a time and stated whether it was formal authority or observed influence (Decision 5). A further result was a change in attitude: everyone the team met had a sensible reason for what they did, and the search for a bad actor ended. The trouble lay in how sensible actions connect, which could only be seen by following the food.

## Activity 4: Tracing the Existing Process {#sec-a4}

#### Purpose

The fourth activity set out to understand how the system actually operates, as distinct from how it is supposed to, and to record the rules, the escalation routes and the workarounds.

#### Method

The team used interviews and observation. The student half was traced from six accounts, and the operator half from interviews and kitchen visits. Each decision was written down together with the person who takes it, the information on which it is based, and the exception paths around it. Formal rules were separated from informal ones, and the points where they diverge were listed.

#### Process

For the student half, five students were interviewed and the team member's first account was added, making six voices. They described how they decide whether to wake, whether to check the app, whether to go early, whether to cancel and whether to resell. One transcript had a labelling gap, so the team counted four to five distinct people and said so. [[fig:daily]] shows the decision cycle on the student side. The operator half was dark at first, because nobody on the student side could see how a registration became a quantity of food. The team had assumed that the institution sends each kitchen a count four days ahead and that the kitchen cooks to it. Visits to the kitchens and the request to see their records overturned this. One kitchen allowed its books to be photographed page by page, and the operators described their days. [[fig:kadamba-process]] gives the operator side at Kadamba and [[fig:exceptions]] the exceptions that people use to get around the rules.

#### Deliverable

The Current-State Process Map and Process Trace (Deliverable 4) records the interviews and observations, the decision points for students and operators, the formal and informal rules, the negotiation and escalation routes, and the exceptions, workarounds and dependencies.

#### Findings

There is no forecast. The four-day close applies only to the registration count. Purchasing follows each kitchen's own schedule, and each caterer converts the closed count into food by its own rule ([[tab:kitchens]]). All three kitchens share two properties. A cooked batch can be topped up but never reduced, and the first batch is fixed before any diner arrives. At Kadamba every meal also passes a safety sequence of cooking temperature, a sample kept for seventy-two hours, a wrapped display plate at the entrance, serving temperature and a tasting by the facility team. The sequence checks safety and taste and never checks how much was cooked.

The student side held a result that the team had not expected. Four of six respondents had bought or sold a registration through the messaging group, and none had used Skip Meal to recover any value. The unofficial market worked better than the official tool, so the official tool was ignored. A hypothesis carried from the start also failed: friends were not found to pull each other to breakfast. Six respondents gave the same answer, that a friend might make a morning more pleasant but nobody skips because a friend skips.

Several earlier statements changed. No forecast is locked four days ahead. Leftover food at Kadamba goes to a garbage contractor, and nothing is composted. The food that students leave is eaten by people outside the student registration: about thirty extra portions are cooked in advance for thirty to fifty of them, such as night-shift security, and about half of Kadamba's own staff eat the same food.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

Describing one clean operator would have been easier to write, but the three differ in the way that matters, and merging them would have hidden the finding. Each caterer was traced from registration to bin (Decision 6). The second decision concerned revision. When field evidence contradicted a finding already written, the team changed the finding everywhere. This was done in three passes as evidence arrived in batches, and old and new versions were not blended (Decision 7). The visits changed the team's picture of the kitchens from passive recipients of a count to active measurers of the gap, which corrected for it privately and absorbed the cost. The obvious next question was how large the gap is.

## Activity 5: Analysing System Behaviour over Time {#sec-a5}

#### Purpose

The purpose of the fifth activity was to understand how individual decisions accumulate into outcomes at the level of the system, by looking at the records over a morning, a week and a month, at significant changes and their triggers, and at recurring patterns.

#### Method

Records were collected and analysed as behaviour-over-time charts (Sterman, 2000). Timelines were built at four scales: the service day, the weekday pattern, the month, and the planning horizon, which shows when each decision in the chain becomes fixed. Significant changes in rules and operations were placed on an event timeline, with undated items kept apart and no date guessed. Candidate feedback patterns and unintended consequences were noted for testing in Phase 2.

#### Process

The team asked the dining office for the one thing that only it holds, which was a record of every breakfast scan at Kadamba for April 2026. It contained 32,162 booking rows. It then photographed Yuktāhār's attendance registers for twenty days in September, with breakfast and lunch side by side. Until these arrived, everything the team knew about the crowd came from six people recalling their mornings. Turnout was computed as scans divided by registrations, by day, weekday, line and quarter hour.

#### Deliverable

The System Timeline and Behaviour-Over-Time Map (Deliverable 5) contains the records and observations, the timelines, the significant changes and their triggers, the feedback patterns and the unintended consequences, together with an account of outstanding data.

#### Findings

Of 32,162 breakfast registrations at Kadamba in April, 11,941 were scanned, which is 37.1 per cent, and 62.9 per cent were not (confirmed). The registration count hardly moved and stayed near 1,070 a day, Sunday included, while the number who ate rose and fell with the weekday. Turnout ran from 42.1 per cent on Tuesdays to 27.3 per cent on Sundays, with the three lowest days of the month all Sundays ([[fig:weekday]]). A registration made once and left standing cannot follow a decision made each morning. The arrivals are late ([[fig:crowd]]). A small burst at 7:30 gives way to a flat plateau until about nine and then a steep climb, so that 26.5 per cent of all scans fall between 9:15 and 9:30, and the rate is still rising at 9:29. A further 9.2 per cent arrive after the close, through a back door that staff do not close. The latest scan was at 10:07. On vegetarian and non-vegetarian lines, turnout was 32.4 per cent and 48.5 per cent, so the line mattered more than the cuisine.

![Share of Kadamba registrations eaten, by weekday, April 2026.|Source: Kadamba scan export, April 2026 (confirmed).](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d19-weekday-turnout.png){w=82 #weekday}

![Arrival times of Kadamba's breakfast diners, April 2026.|Source: Kadamba scan export, April 2026 (confirmed).](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/diagram8-crowd-curve.png){w=96 #crowd}

At Yuktāhār the two meals could be compared from the same book, on the same days, with the same cooks and the same portal. Lunch ran between 56 and 89 per cent and breakfast between 17.7 and 40.2 per cent, with a mean near 28 per cent ([[fig:yuk]]). The head of that kitchen keeps the best records on the campus, and still believed breakfast turnout to be about fifty per cent. Even people who hold the numbers misjudged the level. Individual students changed their habits in different directions during the month, but the totals did not: each week of April sat between 35 and 39 per cent.

![Yuktāhār breakfast and lunch turnout, September 2026. The dashed line marks the level that the head of the kitchen believed breakfast to have.|Source: Yuktāhār attendance registers, twenty days of September 2026 (confirmed).](deliverables/phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d20-yuktahaar-breakfast-vs-lunch.png){w=96 #yuk}

The gap was twice the size in the first submission, which had said that about thirty per cent of registrations go unused. Two students had said that only thirty per cent turn up, and the team had read that as an unsupported impression. The records showed the opposite: about 63 per cent of Kadamba's breakfasts go unused, and about 72 per cent at Yuktāhār. Thirty per cent was a lunch-sized number. On the planning side, registration is fixed a month ahead as a default, groceries are bought about two weeks before at Yuktāhār, the count closes four days ahead, and the first batch is cooked from 5:30 on the day. The rules for registration and billing have been unchanged since early 2025, while the kitchens behind them have changed several times.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

The team could wait for more records, which would have been safer and would never have ended, or write with what it had. New data arrived in batches and each batch meant revision. The team froze the evidence at a stated date, wrote it up, and listed what was missing: Bakul has no records at all, and no month other than April or September is covered (Decision 9). Where recall and records disagreed, the records were trusted, and lunch at the same kitchen was used as the control for breakfast (Decision 8). The gap was now seen to be a puzzle about kitchens as well as students. If only a third of registrants eat, the question becomes what the kitchens cook for.

## Synthesis of Phase 1 {#sec-p1synth}

Taken together, the five activities produced a picture in which registration is a default that carries no signal of demand, every kitchen applies its own guess to it, and the gap is measured twice without being passed on. Four statements summarise what Phase 1 established. The binding constraint is the first batch, which is cooked before anyone arrives and cannot be reduced; the kitchens buy on their own schedules. Shortage and surplus occur on the same morning at Kadamba, because a late crowd meets a fixed batch. Academic administration is the clearest sleeping stakeholder. Surplus is absorbed out of sight, by resale for the student and by reuse, outside diners, reheating and the bin for the food, and each absorber is locally useful. These statements frame the model built in Phase 2.
