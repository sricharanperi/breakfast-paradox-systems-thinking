# Synthesis of Findings {#ch-synthesis}

## Findings by Research Question {#sec-byrq}

The four research questions of [[ch:method]] can now be answered from the work of the four phases.

**How does a registration become cooked food, and where does the food go?** It does not become food by any common rule. After the registration closes four days ahead, each operator applies its own conversion to the count: a fixed share at Kadamba, a book-based lookup at Yuktāhār and a weekly judgement with a floor at Bakul. The first batch is fixed before any diner arrives and can be topped up but never reduced. Surplus leaves by reuse, by outside diners and staff, by reheating, by the bin and, for students, by resale.

**Who has authority and who has leverage?** Authority over the registration rule, the cut-off, the bill, the menu and the timetable is divided among offices that do not coordinate. The operators have the knowledge and a degree of local power over quantity and no authority over the rules. Students bear the cost and have the least power. Academic administration is a sleeping stakeholder with no channel to the mess.

**Why does the gap persist when it is measured every day?** Three structures keep it in place: an automatic standing registration with a narrow early exit, billing at the moment of registration, and measurement that is not routed to anyone who sets the rules. Two conditions keep those structures from being questioned, namely that attention goes to loud problems and that an absorption layer takes each consequence before it becomes a signal.

**Where could a change act, what must it protect and what would it do?** Four intervention points lie along the chain, twelve leverage points were identified, and seven principles say what a design must respect. The recommended design builds the missing route, changes the default on a fixed date and tunes the kitchen beside them. In the model its effect in the kitchen is large and the effect on students is small until the rule moves.

## Decisions across the Project {#sec-decisions-summary}

Twenty-three decisions shaped the project, and they are listed in full in Appendix B. They fall into four groups. Six decisions concern scope and evidence: doubting the opening description, studying breakfast only with lunch as a control, labelling every fact, freezing the evidence at a stated date, trusting records over recall, and changing a finding everywhere when evidence overturned it. Six concern how the system was drawn: separating the operators, judging power decision by decision, tracing each caterer, building the map around the registration count, accepting a loop only when every link is evidenced, and placing the cause in the structure. Six concern the problem and the levers: reframing the problem as a stable equilibrium, never removing an absorber to create a signal, holding ideas that depend on the payment basis, ranking by depth and then by feasibility, making measurement blame-free, and keeping a foil. The last five concern the design: gates before scores, a Sunday skip on a fixed date, a pilot on Kadamba's vegetarian Sunday line, sizing nothing for Bakul, and a planned withdrawal of the team.

# Discussion {#ch-discussion}

## Interpretation of the Main Findings {#sec-interpretation}

The central finding is that the gap between registration and attendance arises from a default that no one chose to keep, and that the gap is measured every day. This is the pattern that Meadows (2008) describes when she says that structure produces behaviour: sensible people acting on the signals they receive produce an outcome that none of them would design. The records strengthen the reading. The gap is about equally large at Kadamba and at Yuktāhār, which share the rules and differ in kitchen practice, so the registration structure explains it and the kitchen culture only decides how much of it becomes surplus. Yuktāhār, the one hall that uses its records to plan, shows the same gap. Learning in the kitchen therefore cannot close a gap that begins at the registration.

The finding that every loop that works sits inside a kitchen is also a finding about information. The system corrects locally and has no correction at the centre, which is why a quick fix in the kitchen can make the central problem harder to see. This was the reason for the decision to fix the Sunday skip on a date. The shifting-the-burden archetype (Senge, 1994) predicted the risk, and the gates were written to guard against it.

## Key Design Decisions and Trade-offs {#sec-tradeoffs}

The recommended design rests on trade-offs that are summarised in [[tab:tradeoffs]]. Each was a real choice between options that each had a cost, and the reasons are those given in the phase chapters.

Table: Principal trade-offs and the choices made. {#tradeoffs cols=26,28,46}
| Question | Choice made | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Believe the opening description or doubt it | Doubt it and test each link | A neat story that proves wrong shapes every later question |
| Whole mess or breakfast only | Breakfast, with lunch and dinner as a control | Depth, and a way to tell whether the cause is the meal or the system |
| One operator or three | Three, traced separately | The differences were the finding |
| Score the actor or the decision | The decision | The same actor can be decisive on one question and absent on the next |
| Many loops or few | Few, each fully evidenced | A loop with an assumed link is a hypothesis |
| One root cause or a structure | A structure of three parts and two conditions | The evidence did not support a single cause |
| Deepest lever or the one the team can move | Rank by depth, then judge feasibility | The team can build routes and propose, not instruct |
| Test only good ideas or include a foil | Include a foil in every test | Otherwise every test passes |
| Kitchen pilot decides the exit or a fixed date | A fixed date | A better kitchen can make the registration look unimportant |

## Implications for the Institution {#sec-implications}

The recommendation asks little of students and a good deal of the institution. The records that would calibrate every kitchen are already kept, so the first step costs a weekly sheet and a meeting. The second step is a decision for the rule owners, who today hold only fragments of the outcome: a Sunday skip that removes the charge. A third implication concerns ownership. No office is responsible for the whole chain from registration to bin, and the project holds back the proposal of a named owner until the payment basis is known, since that fact decides who has a reason to close the gap. The institution's own posters give its aim as minimising food wastage through advance meal planning. The analysis suggests that the planning the posters describe happens at the registration, which is not where the quantity of food is decided.

## Reflection on the Process {#sec-reflection}

The most useful steps of the project were the ones that went to the kitchens and asked for records. For several weeks the team reasoned about the system from the student side and from the published rules, and the first honest look inside a kitchen changed more than any analysis had. With hindsight the team would go to the kitchens first, would ask for the records sooner because they existed all along, and would state how sure it was from the first document. That last habit allowed findings to be changed cleanly when evidence arrived. The project also taught that measurement and routing are different things. The system measures its problem daily and still cannot see it, because the measurement is not delivered.

# Limitations and Ethical Considerations {#ch-limits}

## Limitations of the Evidence {#sec-limits-evidence}

The evidence has five main limits. First, six students were interviewed, one transcript had a labelling gap, and six people are not a population. Second, each kitchen was visited once and the operators describe their own practice. Third, two sets of records cover two halls in two different months, and Bakul has no records at all. Fourth, the scenario model uses only Kadamba's scans, and its stress tests apply assumed shocks. Fifth, the stakeholder responses are simulated from stated positions and no feedback has been collected, and every co-design test is planned and has not been run. Each limit is placed beside the claim it affects.

## Open Questions {#sec-open}

A number of questions remain open and are collected in Appendix D. The most consequential is what a plate means in each operator's monthly payment. Others concern who set the four-day close and the limit of five, whether a Skip Meal declaration reaches the Kadamba kitchen, who heads the dining services body, what Kadamba's waste figure is a percentage of, whether a late batch can be cooked at 9:00 without slowing service, how many students would take a Sunday skip (the team assumed a quarter to a half), and which registration rule applied at Yuktāhār in June.

## Ethical Considerations {#sec-ethics-final}

The study involved interviews with students, staff and operators, photographs of kitchen records and an export of administrative data. Individuals are referred to by role only. Kitchen records are used in aggregate and the team set itself the rule that no recorded figure may be used against the person who recorded it. The recommended intervention was designed so as not to remove any arrangement that feeds or protects someone: resale, reuse, the meals of the outside diners and staff, and late service were kept. The pilot has stop conditions that include the withdrawal of consent and the use of any figure against a person. Finally, the team's plan includes its own withdrawal, so that the system does not come to depend on a student project.

# Conclusion and Future Work {#ch-conclusion}

## Conclusion {#sec-conclusion}

The project began from the claim that students skip breakfast because early classes collide with late nights, and it ended with a different account. Breakfast registration at IIIT Hyderabad is a default that no one chose to keep, the charge falls on the registration whether or not the meal is eaten, and each kitchen guesses how many will come while the records that could correct the guess are never delivered to those who set the rules. About 63 per cent of Kadamba's breakfast registrations in April 2026 went unused, and about 72 per cent at Yuktāhār in September, while lunch under the same rules worked. The paradox describes a stable system. Every actor behaves reasonably, and each consequence is absorbed before it reaches the people who could change the rules.

The recommended response has three parts that run together: build the missing routes through a weekly ledger and joint readings, change the default with a Sunday skip on a fixed date, and tune the kitchen alongside, labelled as the quick fix. A pilot on Kadamba's vegetarian Sunday line is designed, with success and stop criteria, and the model projects a large change in the kitchen and a small one for students until the rule moves. All of this remains projected, simulated or planned, and none of it has yet been tested on the campus.

## Future Work {#sec-future}

Several steps would move the work forward. The first is to obtain what the records would show: breakfast scans for months other than April, lunch and dinner exports, scan records for Yuktāhār, Bakul and Palash, and an item-level record for Bakul. The second is to settle the payment basis for each operator, since it gates the held levers. The third is to run the co-design tests with a supervisor and a ledger keeper, which are planned and have not been done. The fourth is the pilot itself, with its stop conditions. A fifth is to test the Sunday skip against student uptake, which the model assumes and has not observed. A last piece of work is to extend the analysis to lunch and dinner, which were kept outside the boundary as a control.
