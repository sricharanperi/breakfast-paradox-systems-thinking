# Phase 2: Modelling the System {#ch-phase2}

Phase 2 turned the findings of Phase 1 into a model of the system, tested which of the suspected feedback mechanisms hold, traced the visible events to their causes, and reframed the problem. It was first written on 12 to 14 September and rewritten on the revised evidence on 27 September. Its three activities produced five deliverables.

## Activity 6: Developing the System Map and Causal Loop Analysis {#sec-a6}

#### Purpose

The purpose of this activity was to build one representation of the interconnected elements of the system, with its bottlenecks, tensions and feedback loops, and to find out which of the feedback mechanisms that the team suspected actually operate.

#### Method

Two methods were combined. The first was a rich picture and service blueprint (Shostack, 1984): actors, flows, resources and constraints were put on one map, and each part was asked what it receives, what it passes on and where it stops. The blueprint places the steps of one breakfast in lanes separated by a line of visibility. The second method was causal loop analysis, with a strict test: a loop was accepted only if its last link really causes its first and every link in between is evidenced.

#### Process

The map was built around the registration count and the three kitchens. It included things beyond the kitchen door that shape the system, such as suppliers, waste, the class timetable and the informal ways around the rules. The team then listed every feedback mechanism that it had suspected, eighteen in all, numbered L1 to L17 with one extra (L3b), and applied the closure test to each. Mechanisms were sorted into four classes: loops that close with every link evidenced, candidate loops with one assumed link, chains that do not close, and mechanisms that are contraindicated, unresolved, absent or static. [[fig:sysmap]] gives the system at a glance, and [[fig:blueprint]] the blueprint.

#### Deliverables

The System Map (Deliverable 1 of Phase 2) holds the actors, process, resources and constraints, the three kitchens, waste and its destinations, the service timeline, conflicts and dependencies, the formal and informal structure, and the bottlenecks and tensions. The Causal Loop and Feedback Analysis (Deliverable 2) holds the loop status dashboard, the loops and candidate loops, the chains that do not close, the absent mechanisms, the unintended consequences and the structural causes.

#### Findings from the system map

The three kitchens were described in full for the first time, as set out in [[tab:kitchens]]. Surplus leaves each kitchen by a different route. Yuktāhār reuses it. At Kadamba it feeds the outside diners and half the staff, and some is held and reheated into later service, which the team judges to lower its quality (candidate). Latecomers are served at the back door, and whatever remains goes to the bin. Each of these absorbs a consequence before anyone has to notice it. Resale absorbs a student's loss, and outside diners absorb surplus food. Absorbers are usually kind, which is why they hide problems.

The governance map separated two routes. The complaint route exists and works for the same-incident fixes: a student writes to the director or the site team, the operator's manager acts, and the fix is made that day. The route for changing a rule is absent. The operators' books and the dining office's scan records measure the gap daily, and nothing carries them to the owners of registration, billing and menu rules, who are spread across separate offices. The system has also changed its own rules once, quickly. During a gas shortage, registration was made optional for a while and the cancellation limit was raised from five to ten, billing followed who came, and the no-show problem eased (single-sourced, one senior account). Then the rules went back. The system knows how to do this and does not know why it should keep doing it. [[fig:bottleneck]] shows the bottleneck chain from the standing booking to a shortage and a surplus on the same morning.

![The bottleneck chain: from a standing booking to shortage and surplus on the same morning, and what keeps each link in place.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-bottlenecks.png){w=100 #bottleneck}

#### Findings from the loop analysis

[[fig:dashboard]] sorts the eighteen mechanisms. Three close with every link evidenced, and all three are balancing loops: L4, the route by which menu complaints reach the committee through the student council; L11, Yuktāhār's record-keeping, in which today's waste shapes the plan for the same menu day two weeks later; and L12, the counter top-up, which can only add food. Five are candidates with one assumed link, six are chains that do not close, and four are contraindicated, unresolved, absent or static. Every loop that really works sits inside a kitchen, and none touches the registration.

![Loop status: eighteen mechanisms tested against the closure rule.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-dashboard.png){w=96 #dashboard}

The most important chain, L13, is the core of the paradox, and it does not close. Registration is automatic, so the count stays flat. The kitchen cooks a fixed share of a flat count. That share is too large on exactly the days and the line where turnout is lowest. The surplus is held and reheated, which may lower quality, but nothing found shows poor quality sending students away, so the last link has no evidence. Resale (L1) is the clearest candidate loop and is a shifting-the-burden structure: relief for the student makes the problem tolerable, so nobody fixes the cause. The last link, that relief reduces the pressure to fix billing, is not evidenced. Two earlier names, R1 and B1 to B4, were retired. The gas shortage rule is not a loop either, since it is a calendar switch that someone flips.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

A map with every box is true and unreadable, so the team drew one clean picture around the registration count and the three kitchens and kept the detail in the text. It kept academic timing on the map although it has no channel to the mess (Decision 10). On the loops, calling more things loops would have made the analysis look richer, but a loop with one assumed link is a hypothesis, and a report full of them cannot tell a reader what to trust. The team kept the strict rule: something is a loop only when every link is evidenced, and open chains stay chains with a dashed closing link (Decision 11). The conclusion was that the system has feedback in many places and none at the one place that matters.

## Activity 7: Identifying Systemic Patterns and Root Causes {#sec-a7}

#### Purpose

This activity moves from symptoms to the structures and mechanisms that produce them, distinguishing events, patterns, structures and mental models, and identifying structural causes and tensions.

#### Method

Seven concrete events were each traced down the iceberg. The event was the thing seen. The pattern was how often it recurs and under what conditions, the structure was the rule, schedule or arrangement that produces the pattern, and the mental model was the belief that holds the structure in place. Archetypes (Senge, 1994) were tested against the chains, and the team listed unintended consequences, systemic tensions, power asymmetries and workarounds.

#### Process

The seven chains were: students register and do not show up; food runs out before the close while the bin fills; complaints get no answer until something forces one; a fix that worked and was switched off; a menu change that helped and cast a shadow, because a known menu lets students decide in advance not to come; a real part of the gap that is only ordinary variation in life; and a kitchen that cooks to a guess and absorbs the difference. [[fig:chains]] sets the seven side by side.

![The seven chains, each traced from event to pattern, structure, mental model and root cause.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07-seven-chains.png){w=100 #chains}

#### Deliverable

The Systemic Problem Analysis (Deliverable 3 of Phase 2) holds the seven chains, the structural causes, the loops, the archetypes, the unintended consequences, eleven systemic tensions, the power asymmetries, the workarounds and a synthesis.

#### Findings

Three structures recur in every chain ([[fig:rootcauses]]). The first is that registration is an automatic standing default with a narrow and early exit, so it cannot follow a decision made in the morning. The second is that billing fires when the registration is made, so an unused registration costs the system nothing. The third is that the gap is measured twice and neither measurement reaches anyone who sets the rules. Two conditions keep these in place. Attention goes to loud problems: a run-out has a complaint route that reaches a managing director, while a surplus is silent. And an absorption layer takes each consequence before it becomes a signal.

![Three structures, two conditions and the mechanism beneath them.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07-root-causes.png){w=96 #rootcauses}

The conversion of registrations into a first batch comes in three forms, and the operators hold three attitudes to it. Kadamba cooks to the registration because it is a responsibility and the firm is paid for it. Yuktāhār treats waste as a cost it wants to cut, and made its weighing blame-free before it worked. Bakul treats a shortage as the failure to avoid and accepts waste as its price (single-sourced for all three). The gap is about equally large at Kadamba and at Yuktāhār, so the cause lies in the registration, and the kitchens' culture decides only how much of the gap turns into surplus (candidate).

Three archetypes fit the evidence ([[fig:archetypes]]). Shifting the burden has strong support: resale and kitchen skill each ease a symptom, so the cause is not touched. Fixes that fail is a candidate and rests on second-hand reports: a tightening of the cancellation rules in December 2024, reportedly to reduce office workload, may have strengthened the problem it aimed at. Accidental adversaries applies at Yuktāhār, where the weighing of waste was first read as being caught and worked only when management made it blame-free. One tension remained unresolved. Kadamba's operator reports that about 5 to 10 per cent of food is wasted each day, but a first batch of 70 per cent against a turnout of 37 per cent should leave far more. Either the outside diners, reheating and small top-ups absorb most of it, or the figure is on a different base. The team does not know which, and left it open.

![Two archetypes at work: shifting the burden and fixes that fail.|Source: authors' analysis; the second archetype is a candidate.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07-archetypes.png){w=96 #archetypes}

Two earlier claims were corrected in this activity. Cuisine had been offered as the explanation for low uptake at the north Indian halls, based on a single portal snapshot that showed registrations as a share of capacity, which does not measure attendance. Inside Kadamba the vegetarian and non-vegetarian lines differ in turnout, so the item and the line matter more than the cuisine. And it was not true that every operator distrusts the registration: only Yuktāhār does.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

A single root cause would give a clean headline and a clean fix, but the evidence did not support one. The team described structures and stated which of them the others depend on, and placed the cause in the registration structure, which neither students nor operators control (Decision 12). The gap persists because it is comfortable for each person who touches it, and the next step was to say so as a new question.

## Activity 8: Reframing the Problem {#sec-a8}

#### Purpose

The aim of the activity was to convert the operational question into a systemic design opportunity by challenging initial assumptions, comparing stakeholder perspectives, finding contradictions and testing alternative framings against the evidence.

#### Method

Each assumption behind the first question was tested against the records and the operators' accounts and marked with a status such as confirmed, revised, overturned, conflicting or open ([[fig:assumptions]]). Stakeholder perspectives were compared one against another. Four alternative framings were scored against four tests: whether the framing explains why breakfast fails when lunch works, why the problem persists unseen, whether it reaches the registration, and whether it fits the evidence ([[fig:framings]]).

![Assumptions tested against the evidence.|Source: authors' analysis of records and interviews.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s08r-assumptions.png){w=92 #assumptions}

![Alternative framings against four tests.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s08r-framings.png){w=92 #framings}

#### Process

The first question had been an operations question: why do students not eat what they register for? It was turned into a statement about the system. The system holds the gap between registration and eating in place because every actor's behaviour inside it is reasonable and every consequence is absorbed before it reaches anyone who sets the rules. Four other framings were tried and found insufficient. Student behaviour does not explain why most did not choose to register. Operator waste does not touch the registration, and operators already adjust. A clash with the 8:30 class explains part of the pattern, since late sleep is the reason students give most often, but the class start cannot be moved by this project. A forecasting gap would not reach the registration, and the kitchens' trouble is a batch that cannot be shrunk, whatever the prediction.

#### Deliverables

The Reframed Systemic Problem Statement (Deliverable 4 of Phase 2) holds the challenged assumptions, the stakeholder perspectives, the contradictions, the iceberg reading, the explanation of why the system holds steady, the alternative framings and their validation. The Design Opportunity Statement (Deliverable 5) holds the opportunity, its validation, four intervention points, the conditions that any intervention must respect, how the four interact, the questions that gate them and what Phase 3 inherits.

#### Findings

Five assumptions behind the first question did not survive. Non-attendance is not always a problem, since some skipping is ordinary. Students are not careless, and most are not choosing a registration at all. The fix is not unknown, because the institution has run attendance-based billing twice already. The gap is measured in three ways and never reconciled. And it is not one problem. The reframed statement reads that the breakfast paradox describes a stable system, held in equilibrium by three structures and two conditions.

The opportunity follows from this. The system needs a way to notice its own cost, and since the measurement exists, the work is to build the missing routes. The team marked four intervention points along the chain from registration to bin ([[fig:fourpoints]]). On process, the recorded turnout by hall, weekday and line should be fed into the decision that sets the first batch. On the rule, a standing, breakfast-only version of attendance-based billing, which the institution has already run twice, could be brought back. On decision rights, one owner should be named for the whole booked, cooked and eaten outcome. On information flow, a trigger that fires on measured cost as well as on complaint volume should be added.

![Four intervention points on the chain from registration to bin.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s08d-four-points.png){w=96 #fourpoints}

The Design Opportunity Statement also lists what any intervention must respect. An absorber must not be removed to create a signal, and the plan must account for the outside diners who eat the surplus. Action must come before the first batch is cooked, and no safety step may be traded for speed. Waste must be cut without raising the chance of a shortage, and turnout must not be used as the measure of success. Any new measurement must be blame-free and built on the paper books that kitchens already keep. The class start must not be treated as the lever, and the fix must not be aimed at students or at operators.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

One question stopped the team. What does a plate mean in each operator's monthly payment, a registered plate or a served plate? Kadamba says it is paid for what is registered, and Bakul's manager says plates served. If registered plates are paid, an operator earns on every no-show, and if served plates are paid, the operator pays for every uneaten portion. The answer decides who has a reason to close the gap, and nobody on the team has seen a contract. The question was marked open and every idea that depended on it was held back (Decision 15). The team reframed the problem as a stable equilibrium (Decision 13) and set the rule that no absorber is removed in order to create a signal, with the person or group who eats each surplus named (Decision 14). The second trade-off was between the deep fix, the registration, which belongs to rule owners the team cannot instruct, and the quick fix in the kitchen, which is within reach but cannot move the student's cost by itself. The team decided to plan both and to keep them labelled as different things. With this activity the project moved from describing to designing.
