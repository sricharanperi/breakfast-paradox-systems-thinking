# Phase 3: Identifying Leverage Points and Design Principles {#ch-phase3}

Phase 3 consists of Activity 9. It asked where and how the system could be changed, and what any change would have to respect. It was written on 27 and 28 September 2026, after the second batch of field evidence had been absorbed, and it takes its inputs from the Design Opportunity Statement of Phase 2.

## Activity 9: Defining Design Principles and Intervention Opportunities {#sec-a9}

#### Purpose

The purpose of this activity was to identify leverage points, to explore changes to rules, roles, incentives, information flows and processes, to develop design principles and to identify the potential intervention points.

#### Method

Leverage points were ranked on Meadows' (1999) scale of twelve places to intervene, counted from the deepest, so that place 1 is the power to transcend the paradigm, place 3 the goals of the system, place 5 its rules, place 6 its information flows and place 12 its parameters. Each lever was ranked by depth and then judged by what a student team could actually do with it: build it, propose it to a forum that exists, or only write it down. The archetype diagnosis from Phase 2 was used to order the levers, since a quick fix can strengthen the structure that it relieves. The three kitchens were compared with one another, since they share rules but plan differently. Along the breakfast, from registration to bin, the points where a lever can act were marked as intervention points.

#### Process

The team named twelve leverage points, LP1 to LP12 ([[tab:lp]]), and laid them against the scale ([[fig:depth]]). Each was checked against a rule that came out of Phase 2: a lever that removes something which protects a person is ruled out. Resale, the reuse of leftovers, the meals for outside diners and staff, the walk-in buffer and late service were therefore kept, and the levers were aimed at three things that protect nobody: the over-cooked first batch, repeated reheating and the bin. Fourteen intervention points, IP1 to IP14, were marked from the default registration itself, through the day-before plan, the 5:30 first batch, the tasting, the first half hour and the counter check at 9:00, to the crowd at the close, the leftovers, the daily record, the bin, the records that go nowhere, and the menu cycle ([[fig:ipchain]]).

#### Deliverables

The Leverage-Point and System Intervention Map (Deliverable 1 of Phases 3 and 4) shows the twelve levers on the loops of Phase 2 and on the breakfast chain, coloured by depth. The Design Principles (Deliverable 2) are seven tests that every concept must pass.

#### Findings

The deepest levers are beliefs and goals, and a student team cannot instruct a committee. The levers that the team can move are routes, kitchen practice and a proposal. Two of the twelve, the named owner and the cost of a registration, were held because both wait on facts that the team does not have. One point on the breakfast chain stands out because several levers act on it together, and that point is Sunday. Kadamba's Sunday turnout is 27.3 per cent, so a vegetarian first batch of 70 per cent cooks about 3.1 times what Sunday diners eat, against about 1.9 times on a Tuesday (candidate; the team's arithmetic). About 46 per cent of Sunday scans arrive after 9:15. Three levers act hardest there together: the exit from the default, the calibrated batch and the late batch ([[fig:sunday]]). Sunday on Kadamba's vegetarian line therefore became the first test slice.

![Sunday breakfast: the point where three levers meet.|Source: Kadamba scan export, April 2026 (confirmed); levers from authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-3-4-rebuild/Assets/a9-sunday.png){w=92 #sunday}

The comparison put Kadamba beside Yuktāhār and Bakul. The three halls share registration and billing rules, yet Yuktāhār learns from its books while Kadamba cooks a fixed ratio and Bakul works from a manager's judgement. A learning practice is therefore possible under today's rules. The halls differ in five other ways, among them scale, lines, firm, month and measurement, so the comparison cannot prove why. The question of why nothing changes by itself received a direct answer. The present arrangement pays everyone something: a student has no decision to make and a guaranteed seat, the caterers have the revenue they are used to or protection from shortage, and the institution has a low workload and a predictable budget. Each lever was checked against this to see whether it keeps what each person values and changes only what drives the over-registration.

The seven design principles are shown in [[fig:principles]]. They say, in short: route before you measure; make measurement blame-free and judge over months; aim changes at the default, the goal and the belief; act on the first batch the day before and in the morning only through the top-up; feed the absorbers by plan and target only the over-cooked batch, the repeated reheating and the bin; measure cooked against eaten, run-outs and quality, with turnout alone never taken as success; and use plural measures and never a single target.

![The seven design principles, with what each rules out.|Source: authors' analysis.](deliverables/phase-3-4-rebuild/Assets/a9-principles.png){w=88 #principles}

#### Decisions and trade-offs

The choice was between the deepest lever and the one a student team can move. The team ranked by depth first, then judged feasibility honestly, and said which levers were held and why (Decision 16). It also made blame-free measurement a rule of the design: no recorded figure may be used against the person who recorded it (Decision 17). The team had expected to find one clever lever. What it found was a pairing, a deep fix that needs others and a quick fix that can start now.

# Phase 4: Designing, Testing and Evaluating Interventions {#ch-phase4}

Phase 4 consists of Activity 10. It was written together with Phase 3 on 27 and 28 September. A pilot was not possible within the course, so every test in this chapter is a test on paper, using a model built on recorded data. The results of the model are labelled projected, the expected reactions of people are labelled simulated, and every co-design test is labelled planned, since none has been carried out.

## Activity 10: Designing, Testing and Evaluating Interventions {#sec-a10}

#### Purpose

The activity aimed to generate alternative interventions, to develop scenarios and prototypes, to simulate stakeholder responses, to test the interventions against the objectives of the system, to identify unintended consequences, and to refine the intervention.

#### Method

Eight concepts were drawn from the levers. A scenario model replayed proposed kitchen rules over twenty-three real April days of Kadamba's scans, and stress tests applied assumed shocks. The expected responses of twelve stakeholder groups were reasoned from their stated positions. Concepts were assessed in two steps. Four pass-or-fail gates came first, and concepts that passed were then scored from 0 to 4 on nine criteria worth 400 points in all.

#### Process

**Alternatives.** [[tab:concepts]] lists the eight concepts and [[fig:concepts]] shows which levers each pulls. Concept G, the Ratio Cut, was included as a foil: a blunt reduction of the first batch that the team expected to fail, kept in every test so that a good idea could be compared with a weak one.

**Prototypes.** The team prepared a plan card for the evening before, a daily sheet, the weekly ledger and the text of a Sunday skip proposal. The plan card was worked through for one day. On Sunday 19 April 2026, Kadamba's vegetarian line had 817 registrations and 179 diners. The baseline of 70 per cent would have cooked 572 portions. The plan card for the Saturday evening expects 164 eaters, taken from the mean of the same weekday in earlier weeks, and sets a first batch of 134 portions. At 9:00, 62 diners had scanned against a usual share of 39.4 per cent by that time, which projects a day of about 157 eaters; with a margin of 10 per cent the late batch is 39 portions. In all 173 portions are cooked for 179 diners, which leaves six for the ordinary counter top-up (projected). Every plan card also names a separate allowance for the outside diners and the staff meal. The co-design tests, in which a supervisor and a ledger keeper would use the prototypes, are planned and have not been done.

**Scenario model.** The model runs the proposed rules over the twenty-three recorded days. The results are given in [[tab:model]] and [[fig:scenarios]]. A calibrated first batch on its own cuts surplus from 362 portions a day to 60, but runs 16.8 portions short at the crest. Adding a 9:00 late batch at 1.5 times cover with a 10 per cent buffer gives a surplus of 91, a shortage of 2.6 and 3.4 diners waiting. A projection made at 8:00 instead of 9:00 is wrong by about 35 per cent, so the late batch waits until 9:00. The blunt cut to 50 per cent leaves about 150 portions of surplus and starts non-vegetarian run-outs. It does not fix the waste it aims at and moves the cost to the crowd at the close.

The refined design was then stressed with assumed shocks: an exam or festival week with a quarter fewer eaters and no warning, an exam week announced in advance, a favourite item that draws a quarter more eaters, ten per cent fewer or more eaters than forecast, a crest ten points heavier after 9:15, a gas shortage that makes every batch take thirty minutes longer, and a failed late batch. The surplus stayed between about 33 and 123 portions in every case. The worst case was the failed 9:00 batch, in which the shortage at the crest rose to about 44 portions (projected). The shocks are assumed and explore how the rule responds; they do not state how often each would occur.

**Stakeholder responses.** The expected reactions of twelve groups were written down and each was used to alter the design (simulated). Near-daily eaters would hardly notice a change. Sunday and frequent skippers would take a simple, free skip. A caterer paid on registered plates would oppose a skip, and one paid on served plates would be neutral. Kadamba's supervisors fear a run-out and a reprimand and would accept a late batch that comes with a reserve and a named staff meal. Yuktāhār would welcome a paper ledger. The facilities department and the dining office are cautious about work that they do not control.

**Evaluation framework.** The four gates ask whether all absorbers survive, whether every safety step is kept, whether the concept is aimed at structure without waiting on an unknown that could reverse it, and whether every kitchen lever is paired with a route, default, goal or belief lever that lands on a fixed date. Concepts that pass are scored on booked against eaten, cooked against eaten, service at the crest, fundamental capability, fairness, food quality, desirability, feasibility and reversibility. The weights are the team's judgement and are stated as such. [[fig:scores]] gives the result.

![Concepts scored against the system objectives, with the gate each failing concept does not pass.|Source: authors' analysis; scores are model results (projected).](deliverables/phase-3-4-rebuild/Assets/a10-gates-scores.png){w=96 #scores}

The Breakfast Ledger leads among single concepts, because it builds the route that every other lever needs, though on its own it moves no food and no money. The Kitchen Learning Kit alone has the largest physical effect and the worst performance at the crest. Two bundles score the same, since a weighted score cannot see order. One of them lets the Sunday skip wait until the kitchen has proved itself, and that fails the fourth gate. It is a small case of shifting the burden, because if the kitchen fix comes first and works, nobody feels pressure to change the registration. The gates therefore overrule the score.

**Unintended consequences.** Every second-order effect that the team found is a loop pushing back. A smaller batch could take food from the outside diners, so every plan card names an allowance and a missed meal stops the pilot. One loud shortage could bring the hedge back, so results are judged over months ([[fig:hedge]]). A ledger can be read as an audit, which breeds round numbers and hidden leftovers, so it is blame-free and never used for a deduction. A grace period after 9:30 could soften the close, so it is withdrawn after four weeks if the late share stays above 9.2 per cent.

![The hedge ratchet: one loud shortage pushes the first batch back to its old size.|Source: authors' analysis; a candidate loop.](deliverables/phase-3-4-rebuild/Assets/a10-hedge-loop.png){w=62 #hedge}

**Refinement.** The tests changed the design in twelve ways. The main change was that the kitchen bundle must never run alone, and the Sunday exit must not wait for it.

#### The recommended intervention

The recommendation consists of three parts that run together. The Ledger and the Shared Reading build the route. The Sunday skip changes the default on a fixed date, whatever the kitchen pilot shows. The kitchen bundle, with its plan card, allowance, 9:00 late batch and daily sheet, runs beside them and is always labelled as the quick fix. [[fig:sequence]] gives the sequence over twenty weeks.

![The recommended twenty-week sequence, with decision points on fixed dates and the handover.|Source: authors' analysis (planned).](deliverables/phase-3-4-rebuild/Assets/a10-sequence.png){w=100 #sequence}

In weeks 0 to 4 the team builds the Ledger, runs co-design tests and holds a joint reading with the dining office and the kitchens. At week 6 the Sunday skip goes to the rule owner with the plate-basis question attached. In weeks 5 to 8 Kadamba's Sunday vegetarian line runs the plan card and the late batch. If the skip is adopted it starts at about week 10. At week 14 the team decides whether an every-day skip should follow, only if Sunday uptake is at least a quarter, no Sunday runs more than ten portions short, and the calibrated batch holds on both lines. Later steps, an opt-in on the quietest days and a change in billing, wait for facts that the team does not have. Handover begins at week 16 and the team steps back at week 20.

#### The pilot, and who has to act

The pilot is Kadamba's vegetarian line, Sunday breakfast, four Sundays, judged at week 16. The supervisor runs it and the team fills the ledger. It succeeds if Sunday vegetarian surplus falls from about 370 to under 100, no Sunday runs more than ten portions short, run-outs are no more frequent than on comparison days, every allowance meal is served and portion size and taste hold. It stops if shortage runs past one top-up two Sundays running, if a staff meal is missed, if portions fall below the standard, if any figure is used against a person, or if consent is withdrawn. Of thirteen actions, the team can complete two itself (the Ledger with the plan cards, and the co-design tests) and can convene or support four that others decide. Six it can carry forward only as proposals with evidence: a quantity line at the tasting, a cost trigger, a stated goal, the Sunday exit, the billing basis and an owner of the outcome. The thirteenth is the handover, which the team must complete itself, because the team should not become the thing the system depends on. By week 20, two ledger pages and one forum should have run without it.

#### Expected effect

The expected effects are projected and are shown in [[fig:botproj]]. First-batch surplus at Kadamba falls from a range of about 254 to 471 portions a day to a range of about 69 to 120 over roughly four months. The weekly charge for uneaten breakfasts falls from about Rs 2.47 lakh to between Rs 2.27 and Rs 2.37 lakh with the Sunday skip, and to between Rs 1.23 and Rs 1.85 lakh if the every-day skip follows. In April alone, Rs 10.6 lakh was charged for uneaten Kadamba breakfasts. The change in the kitchen is large and the change for students is small until the rule moves, which is what the diagnosis predicts. Bakul is not part of the pilot. It has no records and is a different system, with food cooked far away, a floor set by judgement and a payment basis reported as plates served. Nothing is sized for Bakul, and the recommendation reaches it only once it has its own scans, a measured top-up time, an item-level record and a confirmed payment basis.

#### Deliverables

The deliverables of Activity 10 are the Intervention Concepts and Prototypes (Deliverable 3 of Phases 3 and 4), the Scenario Model and Impact Evaluation (Deliverable 4), and the Final System Design Recommendation (Deliverable 5), which holds the recommended intervention, the architecture of the path, the theory of change, the interventions across the iceberg, the pilot design and monitoring, the actions required of each party, and the before and after.

#### Decisions and trade-offs

Four decisions were taken. The team kept a concept it expected to fail in every test, because testing only good ideas risks every test passing (Decision 18). It applied four gates before any score and let the gates overrule the score (Decision 19). It set the Sunday skip on a fixed date independent of the kitchen pilot (Decision 20), because a kitchen that improves can make the registration look unimportant, and the exit would then never happen. It chose the Kadamba vegetarian line on Sunday for the pilot, where three levers meet (Decision 21). It sized nothing for Bakul until Bakul has records (Decision 22), and planned its own withdrawal: the Ledger passes to the dining office by role, and the team steps back at week 20 (Decision 23). The team began by wanting to explain why students skip breakfast and ended with a design that asks little of students and a good deal of the institution.
