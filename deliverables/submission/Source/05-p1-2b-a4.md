### Decisions and Interactions {#sec-a4-decisions}

#### The student side

The CDS handbook requires registration and cancellation at least four days ahead, and Kadamba's manager calls it weekly compulsory, while three of six students say they register for a month. Both hold: the portal locks weekly and students set a standing booking once. A team member's own experience as a Kadamba student adds that registration is automatic and mandatory, holidays included, with five cancellations per meal type per month as the only exit.

#### The operators' three logics

Kadamba cooks a fixed share of registrations and tops up reactively; Yuktāhār learns from its own books and re-projects from the first half hour; Bakul's manager varies the share by item and adjusts it weekly by judgement, never below a floor kept against shortage. An earlier reading from a noisy recording that gave only the ratio had Bakul resembling Kadamba ([[tab:a4-regimes]]).

Each regime's decisions follow, labelled by confidence.

**Kadamba.** Prism's management sets the first batch the day before at about 70 per cent of registrations, veg and non-veg separately, by a per-person gram standard per dish (idli 4 a head from about 120 g of batter, sambar 100 g); biryani lunch is cooked at 100 per cent (two pieces of 150 to 200 g with unlimited rice, so when some diners take three, later ones run short). About 30 extra portions feed 30 to 50 outsiders such as night-shift security, and about half the staff eat the same food. Dry goods are ordered a week ahead, perishables the day before and some vegetables two to three days ahead. Items above gram standard are bonda, puri, vada and idli. After 9:30 latecomers enter by the back door and the mess cannot say no (on one biryani day the door reopened for 5 to 10 minutes, from noisy audio; scans after 9:30 on every April day support it at breakfast).

**Yuktāhār.** The storekeeper, coordinator and kitchen staff meet the day before to set breakfast and lunch quantities (a second meeting sets snacks and dinner) from portal registrations (regular, Jain, cash), the Wastage Book page for the matching past week (the menu repeats on a two-week cycle), the month's pattern, the weekday register and events only if raised; the site head believes breakfast runs at about 50 per cent. The meeting also raises discrepancies, impurities and leave requests (no formal policy; colleagues absorb the load). Raw material comes from many local shops and godowns, so stones are expected: a grams-of-impurity norm per item applies, and exceeding it means a complaint or new supplier. Cooking runs 5:30 to 7:30, mostly in one batch and in two for items that dry out such as poha; the site head opposes general batching because "student fluctuation is very high". The first half hour is a sample for the rest (breakfast use candidate), and a second roti counter opens at peak.

**Bakul.** Every statement is the manager's own and every number his estimate. He reviews the first batch weekly at about 70 to 80 per cent of registrations, by item, from attendance history by day (if Mondays ran near 70 per cent he cooks about 80 per cent; that 70 sits above his own breakfast estimate of at most about 60 per cent, so may not describe breakfast), and keeps a floor ("there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs") of about 70 per cent even on low weekends. Whether history is written down is open. Hafizpet starts labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha around 3 AM to arrive about 7, so food is held for hours; the 8 km drive takes about 20 minutes early and 40 later, in insulated boxes at about 60 to 72 °C. An earlier noisy reading put a top-up within 30 minutes, possibly the check interval; the drive alone is 20 to 40 minutes, so the figure is flagged and not relied on (lead time open).

Two candidate readings follow. His first batch and weekend floor both exceed the highest turnout he expects, so the batch exceeds turnout by design; if Bakul resembles the recorded halls at 28 to 37 per cent the gap is larger and cannot be sized without records.

The menu is chosen by the student committee from an operator-supplied list (formal, single-sourced). Operators see per-item waste (at Kadamba uttapam, set dosa and upma waste while puri, bonda, vada and idli move) and shift quantities, as Kadamba did in cutting ragi idli and increasing plain idli, but cannot change the menu. The Bakul manager's objection concerns pairings ([[sec:a3-authority]]).

### Formal and Informal Rules {#sec-a4-rules}

#### Rules on the student side

The CDS portal enforces registration four days ahead (automatic if a student does nothing; the handbook gives demand estimation as the reason), automatic vegetarian allocation for non-registrants, the cancellation cap of 5 per meal type per month (used by most; beyond it the charge stands) and Skip Meal without refund. Skip Meal was meant to reduce kitchen preparation, not billing, and no respondent who addressed it used it; its effect on Kadamba's count is unconfirmed. One respondent relies on allocation; vegetarian registrations at Kadamba were used at 32.4 per cent in April against 48.5 per cent for non-vegetarian, and whether default allocation explains part is a candidate.

Student norms are unwritten: registering for the whole month, the path of least resistance that automatic registration makes the default; checking the menu first (one respondent); going early to avoid queues (a minority). At Yuktāhār the culture is family-like, and one project manager trained all staff at his farmhouse on good quality (single-sourced).

Workarounds fill gaps in the formal system. Mess Cell resale compensates for Skip Meal's lack of refund, and Kadamba's manager describes students who register while away while a friend eats in their place. Snacks or a later meal compensate for skipping, early registration for a popular mess filling, and the back door after close. Operator workarounds are listed under the exceptions below.

### Exceptions, Workarounds and Dependencies {#sec-a4-exceptions}

![Exception and workaround paths, student side and operator side.|Source: student interviews, operator accounts and Kadamba scan records.](phase-1-rebuild/Assets/d9-exception-paths.png){w=86 #a4-exceptions}

#### Operator exceptions

[[fig:a4-exceptions]] sets out what goes wrong and what people do about it. On the operator side:

- Scanner failure: Kadamba staff write down ID and name by hand; the April export shows no bulk entry (never more than two scans in one second), so hand-logged diners may be missing (candidate undercount).
- Gas shortage: Kadamba cut quantities a little and bought some items outside (first operator account; cause single-sourced).
- Equipment and supply: Kadamba's bread grill is broken and unreplaced; Bakul's paneer has moved between three brands after complaints.
- Events, exams, holidays: nothing formal tells the kitchen; they reach Yuktāhār's meeting only if mentioned, and lower turnout in ways the site head calls unavoidable.
- Off-site transport and latecomers: every Bakul top-up is a phone call, a cook and a 20 to 40 minute drive against a fixed close. Kadamba staff do not refuse latecomers, and last-minute registrations are called a pain (the wording covers breakfast and dinner closes).
- Records that disagree: for breakfast on 3 September at Yuktāhār the Wastage Book records 77 diners (28 per cent) and the attendance register, on a partly obscured row, 110 (40 per cent). Which feeds planning is unknown; the two-book rule covers materials, not diner counts, and the books have different keepers (candidate).

The operator workarounds (leftover reuse, outsiders and staff eating the same food, holding and reheating, late-door service, raw stock held for top-ups) are absorption mechanisms: each soaks up part of the gap before it becomes visible and is locally beneficial. The outside diners and the half of Kadamba's staff who eat at the mess are real people fed by the surplus, so any change to the first batch has to plan for them.

#### Dependencies

Eating breakfast at the mess depends on waking with enough time and alertness (the most cited constraint), on the desired item being available (run-outs confirmed by at least three of six), on no clash with class-attendance pressure (one respondent, and itself dependent on academic scheduling, which the power analysis found disconnected from mess governance), and on the mess being open, since the 9:30 cut-off is the most requested change.

Item availability depends on a chain the trace can now follow. The four-day lock applies to registration, not to cooking or buying; Yuktāhār buys groceries fortnightly, vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily.

The operators also depend on supplier cadence and quality; gas, electricity and water (logged daily at Yuktāhār); equipment (Kadamba's roti line, multi-item cooker, tilting curry machine); one person running all of Kadamba's inventory, a single point of failure; the two-week menu rotation behind the matching-week page; the daily meeting as Yuktāhār's only channel for event information; and Hafizpet transport. Yuktāhār chooses inputs for quality (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months) with 16 kitchen staff and two project managers.

Where uneaten food finally goes is only partly settled. The facilities head described production and plate waste going via a garbage collector to an outside compost facility; Kadamba's manager said there is no composting and the bin goes to the garbage contractor (single-sourced), which replaced the first assumption that composting absorbs the surplus.

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a4-decisions-log}

Decision: The student half of the trace was written first and the kitchen half marked as unobserved until operator evidence existed, in preference to describing the kitchens from assumption.

Decision: The transcript set with a labelling gap was counted as four to five people and not silently relabelled, because neither explanation could be checked. A teammate's independent synthesis of the interviews led to two adopted corrections: the registration cycle is monthly and not weekly, and an apparent two-peak crowd is one building crowd cresting near the close.

Decision: Each caterer was traced end to end as its own system, since merging the three would have hidden three forecasting logics. The Bakul account was traced as the manager's own statement with no number generalised to other halls; where it conflicts with Kadamba's, both stay in place.

Decision: When field evidence contradicted a finding already written, it was changed in every place and old and new versions were not blended. The trace was rewritten in three passes (27 September on the operators' interviews and scan records, 27 September on the team's first-hand account, 28 September on the Bakul account). Corrections include replacing a four-day procurement lock by a registration cut-off, a single passive vendor by three operators, and withdrawing composting as an absorber, an unattributed operator chain, and the claim that the best-documented correction acts only on a one-to-two-week delay.

Decision: Scan records replaced a live observation of a full breakfast, as they describe the crowd's shape and timing better than one morning of counting; disposal after close stays open for direct observation.
