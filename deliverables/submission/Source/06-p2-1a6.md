### Actors, Process, Resources and Constraints {#sec-a6-actors}

[[sec:a2]] describes the cast and [[sec:a3]] the authority structure. The map adds three features. The operator layer is drawn as three operators, not one box. A second demand stream exists: Kadamba cooks about 30 extra portions for 30 to 50 outsiders and about half its staff eat at the mess (single-sourced), planned demand and an uncounted sink for surplus (candidate); Yuktāhār's faculty and staff diners number zero to two a breakfast (confirmed).

#### The operator layer in cross-section

[[sec:a4]] describes each kitchen. Against the records ([[fig:a6-batch]]), a 70 per cent batch at Kadamba is about 1.9 times the 37.1 per cent who eat, and 2.6 times on a Sunday (candidate arithmetic: a stated rule applied to a month whose batches were not recorded). The operator's waste figure of 5 to 10 per cent has no stated base (single-sourced).

![First batch against turnout at each hall. Only Kadamba's turnout and Yuktāhār's register are records.|Source: Kadamba scan records, April 2026; Yuktāhār registers, September 2026; operator statements (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-batch-vs-turnout.png){w=88 #a6-batch}

The halls differ in learning, not data. Both on-site kitchens know on the day how many are eating, but only Yuktāhār records the outcome where it returns to the next decision about the same meal. Bakul corrects against surplus by one person's judgement, down to a floor.

#### Registration, procurement and service

[[sec:a1]] sets out the rules and [[sec:a5]] the counts. Four points belong to the map. First, the four-day lock applies to registration, not buying: Kadamba buys dry goods a week ahead and perishables one to three days ahead (single-sourced), and Yuktāhār's vegetables arrive two to three times a week and its milk daily (confirmed), so only dry goods are committed before the close (candidate). What binds waste is the cooked first batch: raw stock is recoverable, cooked food lasts three to four hours, and a batch can be topped up but never shrunk.

#### The Yuktāhār book system

Yuktāhār runs on paper books that feed each other. Every book was photographed (confirmed); the connections are the operator's account (single-sourced). The day's registrations go into the Grocery Book header; at the planning meeting the matching-week Wastage Book entry, the weekday register and the month's pattern become a per-dish list in kilograms, which is the issue in the Grocery Book, balanced by the stock register and driving purchasing.

![How Yuktāhār's books feed each other, and where the chain stops.|Source: photographed books (confirmed); connections from the operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-book-network.png){w=100 #a6-book-network}

Two rules hold the books together: the store book's issue must match the physical book's cooked quantity (4.5 kg of toor dal issued, balance 49.45 kg, agreed on the day checked), and every book is kept by one person and cross-checked by another, "not to find fault". The books reconcile materials, not diners: for 3 September the Wastage Book and the attendance register, kept by different people, record 77 and 110 breakfasts (confirmed).

### Waste, Environment and the Routes of Surplus {#sec-a6-waste}

Kadamba's waste goes into a dustbin emptied by a garbage contractor, with no composting (single-sourced); the facilities head had described an outside compost facility, but the operator's account is first-hand and more recent, and the contractor's onward route is unverified. Suspected contamination goes to a laboratory, and Kadamba holds an external five-star Eat Right Campus rating. Yuktāhār weighs waste per item and meal and sets the next matching day's issue from it (confirmed); Kadamba's figure is a quoted percentage with no record seen.

Surplus is absorbed before the rule-setters see it: reuse at Yuktāhār; outsiders, half the staff and reheating at Kadamba (candidate); the walk-in buffer; latecomers after the nominal close; finally the bin. Each does real good, so the problem persists without anyone ignoring it and no fix should work by removing one. Nobody has measured the cost of the 20,221 Kadamba breakfasts registered and never eaten in April, including the landfill cost of food sent to general garbage.

### The Service Timeline and Blueprint {#sec-a6-timeline}

[[sec:a5]] reports the Kadamba crowd curve; here the question is how each operator's picture of the crest compares with it. The Kadamba operator's rule of thumb has the same shape (about 100 early, 100 through the middle, 150 in the last ten minutes), but the records show 19.8 per cent of scans, about 79 a day, in the last ten minutes against about 43 per cent in his example (candidate).

The service blueprint ([[fig:a6-blueprint]]) separates what the student sees from what the kitchen does. Behind the line the kitchen reacts within a meal: at Kadamba the supervisor reads what is left of each item beside the scan count and tells the chef how much more to cook; Yuktāhār projects from its first half hour, checks every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close and opens a second counter in surges; Kadamba reopens its front door for five to ten minutes for latecomers (25 came after the close on one biryani day), and after 9:30 latecomers use the back door and cannot be refused (confirmed).

![Service blueprint: what the student sees above the line, what the kitchen does below it, and the one signal that crosses it.|Source: authors' analysis; Bakul differences from the manager's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-blueprint.png){w=100 #a6-blueprint}

The response runs one way: it adds food, counters and minutes and cannot take back a cooked batch. No signal crosses the line of visibility from one meal to the next. One artefact crosses at every meal, a wrapped display plate showing the full menu (confirmed); it carries the menu, never quantity.

### Conflicts and Dependencies {#sec-a6-conflicts}

Students depend on the operator for food; no operator depends on any one student. The institution depends on students registering accurately although nothing in billing requires it, and on operators for the only item-level record of what is eaten and wasted, for which it has no channel. Operators depend on the institution for the menu, rules and exam and event calendar, which reaches them only informally; Bakul also depends on the road from Hafizpet.

#### Food-safety checkpoints

Assurance is routine and runs before any laboratory step: the Kadamba sequence ([[sec:a4]]), a weekly quality-control inspection and a daily reading of dissolved solids in water (plate and tasting confirmed by observation, the rest single-sourced). Yuktāhār has a tasting team and logs fridge temperatures three times a day, all within 1.4 to 3.5 °C in September (confirmed).

### Formal and Informal Structure {#sec-a6-structure}

CDS sits under CFS. Low-intensity feedback goes straight to the CFS Head; high-intensity feedback escalates through the Student Council to the Committee. Below CDS sits a contract relation that governance diagrams usually omit: CDS tenders each hall to an outsourced vendor. [[fig:a6-governance]] separates three routes.

![CDS, CFS and the vendors: the complaint route that works for incidents, the escalation route that produced the menu rotation, and the rule-change route that does not exist.|Source: authors' analysis of interviews and posters.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-governance.png){w=100 #a6-governance}

Yuktāhār has no hierarchy and everyone does every job (confirmed); colleagues absorb the load raised at the daily meeting, and staff describe a family-like culture (single-sourced).

The escalation route's one result is the clearest working feedback in the project: a long-standing complaint about a single fixed menu went through the Student Council to the Committee and came out as a menu that rotates every two weeks (operators confirm). The rotation makes Yuktāhār's matching-day lookup possible, so a governance loop enables a kitchen loop. Operators see which items waste each time they appear (uttapam, set dosa and upma at Kadamba, while puri, bonda, vada and idli are eaten) and have no route to the menu owner.

Outside the formal structure the resale market is a student messaging group, the student publication has no confirmed power to act, and at least one unofficial tool manages registration outside the portal. Academic administration has no formal or informal relationship to any part of mess governance, a confirmed absence ([[sec:a2]], [[sec:a3]]).

### The Class Schedule and Who Else Needs Breakfast {#sec-a6-class}

The project had treated 8:30 classes as an obstacle, which is probably unfair: the timing may suit harder lectures or let each programme fit five days on shared halls.

Faculty and staff demand is small and counted at Yuktāhār and larger at Kadamba, where it helps absorb surplus; whether it is a written entitlement, and whether anyone counts or bills it, is unknown.

### The System Already Solved This Once {#sec-a6-precedent}

During a cooking-gas shortage the mess cut to one item at the same price and the cancellation limit rose from five to ten a month. During holidays and long weekends such as the campus festival, registration stopped being required: students could walk in and scan, and someone who neither registered nor came was not charged (single-sourced; the holiday part is disputed, since the team's own account is that students stay registered through holidays). If it holds, "nobody has worked this out" becomes "this was worked out and switched off". The usual reason, protecting the vendor's four-day lead time, is weakened, since kitchens already absorb unpredictability by topping up and Kadamba orders perishables one day ahead.

### Bottlenecks and What Actually Closes the Loop {#sec-a6-bottlenecks}

The bottleneck is the cooked first batch, a ratio of a standing booking, which can be topped up but never shrunk, against a crowd that at Kadamba crests in the last fifteen minutes ([[fig:a6-bottleneck]]). At Bakul the batch leaves Hafizpet before any turnout signal exists, and late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals; at Kadamba the safety sequence fixes the batch before 7:30, so the constraint costs quality as well as food. The 9:30 close is a precise cut-off against a crowd that peaks right before it and in practice runs to 9:35 or 9:45.

![The bottleneck chain from the booking to the same-morning shortage and surplus, with what keeps each link in place.|Source: authors' analysis.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s06-bottlenecks.png){w=100 #a6-bottleneck}

### Culture, Health and What Is Not a Problem {#sec-a6-culture}

Breakfast menus lean toward nutrient-focused items such as dal, sprouts and plain rotis, a plausible reason for turnout below lunch alongside the billing incentive; Yuktāhār confirms that breakfast runs far below lunch on the same days under the same rules, so both can hold. Bakul's manager offers that students may dislike the menu or may not wake up (his view): the second matches the late sleep students most often cite, the first is his hypothesis, since the six student interviews do not support food quality as a primary reason.

### The Closure Test and the Loop Dashboard {#sec-a6-loops}

A causal loop exists only if the last variable in a chain causes the first. A loop with an even number of negative links reinforces whatever starts it; one with an odd number pushes back toward a steady state; a chain that never closes is drawn with a dashed closing link. Each mechanism was asked three questions: is every link supported by evidence, does the last link really cause the first, and what is the delay around the cycle. Eighteen mechanisms were tested, L1 to L17 plus L3b, a reinforcing variant of L3.

### Loops That Close {#sec-a6-closed}

#### L11: Yuktāhār's waste-learning loop

Cooked quantity above what is eaten raises the leftover and run-out time written in the Wastage Book. A planning meeting held twice a day reads that record against the matching past day (menu weeks one and three are identical, as are two and four), the month's turnout pattern, events, and registrations counted as regular plus Jain plus cash meals, and sets the next day's issue. A larger leftover lowers the next issue, which lowers the surplus: one negative link, so the loop balances, and every link is evidenced by the photographed pages and the site head's account.

![L11: Yuktāhār's Wastage Book loop, its two delays, and the preconditions that keep the record honest.|Source: Yuktāhār registers (confirmed); operator account.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l11.png){w=100 #a6-l11}

The loop exists because weighing, begun in August, was made blame-free after staff first feared blame ("they are catching us"); by December staff reported lower waste (self-report, no before-and-after figures). Peer cross-checking by staff who rotate across every job and the two-book rule keep the record honest, and it all runs on paper, a commercial ERP system having been dropped as too costly for data already in the books (single-sourced). Three limits apply: the loop adjusts quantity and never registration; it rests on one operator's account from one visit; and Kadamba keeps per-dish gram standards (four idlis from about 120 g of batter, 100 g of sambar) but no learning record: "We don't have that idea… it was wasted many times." Operator stance may explain why the loop is present at one hall, absent at another and bounded at the third (candidate).

#### L12: counter monitoring and top-up

Within a meal, food at the counter falls, a periodic check registers the risk of running out, the counter asks for more, and food rises again: one negative link, balancing, described by all three operators ([[fig:a6-l12]]). Kadamba's supervisor watches each item against its gram standard and asks the chef for portions, combining registrations with turnout so far; top-ups take 10 to 15 minutes (single-sourced). Yuktāhār staff check every 30 minutes from about 45 minutes before close, open a second counter at surges and re-cook by the run-out rule (the 9:00 re-cook on 3 September is on record, confirmed).

![L12: top-up at three halls, and the delay that decides how well each works.|Source: operator accounts (single-sourced); Yuktāhār registers (confirmed).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l12.png){w=100 #a6-l12}

The delay decides the behaviour. On site, a top-up requested when the final surge shows lands at or after the 9:30 close, so the loop cannot track the crest and is pre-empted only by a buffer cooked in advance, which is L14's hedge. Off site no top-up can land late, so the only hedge is food already held: Bakul's manager keeps food for about 50 at 9:20 against the 10 to 15 he says arrive in the last ten minutes, a reserve that is, as a candidate reading, the structural consequence of the transport delay, an insurance premium paid in surplus.

#### L4: the menu rotation response

Every link of this governance loop traces to a direct account. A single fixed menu produced menu fatigue; the feedback reached the CDS Student Council, which escalated it to the Student Parliament, and the CDS Committee approved a biweekly rotation. Variety rose and, by the facilities head's account, fatigue complaints fell; operators' records confirm the two-week cycle.

![L4: the menu-rotation loop, closed end to end by a loud complaint with a route.|Source: facilities head's account and operators' records.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l4.png){w=86 #a6-l4}

### Candidate Loops {#sec-a6-candidates}

Each of these would close if its last link held, but that link is assumed, so each is drawn dashed and not counted as a loop.

**L1, speculative registration and the resale safety net.** A student registers without firm intent, producing an unused registration that feeds the resale market. Two respondents show the first links ("Kadamba veg breakfast costs around Rs 48 and I book it every day and never avail it"; "I have both bought and sold registrations"), and Kadamba's manager describes the transfer from the kitchen side (single-sourced); [[sec:a5]] draws the loop.

**L9, waste absorption.** Surplus passes through five routes before the bin: reuse at Yuktāhār (idli becomes upma or poha for lunch, batter returns to the fridge, milk is set as curd; confirmed); outsiders and staff at Kadamba (single-sourced; whether their 30 portions sit inside or on top of the 70 per cent batch is unconfirmed); held and reheated food (the team's judgement); latecomers at the back door (L15); and the contractor's dustbin. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown. A 70 per cent first batch of about 750 against about 398 eaten a day leaves roughly 350 portions before any top-up (candidate arithmetic).

**L17, kitchen competence shifts the burden.** Operators discounting registrations and topping up live (L11, L12) relieve the gap's visible consequences every morning: queues clear, shortages are re-cooked, surplus is reused, fed to outsiders and staff, held or binned. Because each morning is handled locally, no signal accumulates upstream, which plausibly reduces pressure for the fundamental fix, a booking that tracks the daily decision to eat ([[fig:a6-l17]]). The shift is partly benign: the surplus has beneficiaries, so cutting the first batch takes their meals unless it plans for them, and an operator that treats the count as an obligation it is paid to meet has no motive to raise a signal. Bakul's floor turns a risk of visible shortage into a silent surplus that it bears itself, acceptable if it is paid on plates served, as its manager reports (candidate).

![L17: kitchen competence relieves the symptom each morning, so the fundamental fix never gets pressure.|Source: authors' analysis; the dashed link is assumed.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l17.png){w=100 #a6-l17}

**L3b, Kadamba demand lock-in.** Kadamba's reputation plausibly drives scarcity, and scarcity drives slot-locking for the respondent who registers all month because Kadamba fills up. April is consistent with that, but automatic registration predicts the same pattern, and the Bakul operator's belief that Kadamba gets 90 to 95 per cent turnout is contradicted for breakfast by the records. The closing link, that locking reinforces the reputation, is the weakest; the loop is unconfirmed.

**L15, the soft close.** At Kadamba, when the front door closes at 9:30 people come in by the back door and the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced). The operator's wording covers dinner as well as breakfast, so breakfast back-door entry is a candidate, supported by the records: 9.2 per cent of April scans came after 9:30, the latest at 10:07 (confirmed). If students learn that the close is soft they arrive later, which builds the late crest and brings more latecomers, a cycle with no negative link.

### Chains That Do Not Close {#sec-a6-chains}

These are real, evidenced mechanisms, and none closes. For the first two that is the diagnostic point.

#### L13: from standing booking to fixed-ratio surplus

This is the core chain of the Breakfast Paradox. Registration is mandatory and automatic, running whether or not the student is on campus, and the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead (confirmed). The registered count therefore stays flat, 928 to 1,123 a day at Kadamba in April, while turnout swings with the weekday and breakfast at Yuktāhār runs far below lunch under the same booking ([[sec:a5]]).

![L13: the core chain. A flat booking meets a fixed ratio; the quality branch is dashed because its closing link is not evidenced.|Source: authors' analysis of Kadamba scan records and operator accounts.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l13.png){w=100 #a6-l13}

Four delays separate the decision from the meal: the cancellation lead of four days, which decouples the daily decision to eat from the bill; quantity set the day before, against the procurement tiers above; the chef's arrival at 5:30 with live items from 7:00; and the safety sequence, which finishes before any turnout signal exists. No link runs from the surplus back to the booking or the rule: operators control quantities, nothing routes their registers to the rule owner, and Kadamba treats the count as the obligation.

The surplus has an open branch at Kadamba. In the team's judgement (candidate) the main quality problem is food cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, one of them the display plate. If lower quality led to more skipping, the branch would close into a reinforcing loop, but that link is not evidenced, so it is dashed.

#### L14: from the closing rush to a just-in-case batch

Arrivals at Kadamba crest at the close ([[sec:a5]]): about 79 scans a day fell between 9:20 and 9:29, reaching about nine a minute at 9:29, and Yuktāhār's site head says the rush is always at closing time (both confirmed). Where the crest comes at the close, demand outruns what a top-up delivers in time, so one morning produces shortage and surplus together.

![L14: shortage is loud and surplus is silent, so the ratio can ratchet upward. The dashed link is a candidate.|Source: authors' analysis; arrivals from Kadamba scan records.](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l14.png){w=100 #a6-l14}

A candidate reinforcing closure sits on the chain. Shortages show as queues, complaints and back-door arrivals, while surplus is absorbed quietly, so an operator that raises its ratio after run-outs and never lowers it after surplus ratchets the ratio upward. No change of ratio in either direction is on record, so this is an asymmetry of intent, not an observed drift; Bakul states its premise as a rule (shortage outweighs waste, and the weekly review stops at a floor, single-sourced).

#### L2: Skip Meal

Skip Meal lets a student who knows they will not attend tell the kitchen to prepare less. Its first link, use by students with real non-attendance intent, is confirmed and close to zero: four of six respondents who addressed it had never used it, one because "it doesn't return the money." The kitchen link is in open conflict, as set out above, and even if it holds, nothing connects the resulting waste back to whether a student uses Skip Meal next time, so no self-correcting dynamic exists ([[fig:a6-l2]]).

![L2: Skip Meal. The student link is confirmed and near zero; the kitchen link is in open conflict.|Source: student interviews; Kadamba operator's account (single-sourced).](phase-2-rebuild/Assets/s07b-l2.png){w=100 #a6-l2}

#### L5, L6 and L7

**L5, menu transparency.** The rotation that closed L4 made the menu predictable, so a student can decide in advance not to come on a disliked day, a side effect the facilities head raised unprompted. Turnout does depend on the item: Yuktāhār's site head reports longer queues on aloo paratha and dosa days and 92 per cent for Sunday-night paneer, and Kadamba non-vegetarian breakfasts ran at 48.5 per cent against 32.4 for vegetarian (both confirmed); Bakul's manager says pav bhaji draws more students and muesli and Chocos go readily (single-sourced).

**L6, shock-adaptive relaxation.** In a gas shortage, and separately in some holidays and long weekends, the institution made registration non-compulsory and billed only for consumption ([[sec:a6-precedent]]). The trigger and the walk-in mechanism are confirmed; Kadamba's operator describes cutting quantities and buying some items outside during the shock (single-sourced); which holidays were relaxed is unconfirmed.

**L7, late-night workload to skipping.** Staying up late is the best-evidenced single cause of skipping: "If I sleep around 3:00 or 4:00 a.m. or later, I don't tend to wake up. I prefer sleep over breakfast." The records match as a population pattern, since the export carries no student identifier: Sunday is Kadamba's lowest breakfast at 27.3 per cent and among Yuktāhār's lowest at 17.9 to 24.1 per cent, and Kadamba's median scan falls between 8:51 and 9:11, consistent with late waking.

### Absent, Disconfirmed and Static Mechanisms {#sec-a6-absent}

**L3, capacity redistribution (strongly contraindicated).** The plausible story, that scarcity at Kadamba pushes students to Bakul or Palash, runs against the evidence: scarcity makes students lock Kadamba down harder, as the respondent who registers all month says directly, and Bakul's low registration is better explained by a standing preference gap. The verdict rests on one quote corroborated by the aggregate pattern, and Bakul and Palash have no attendance records.

**L8, vendor quality discipline (unresolved).** Student billing tracks registration (confirmed), but the vendor's payment is a different link and the evidence conflicts. Bakul's manager says payment is a monthly lump sum with deductions, based on plates served, though "served" is the synthesis's wording of one remark and may mean plates billed; Kadamba's "they are getting paid for it" hints that payment follows registration. No contract has been seen.

**L16, operator waste to menu choice (absent).** Operators see which items waste, all three say students or the committee decide the menu, and Bakul's manager is asking for input; his knowledge of which items go and which pairings work goes unused, though whether Bakul residents share his view is untested. Decision rights sever the loop: operators hold the data and the committee holds the choice.

**L10, the measured-but-unrouted gap (a static condition).** The gap is measured in at least three places: the dining office's scan export (62.9 per cent no-shows for Kadamba breakfast), Yuktāhār's register (roughly 72 per cent of registered breakfasts unused) and its other books (confirmed); at Bakul it is the manager's weekly review, possibly unwritten. The facilities head's 35 to 40 per cent breakfast figure is confirmed for Kadamba at 37.1 per cent. Predictable skipping is acted on in the quantity of food, never in the registration rule. The instrument exists, so the failure is a missing route, which is hard to build even where records exist because Yuktāhār's are on paper.

### Unintended Consequences {#sec-a6-unintended}

The rules and actions that produced unintended effects are tabulated in [[tab:a5-unintended]]. ### Structural Causes and the Loop Picture {#sec-a6-structural}

[[sec:a7]] analyses the three structural causes. For the loops they matter in three ways. Registration is an automatic, mandatory standing booking that cannot follow the morning decision to eat, which keeps the count flat in L13.

Beneath the kitchen half, three kitchens convert registrations into a first batch under three regimes, and each can correct only upward within a meal. This mechanism sits inside authority the operator already holds; it explains why L13 produces surplus on low-turnout days, why L12 cannot remove it and why L14's hedge can ratchet. Raw surplus is largely recoverable and cooked surplus is not, so the binding delay lies between the cooked first batch and the late crest, not in the four-day cancellation lead.

### What This Means for the Reframe {#sec-a6-reframe}

Two kinds of closed loop are demonstrated: L4 shows governance closing a loop end to end when a complaint is loud enough, and L11 and L12 show the kitchen closing loops every day. Neither touches registration. L1 and L6 fit a stable equilibrium but neither closes on the evidence. The equilibrium reading rests on the absorption layer (resale and informal transfer, the operator's discount and live top-up, reuse, outsiders and staff, held food, back-door service, outside venues, the bin) and on the asymmetry of attention: shortages are loud and trigger fast correction, while surplus is silent, can ratchet the ratio upward (L14) and never reaches the menu owner (L16).

### Decisions, Trade-offs and Revisions {#sec-a6-decisions}

Decision: Add a service blueprint to the system map as a new method. A Checkland-style rich picture was rejected (the course does not teach it; it would add notation without a finding), as was relabelling an unengaged mechanism as the fixes-that-fail archetype (nothing had been applied and made things worse). The blueprint stayed because it showed which artefacts cross the line of visibility.

Decision: Draw a mechanism as a loop only if its closing link has direct evidence, and re-test closure in every pass. Three previously named loops became open chains and one a calendar switch; calling every plausible cycle a loop would have given a richer picture that could not tell a reader what to trust.

Decision: Use one loop scheme for Phase 2, L1 to L17 plus L3b, with the loop analysis authoritative and a deliverable of its own; the first map's separate loops (R1 and B1 to B4) did not match it.

Decision: Name the cooked first batch, sized as an uncalibrated ratio of registrations, as the binding constraint, replacing the four-day procurement lock: the four-day close applies to registration, operators buy on their own schedules and set quantities the day before, and only dry goods are committed early.

Decision: Treat composting as unverified and remove it from the surplus absorbers, with the bin as the exit, because the facilities head's account could not be reconciled with the operator's first-hand one.

Decision: Describe the gap as measured twice but not routed to the rule owner, and drop the earlier claim that it is invisible: the scan records and the Yuktāhār books both measure it.
