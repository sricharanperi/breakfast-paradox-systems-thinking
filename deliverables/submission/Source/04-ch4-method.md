# Research Methodology and Instruments {#ch-method}

This chapter records how the evidence for the project was planned, gathered, checked and revised. It gives the research objectives and questions, the interview instruments in full, the fieldwork, the treatment of records, the evidence labels, and the protocol by which new evidence changed work already written. The activity chapters report what each method found.

## Research Design {#sec-m-design}

The project is a single-case study of one institutional system, the registration, billing, cooking, serving and disposal of breakfast in the four dining halls of the institute. Three kinds of evidence were combined. Interviews, observation and visits explained how the system works and why people act as they do, because these methods suit an unfamiliar workflow and support claims about mechanism. Administrative records measured the system, because a claim about how often something happens needs counts and not recollection (Nunnally and Farkas, 2016; Hall, 2013).

Two features of the design shaped everything that follows. The opening description of the puzzle was treated as a set of hypotheses to be tested, and each hypothesis was attached to a question in an instrument ([[tab:m-hypotheses]]). Evidence also arrived in batches and not all at once: a self-account and portal screens in the first week, five student interviews on 6 September, a governance interview on 13 September, operator visits and records on 26 and 27 September, and a summary of the Bakul interview on 28 September.

Interviews support mechanism claims of the form "this is how and why", and cannot support claims of the form "most students do this". Prevalence claims in the project rest on records. Where only interviews exist, the text says so and gives the number of voices.

Decision: Use semi-structured interviews and records instead of a survey. The population of interest was small and accessible, the mechanism was unfamiliar, and a survey would have asked students to report on a system whose rules several of them did not know. A survey was kept as a possible later step for any prevalence claim about reasons.

The coverage has gaps that the project did not hide. The head of the dining services body was not interviewed, and two different people are on record for that post. Nobody from the Mess Committee, the Warden's office or the Student Parliament's mess seats was interviewed, although the Parliament's elected mess representatives were identified as the most reachable governance contact and were first on the list for any further round. The academic administration was not interviewed; its absence from the mess system was confirmed from three independent accounts instead.

Hall (2013) offers a working test for when interviews are enough: the team can say who the stakeholders are, what they stand to gain or lose, and whether the accounts agree or conflict. By that test the student side gave a consistent first account of the main mechanisms (registration in blocks, Skip Meal unused, resale), and the governance side did not.

Decision: Treat a team member's account as evidence, labelled by its source, and keep it verbatim. A person who is both a stakeholder and a member of the team is a legitimate first informant, and the team recorded the account without adding or inferring facts, then tested it against screens and later respondents.

## Standards Applied to the Instruments {#sec-m-standards}

The first questionnaire was criticised for being very direct and likely to produce leading answers. The team read the interview and questionnaire chapters of the course's UX-research and interaction-design texts (Sharp, Preece and Rogers, 2019; Nunnally and Farkas, 2016; Hall, 2013; Dumas and Loring, 2008) and rewrote the instruments to a standing standard. The rules were these.

1. Ask the fact before the reason. A question such as "why do you skip" assumes that the person skips, and makes it awkward to say otherwise.
2. Check every question for four kinds of bias: a leading question, a shallow yes or no question, the interviewer's own opinion leaking in, and unconscious assumptions in the wording.
3. Tie every question to a named objective. A question that does not serve one is cut.
4. Keep questions single and plain. Compound questions are split, jargon such as the portal's own term for a toggle is described in ordinary words first, and negative phrasing is avoided.
5. Structure each interview as an introduction, an easy factual question, narrative questions, and an open close. Treat the list as a checklist and not as a script.
6. Conduct the interview neutrally: use the same acknowledgement whatever the answer, probe with an invitation such as "tell me a little more about that", allow silence, note exact words, and keep the interviewer's own talk short.
7. Describe the groups of respondents as roles, meaning relationships to the system, and not as invented personas, because one person can play several roles in a week (Constantine, 2006). A student may register in one role, attend or not in another, and sell on the resale group in a third.
8. Keep mechanism claims apart from prevalence claims, as set out in [[sec:m-design]].

Decision: Rewrite the first questionnaire to these rules and keep them as a standing reference for later rounds. The rewrite changed wording and order but not the topics; one question on whether a refund had been considered was softened to ask first whether the topic had ever come up.

## Conduct of Interviews and Fieldwork {#sec-m-conduct}

### Student interviews {#sec-m-student}

The five student interviews followed the short list in [[tab:m-guide1]]. The source document labelled the respondents "daily eater 1", "daily eater 2", "daily eater 3" and "skip 1" to "skip 3", gave no demographic detail and no date, and was supplied to the project on 6 September. Two team members worked on it independently.

The comparison turned up a labelling gap. The labels "daily eater 2" and "daily eater 3" appear one after the other in the source with only one full question-and-answer transcript following them both. Either a respondent's answers are missing, or one label is a mistake. The team could not tell which from the source and did not relabel anything. It counted five transcripts and, at most, four to five distinct people, and carried that wording into every later use.

Decision: Flag the labelling gap and keep both transcriptions. Relabelling would have swapped one guess for another, and the two independent readings of the same source were the best check available. Where the second reading was better supported (the registration cycle is monthly and not weekly, and the two apparent crowd peaks are one building crowd), the team adopted it.

### Governance interview and kitchen walkthrough {#sec-m-governance}

On 13 September a team member interviewed the head of the facilities department, the parent of the dining services body, and walked through Kadamba's kitchen. The interview covered the governance chain and the route by which complaints escalate, the fortnightly menu rotation, the four-day registration and vendor cadence, how stock is assessed, waste management and food-safety testing. The walkthrough covered equipment and inventory.

Two conflicts surfaced and were recorded without being settled. The overview poster gives Kadamba's capacity as 1,200 while the interview speaks of 420 seats and 1,200 diners in a ratio of one to three, which suggests that the poster's figure is a registration capacity and not a count of seats.

Decision: Record data conflicts as open questions with the evidence for each side. The capacity of 1,200 and the 420 seats were both kept, with the likely reading stated as a guess.

### Observation, photography and posters {#sec-m-observation}

Observation was by visit and by camera. At Yuktāhār the team photographed 26 pages of the registers and books, one at a time, on the day of the visit. At Kadamba the team observed the display plate at the entrance and the tasting, and on the earlier visit the kitchen's equipment and inventory.

### Transcription, translation and reconciliation {#sec-m-transcription}

The audio was transcribed by automatic speech recognition, with machine translation from Telugu into English, run locally. A transcription directly in Telugu script was tried first and abandoned because its output was unusable. The Yuktāhār recording is reliable. The Kadamba and Bakul recordings give the gist, with garbled detail in individual sentences.

Decision: A person who understood the conversation outranks a machine translation of it. Where the two disagreed the team took the observer's account, kept the machine reading for what the observer did not mention, and flagged each remaining unclear number.

## Analysis Methods {#sec-m-analysis}

Different questions called for different methods, and each is described where it is used. The summary below fixes the logic in one place.

Structure and boundary (Activities 1 and 2). The boundary was framed in layers: elements inside, on the edge and outside, each tested by asking what it depends on. Stakeholders were generated through seventeen lenses, tested for a real role, stake or only an imagined one, tiered, and placed on proximity rings.

Power (Activity 3). Authority was analysed decision by decision: who proposes, who approves and who feels the consequence. Power and leverage were marked separately, incentive chains compared stated interest, what the system rewards and observed behaviour, and the analysis followed information flows to the point where each record stops. This follows the power and interest grid of Mendelow (1991), with the one-score-per-actor weakness removed ([[ch:litreview]]).

Process (Activity 4). Each decision point was traced with its actor, its information and its exception paths, in swimlane form. Formal and informal rules were listed side by side, and the points where they diverge were recorded.

Records and behaviour over time (Activity 5). Turnout was computed as scans divided by registrations, by day, weekday, line and quarter-hour, and breakfast was compared with lunch from the same register as a control. Timelines were built at four scales, and rule changes were placed on an event timeline with undated items kept apart.

System-level reasoning (Activities 6 to 8). A causal loop was accepted only if its last link causes its first and every link between them is evidenced. Chains that did not close stayed chains with a dashed closing link. Events were traced down the iceberg, where an event is a single dated occurrence and a standing rule or a statistic belongs in the structure layer. Archetypes were tested against the chains, and assumptions were tested against records.

Leverage and principles (Activity 9). The twelve places to intervene (Meadows, 1999) gave the scale. Each lever was ranked by depth and then judged by what a student team could do with it, and the three kitchens were compared as a natural comparison of rules held constant.

Scenario model and evaluation (Activity 10). The model replays proposed kitchen rules over the 23 recorded days from 8 to 30 April, using forecasts built only from earlier days of the same weekday. A portion is one diner's standard portion, so the model uses no kilograms and no food cost, and rupee figures are the posted registered rate times the counted uneaten registrations, with September rates applied to April as an assumption. The skip uptake and the pace of adoption are assumed.

## Evidence Labels and Source Precedence {#sec-m-evidence}

Every claim carries a label that says what kind of evidence stands behind it. The labels grew with the project: the first four came with the first deliverables, and the rest were added as the work moved from describing the system to designing for it. [[tab:m-labels]] defines them. In prose the labels collapse to the short form shown in the first column.

Table: Evidence labels and what each requires. {#m-labels cols=18,50,32}
| Label | Meaning | Typical source |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed | Read directly off a record, or seen by a team member on site, or stated the same way by independent sources | Scan export, registers, display plate, published rules |
| Single-sourced | Stated by one person or document and not checked against a second; includes an operator's claim about the operator's own practice, heard directly or through a team synthesis | Operator talks, facilities head |
| Candidate | An inference that combines sources and is stated nowhere directly; may be used in prose only with the label | Team reasoning |
| Assumed | A judgement made to continue the analysis, stated as such | Gaps filled to move on |
| Projected | An output of the scenario model: arithmetic on real records under stated assumptions, and not an observed result | Scenario model |
| Simulated | A reasoned expectation of how a person or group would respond, from their stated position and incentives; never feedback from them | Stakeholder simulation |
| Planned | A test or step that has been designed and not carried out | Co-design tests, pilot |

A claim from a machine-translated recording kept the extra mark *(unclear)* until an observer confirmed it. A causal claim that needed validation was a hypothesis and was never written as a finding.

When sources conflicted, a fixed precedence applied. The Bakul summary of 28 September overrode the first operations summary on the Bakul hall where they conflicted; the team's first-hand account of 27 September overrode the first operations summary on Kadamba and Yuktāhār; and the first operations summary stayed the authority for record-level numbers, which the later accounts did not replace.

Decision: State the confidence of every fact from the first document onward, with a small fixed vocabulary, and keep the labels through every later use. The habit let findings change cleanly when evidence arrived, because each claim already said how firmly it stood.

## Revision Protocol {#sec-m-revision}

Evidence arrived in batches, so the team agreed in advance how to treat work already written. Each batch got a dated summary of what it established, the conflicts with earlier sources, and what each activity should consider. Each deliverable affected was then audited section by section against that summary, revised, and checked against the sources and the decision log. A consistency read across each phase reconciled the revised documents against each other, for spelling, loop identifiers, absorber lists and numbers.

Table: Passes of work and what triggered each. {#m-passes cols=18,16,36,30}
| Pass | Dates | Trigger | Main effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| First drafts | 3 to 14 September | Self-account, portal screens, five student interviews, governance interview, posters | Phase 1 written in two halves, one established from the student evidence and one planned for the unobserved kitchen side; Phase 2 built as synthesis of Phase 1 |
| Corrections within Phase 2 | 13 September | Two data corrections and a review of the iceberg against its lecture definition | The iceberg rebuilt with single dated events; loops re-tested and several downgraded to chains |
| Pass 1 | 27 September | 26 photographs, eight recordings, the April scan export | Gap of about 30 per cent replaced by 63 and 72 per cent; four-day lock recast as a registration lock; first batch named the binding constraint; one loop scheme of 18 mechanisms |
| Pass 2 | 27 September | The team member's first-hand account of Kadamba and Yuktāhār | The 70 per cent rule confirmed by an observer; outside diners and half the staff replace the staff estimate; Yuktāhār's book system described in full |
| Phases 3 and 4 | 27 and 28 September | The revised Phase 1 and 2 findings, then lecture material on leverage, archetypes and evaluation | Levers LP1 to LP12, principles P1 to P7, concepts A to H, gates and the recommendation |
| Pass 3 | 28 September | Summary of the Bakul interview | Three operator regimes in place of two; the Bakul share corrected; payment on served plates recorded as a conflict and every lever that depends on it held |
| Rebuild | October | The three passes, taken together | All deliverables and diagrams rebuilt from the revised sources and checked for internal consistency |

Two standing rules came out of the passes. Whenever a later deliverable contradicted or refined an earlier one, the earlier one was patched and not left inconsistent. And evidence was frozen at a stated date for each writing, with the gaps listed, so that the work did not wait for data that would keep arriving.

Decision: Revise upstream phases before downstream ones, and never patch only the downstream document. Phases 3 and 4 depended on the findings under them, and a change made only at the end would have rested on choices that were obvious and unstable.

## Ethics and Data Handling {#sec-m-ethics}

The interviews were carried out as part of a course assignment, and the project record contains no signed consent forms. Interviewees and staff are referred to by role in every deliverable, and organisations are named only where the role cannot be described without them. The scan export carries no student identifier, and only aggregates are reported.

The team applied three further rules. Figures from a kitchen's own records are not used in a way that could be turned against the person who kept them, and this later became a rule of the design. The team did not use anyone's credentials to reach the portal.

## Limits of the Method {#sec-m-limits}

The evidence has the following limits, and each is repeated beside the claim it touches.

- Six student voices, with a labelling gap that leaves four to five distinct people. They support statements about mechanism and no statement about how many.
- One visit to each kitchen. Operators describe their own practice, and self-presentation bias is real: the head of one kitchen believed breakfast turnout to be about 50 per cent where his own register shows about 28.
- Records for two halls in two different months, and none for Bakul or Palash. The model uses only Kadamba's scans.
- Machine translation of noisy Telugu for the Kadamba and Bakul audio. The gist is reliable and individual numbers needed confirmation.
- Two operator accounts reached the team as syntheses and not as transcripts.
- No direct observation of a live breakfast, of disposal, or of any student's morning. The shape of the crowd rests on the scans.
- Stakeholder responses and co-design tests are simulated or planned, and nothing has been tried on the campus.
- The scenario model rests on assumed skip uptake and adoption pace, and the stress shocks are assumed in size and unknown in frequency. Demand on days when an item ran out is censored.
