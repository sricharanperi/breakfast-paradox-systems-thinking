# Synthesis of Findings {#ch-synthesis}

The ten activities answer the nine research objectives of [[ch:method]] in turn. This chapter gathers the answers into one argument, records how the team's beliefs changed on the way, and summarises the decisions that shaped the work. Detail and evidence stay in the activity sections; they are cited here by pointer.

## Findings by Objective {#sec-byobjective}

**Boundary and context (O1, [[sec:a1]]).** The breakfast system has three layers inside its boundary: registration and billing, the kitchens, and governance. Registration is an automatic default with a narrow, early exit, and the stated purpose of the arrangement, minimising wastage through advance planning, is not what the mechanism does. About two thirds of breakfast registrations go unused, while lunch under the same rules mostly works, so the cause lies in the system and not in the meal.

**Participants (O2, [[sec:a2]]).** Twenty-four stakeholders took part in the final map, with the operators as a cluster of three firms at its centre. The clearest absence is a relationship: the body that controls the class timetable has no channel to the people who run the mess.

**Power and leverage (O3, [[sec:a3]]).** Authority over the registration rule, the cut-off, the bill, the menu and the timetable is divided among offices that do not coordinate. Operators hold the knowledge and local power over quantity, and no authority over the rules. Students bear the cost and have the least power. Whether operators are paid on registered or served plates is unresolved and decides who has a reason to close the gap.

**Process (O4, [[sec:a4]]).** No forecast sets the quantity. After the registration closes, each operator converts the count by its own rule, the first batch is fixed before anyone arrives, and a cooked batch can be topped up but never reduced. Students who know in advance resell their registration, because the official exit returns nothing.

**Behaviour over time (O5, [[sec:a5]]).** At Kadamba 62.9 per cent of April registrations went unused, with a flat count and turnout that varies by weekday and crests in the last quarter hour. At Yuktāhār breakfast turnout averaged about 28 per cent against 56 to 89 per cent for lunch. The aggregate was stable even where individuals changed.

**Model and loops (O6, [[sec:a6]]).** Of eighteen suspected feedback mechanisms, three close with every link evidenced, and all three work inside a kitchen. The core chain from a standing registration to a surplus does not close, and no loop touches the registration.

**Root causes and reframe (O7, [[sec:a7]], [[sec:a8]]).** Three structures keep the gap in place: a default registration with a narrow exit, billing at the registration, and a gap that is measured twice and routed to no one who sets the rules. Two conditions keep them unquestioned: attention goes to loud problems, and an absorption layer takes each consequence before it becomes a signal. The problem was reframed as a stable system and not a broken one.

**Leverage and principles (O8, [[sec:a9]]).** Twelve leverage points, fourteen intervention points and seven design principles show that the deepest levers (beliefs and goals) are beyond a student team, and the levers it can move are routes, kitchen practice and a proposal. Sunday on Kadamba's vegetarian line is where three levers act together.

**Design and evaluation (O9, [[sec:a10]]).** Eight concepts were tested on paper against four gates, a weighted score, a backtest on the April records, stress tests and simulated responses. The recommendation builds the missing routes, changes the default through a Sunday skip on a fixed date, and tunes the kitchen alongside as the quick fix. The projected effect is large in the kitchen and small for students until the rule moves.

## How the Beliefs Changed {#sec-beliefs}

The first submissions of Phases 1 and 2 were written before most field evidence existed, and the project changed its mind several times. The large corrections are told here once; the full list, claim by claim, is in [[app:corrections]].

The most consequential concerned the size of the gap. Three documents repeated that about 30 per cent of registrations go unused, a figure that came from two students' impressions. The first record the team received, the Kadamba scans, showed 62.9 per cent, and the Yuktāhār registers showed about 72 per cent. Thirty per cent was a lunch-sized number. A second set of corrections concerned the kitchens. The first drafts described the vendors as one actor cooking to a forecast locked four days ahead. Visits and the team's field accounts showed three operators, each applying its own ratio to a count that closes four days ahead, with quantities set the day before. Composting, listed as an absorber of surplus, did not exist at Kadamba. The off-site kitchen that serves Bakul turned out to be in a different place from the one first assumed, and a reported change in its ratio from 70 to 80 per cent was, on the manager's own account, variation by item.

Two corrections concerned method. On 13 September the iceberg reading was found to be wrong: earlier passes had labelled standing facts about the system's design as events, when an event is a single, concrete occurrence. The reading was rebuilt from the primary sources, and in the same pass five mechanisms drawn as loops were re-tested, three becoming chains, one disconfirmed and one absent. Later, six loops that had been called closed were reduced to three under the closure rule. Two earlier explanations also failed. Cuisine was offered as the reason for low uptake at the north Indian halls, on the strength of a single portal snapshot that showed registrations as a share of capacity. Inside Kadamba, the vegetarian and non-vegetarian lines differ in turnout, so the line and the item matter more than the cuisine. And the idea that friends pull each other to breakfast was disconfirmed by six respondents.

Three revisions came from the design work. The first draft of the recommendation let the Sunday skip wait until the kitchen had proved itself, and the shifting-the-burden pattern showed the risk: a kitchen that improves can make the registration look unimportant, and the exit would never happen. The skip was therefore fixed to a date. Scores changed when the weights were reconsidered, and several levers were renamed or renumbered as the later lectures supplied a clearer scale.

## Decisions across the Project {#sec-decisions-summary}

The register in [[app:decisions]] lists every decision taken, with its date, the alternative rejected, the reason and the section where it applies. They fall into five groups. The first concerns scope and evidence: doubting the opening description, studying breakfast only with lunch and dinner as a control, labelling the confidence of every fact, trusting records over recall, freezing the evidence at a stated date and changing a finding everywhere when new evidence overturned it. The second concerns how the system was drawn: treating the operators as a cluster of three, judging power decision by decision, tracing each caterer separately, building the map around the registration count, and accepting a loop only when every link is evidenced. The third concerns the problem and the levers: placing the cause in the structure, reframing the problem as a stable equilibrium, never removing an absorber to create a signal, holding ideas that depend on the payment basis, and ranking levers by depth before judging feasibility. The fourth concerns the design: making measurement blame-free, keeping a concept expected to fail in every test, applying gates before scores, fixing the Sunday skip to a date, piloting on Kadamba's vegetarian Sunday line, sizing nothing for Bakul without records, and planning the team's withdrawal. The fifth concerns conduct and documentation: referring to people by role, keeping open conflicts open, and writing every deliverable so that it stands alone.
