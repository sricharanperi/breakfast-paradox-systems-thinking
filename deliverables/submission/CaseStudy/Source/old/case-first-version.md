The course handed the team a short story. A student's alarm goes off four times at 7:45 after a late night, he chooses sleep, and by 11:15 he is hungry and cannot think. The hostel dining hall serves breakfast until 9:30, and barely a third of the hostel shows up. The rest of the food is thrown away. The story has one villain, the clock.

In April 2026 the counter at Kadamba, the largest hall, scanned 11,941 breakfasts against 32,162 registrations. Every one of the 20,221 that nobody scanned was still billed. On 26 September the head of another kitchen told the team that about half his students came to breakfast. His own September register said about 28 per cent. Neither number had ever reached the people who decide how many students are booked in.

This case follows the three students who went to look. It is about breakfast, but mostly it is about a system that writes down its own failure every day and has no way of hearing it.

## The story the course told {#s-how}

The course offered four campus puzzles, and the team chose the Breakfast Paradox. Read closely, its brief is a chain: a late night, a skipped alarm, a skipped breakfast, wasted food, and a hungry student at 11:15. Every link is a claim, and the first one is the only one that anyone had actually heard from a student. Late nights do lead to missed mornings, and the first interviews said so plainly. Nobody had yet shown that the food was thrown away, that it mattered, or that the clock was the reason.

So the team did something unglamorous. It tied each claim in the brief to a question that an interview could ask or a record could answer, and set out to find out which links held.

The first links to give were in the question itself. The course asks why students skip a meal that they registered for. They did not register. The portal registered them, for every meal, in every hall, whether they would be on campus or not. And the kitchens, as the team learned within a fortnight, do not cook to the registration count. Each applies a rule of its own. The problem the team ended up working on was therefore this: the registration count overstates who will eat by about two thirds, every kitchen quietly corrects for that in its own way, and the records that could set the correction properly are kept and read by no one who writes the rules.

That turned the aim into something more modest and more useful: to explain why this arrangement persists, and to propose something that a small group of students could actually test. The team drew its boundary around breakfast in the four halls, from the portal to the bin. Lunch and dinner stayed outside the line as a comparison, since the same portal, rules and caterer run them. The class timetable also stayed outside, but on the maps, because it shapes the morning.

## How the team looked {#s-looked}

The team chose interviews and records over a survey. The group was small, the mechanism was unfamiliar even to many of the students inside it, and some of them did not know the rules that governed their own bills. A questionnaire would have counted opinions about a system the respondents could not see. Six students were interviewed instead, and the team treated them as evidence of how the system works, never of how many students behave in a given way.

The rest of the looking was done in rooms and kitchens. On 13 September the head of facilities was interviewed and walked a team member through Kadamba's kitchen. On 26 September the team visited three kitchens, recorded about two hours of operators explaining their own routines, and photographed 26 pages of Yuktāhār's handwritten registers. At Kadamba it watched the display plate go out and the tasting happen. Then came the largest piece of evidence: an export of a month of Kadamba's breakfast registrations and scan times, 32,162 rows with no student identifier, used only in aggregate.

The records changed the work more than the conversations did. Early in the project the team's working figure for unused breakfasts was about 30 per cent, drawn from two students' impressions, and it turned out to describe lunch better than breakfast. Once the records arrived it was replaced by 63 and 72 per cent. After that the team held to one rule: records over recall, and where a person's belief and a person's own book disagreed, the book won.

The limits are the usual ones for a student project, and the team kept them in view. One visit to each kitchen; operators describing their own practice; no records at all from Bakul or Palash; two halls studied in two different months. No signed consent forms were collected, and everyone outside the team appears here by role only.

## A default nobody chose {#s-default}

Every hostel student is registered for every meal, holidays included. The registration is automatic, and the charge follows it. The way out is narrow: five cancellations per meal type per month, made at least four days ahead. A student who has not cancelled and does not turn up still pays. One who changes their mind on the day and walks in pays about 70 per cent more, at the posted rates.

The portal does offer a Skip Meal toggle, and anyone who opens it can read what it is for. It lets the staff know so that they do not prepare food for you, and it tells you, in bold, that you will still be charged.

![What a student sees: the Skip Meal toggle tells the kitchen, and says the charge stays.](Figures/Figure_4.1_m-portal.png){w=62 #f-portal crop=portal}

The students had worked this out for themselves. None of those who talked about Skip Meal had ever got money back through it, and four had used the Mess Cell resale instead. One put it this way: "Even if you want to eat breakfast or not, it is automatically registered. We don't have any opportunity to nullify it."

Pull: It doesn't return the money. | a student, on Skip Meal

The consequence shows in the records. At Kadamba, 37.1 per cent of the month's breakfast registrations were scanned and 62.9 per cent were not. At Yuktāhār in September about 72 per cent went unused, with breakfast turnout between 17.7 and 40.2 per cent and a mean near 28. Lunch at Yuktāhār, under the same portal, the same rules and the same caterer, ran between 56 and 89 per cent. Whatever is going on, it is not a general failure of students to eat in the dining halls. It is something about breakfast.

![Yuktāhār, 1 to 20 September: breakfast and lunch attendance under the same rules. The dashed line marks what the site head believed.](Figures/Figure_5.29_a5-yuk-chart.png){w=100 #f-yukchart}

A second pattern is easy to miss. At Kadamba the number of registrations hardly moves, staying near 1,070 a day, while the number of people who eat swings with the day of the week: 42.1 per cent on Tuesdays, 27.3 per cent on Sundays. The three lowest days in April were all Sundays. The system is told the same thing every morning and gets a different answer at the counter.

![Kadamba, April 2026: the share of registrations that were scanned, by weekday. Registrations stay flat at about 1,070.](Figures/Figure_5.28_a5-weekday-chart.png){w=80 #f-weekday}

Added up, this is 20,221 breakfasts registered at Kadamba in April and not eaten. At the posted rates, the team estimates that students were charged about Rs 10.6 lakh for them.

## Three kitchens, three guesses {#s-kitchens}

Nobody cooks 32,162 breakfasts. The people who do the cooking have to turn a registration count into a pot of food before the first student arrives, and the team found that each hall does this its own way.

Table: How each hall decides how much to cook. {#t-kitchens cols=14,52,34}
|Hall|How the quantity is set|What is known about it|
|Kadamba|A fixed first batch of about 70% of registrations; the supervisor tops up within 10 to 15 minutes if food runs short|The operator's account. The scan records let the team check it against what was eaten.|
|Yuktāhār|No fixed ratio. The same dish on the same day of the menu cycle, two weeks earlier, in the Wastage Book; a daily meeting; a re-cook if food runs out early|Seen on site; the books were photographed.|
|Bakul|70 to 80% by item, with a floor near 70%; cooked at a kitchen about 8 km away in Hafizpet, a 20 to 40 minute drive|The manager's account only. There are no records.|

At Kadamba the arithmetic is stark. A first batch of 70 per cent of about 1,070 registrations is some 750 portions. The counter scanned about 398 a day. That is nearly twice what was eaten, and three times what was eaten on a Sunday. And yet the same kitchen runs short at the close. Arrivals build to one crowd that peaks just before the doors shut: 26.5 per cent of all scans fall between 9:15 and 9:29, about nine students a minute at 9:29, and another 9.2 per cent come in after 9:30. The first batch is cooked hours before this crowd is visible, and it can be topped up but never reduced. Idli, puri and bhatura were reported gone at Kadamba by about 9:20. A kitchen can cook for too many and too few in the same morning.

![The first batch against turnout. Solid bars are records; dashed bars are what operators say.](Figures/Figure_6.2_a6-batch.png){w=92 #f-batch}

![One crowd, cresting just before the doors close. The kitchens are committed to the batch hours earlier.](Figures/Figure_5.26_a5-crowd.png){w=92 #f-crowd}

The more revealing thing is what each person on the inside believes. The operator at Kadamba reckons about 150 diners come in the last ten minutes; the records show about 79. The site head at Yuktāhār told the team that about half his students came to breakfast. His own register showed about 28 per cent. The record that would correct him was in his own book, kept by hand and read, as far as the team could tell, only by his own staff. Yuktāhār's books do get used: the quantity for a dish is set from the page two weeks earlier, so a loop does close, slowly. When the site head saw that only 50 of 267 registered students had eaten on Sunday 6 September, 18.7 per cent, he said the quantity would be cut "not next month, next week".

The staff at Yuktāhār had not always been so open. Weighing the leftovers started out feeling like being checked up on, and the staff's first reaction was that "they are catching us". Only when the weighing was made blame-free did it become something the kitchen used, and the staff later reported less waste. The team took that as a design fact, and carried it into its principles: nobody should be hurt for what they write down.

Bakul's manager spoke from the other side of the same trade. A kitchen a 20 to 40 minute drive from the hall cannot top up in ten minutes, and the manager's rule follows from that.

Pull: There should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs. | the Bakul manager

None of this is an accusation. Each operator behaves sensibly given what it knows and what it is paid for. A remark by Kadamba's operator suggests that the count is what the kitchen is paid for, although the team has not seen a contract, and that matters in a moment. And the surplus does not simply vanish. At Kadamba, by the operator's account, 30 to 50 outsiders and about half the operator's staff eat from the same pots. This is why the team never wrote down "cut the waste" without a second thought for the people who eat it.

## The turn {#s-turn}

The team's first drafts were written between 3 and 14 September, from interviews, posted rules and screens from the portal. The maps were drawn, the story hung together, and the team was fairly pleased with it. Then the evidence arrived in three batches, on 26, 27 and 28 September, and each batch broke something that the team had already written down.

![The project timeline, September to October 2026. Three batches of evidence, on 26, 27 and 28 September, forced revisions of work already written.](Figures/Figure_1.1_timeline.png){w=100 #f-timeline}

Five of the corrections mattered. The first estimate that about 30 per cent of breakfasts went unused became 63 and 72 per cent. "The vendors" turned out to be three operators with three different ways of working, not one. The four-day booking deadline, which the team had named as the bottleneck, was not the real constraint at all; the real constraint was the cooked first batch, which can be topped up and never reduced. Composting, which the team had listed as a place where surplus food went, was withdrawn when it turned out that Kadamba has none. And a claim that Bakul cooked near the airport and had raised its ratio gave way to the plain fact that its kitchen is at Hafizpet, about 8 km away, and the raise was withdrawn everywhere it had appeared.

The first reframing of the problem, written on 14 September, had to go as well. It read like a consultant's memo. It named a fix and the office that should carry it out, and it did so before asking the more awkward question of what would make anyone want to.

The pivot came from something simple. The records had existed all along. The system had been measuring the gap twice a day, in the scan export and in the kitchens' own books, and the team had spent weeks reasoning from the student side instead of asking to see them.

## Why nothing changes {#s-why}

The team's central finding is that the paradox is not a failure. It is a stable arrangement, held in place by choices that are each reasonable on their own. Three structures create the gap. The registration is a default, with a narrow early exit. The billing happens at the booking and not at the meal, so a student who does not eat has no reason to care and no way to say so. And the gap, though measured twice, is routed to no one who sets the rules.

Two further conditions keep it from correcting itself. The first is that action follows loudness. The second is that the cost of the gap is absorbed before authority ever sees it. The team counted eight absorbers: resale on the Mess Cell; top-ups by the operator; reuse of food; the outside diners and staff who eat from the pots; held and reheated food; service through the late door; outside venues; and the bin. Most of them help someone, and that is exactly why they let the problem stay quiet.

The contrast with a loud problem is instructive. Students complained about menu fatigue, the complaint went through the Student Council and Parliament, and within a term it became a fortnightly rotation, approved by the faculty committee of the dining services body. The registration gap is far larger, and it has no such route. Nobody has ever complained about it in a way that the rule setters could hear.

![Why the gap holds steady: three forces create it, eight absorbers take up its cost, and only a loud signal reaches the people who set the rules. (CDS is the dining services body, CFS its parent body.)](Figures/Figure_6.25_a8-equilibrium.png){w=100 #f-equilibrium}

To check whether a feedback loop that might correct the gap existed, the team tested eighteen suspected mechanisms with a strict rule: a loop counts only if every link in it is evidenced. Three passed. Menu rotation, Yuktāhār's Wastage Book loop, and the top-up within a meal all close, all of them balance, and all of them sit inside a kitchen. None of them touches the booking.

There was also a sign that the system had already found a way out once. During a cooking-gas shortage the mess cut to one item at the same price, and the limit on cancellations went from five to ten a month. In holidays, by the account of the chair of the parent body, students walked in and scanned, and nobody who neither registered nor came was charged. When the shortage passed, the arrangement was dropped, without review. Why is unchecked: three explanations are possible, and none has been tested. The holiday part is also disputed, because the team's own account is that students stay registered through holidays. It remains, if true, a sign that the problem had been solved and then switched off.

![The same breakfast under two models. The institution has run both and kept the one that bills the booking.](Figures/Figure_6.16_a7-fixswitch.png){w=92 #f-fixswitch}

The people matter as much as the loops. Students bear nearly all of the visible cost and control almost none of the rules. The operator holds the best data and has no authority over the booking. The body that sets the 8:30 class start has no formal channel to mess governance at all. Decision rights over registration, billing, menu, quantity and timetable are split at least five ways. Whoever holds the knowledge has no authority, and whoever holds the authority has no knowledge. Power and leverage are different maps, and the team drew both.

This changed the question. The course asked why students skip. The team ended up asking why a system does not notice its own cost. Of the six ways of stating the problem that the team tried, only one explained why breakfast differs from lunch, why the pattern persists, why it reaches the booking, and why it fits the evidence.

## Two fixes, not one {#s-fixes}

Having a model, the team went looking for places to push on it. Twelve leverage points were ranked by how deep they reach and how far a student team could realistically go, and eight intervention concepts were drawn against them. One of the eight was a foil: a flat cut of the first batch from 70 to 50 per cent by instruction, kept deliberately to see whether the tests could tell a blunt fix from a considered one.

The trap that shaped everything was an old one, known as shifting the burden. A better-run kitchen relieves the symptom within minutes and may remove whatever pressure there was to fix the booking. The kitchen work is worth doing, and students eat the food that is saved. But it is the quick fix, and the team labelled it so wherever it is reported.

![Shifting the burden: a better-run kitchen relieves the symptom and leaves the cause alone. The dashed link is assumed.](Figures/Figure_7.2_a9-archetype.png){w=78 #f-archetype}

Box: **Quick fix and real fix.** The quick fix is kitchen calibration, which acts on the surplus within a week. The real fix is a route for the record and a way out of the booking that returns money, which acts on the count. Only the second changes what students pay, and it is tied to dates that do not wait on the kitchen results.

Every concept had to pass four gates before it was scored: it must keep the absorbers that protect people; it must keep food safe; it must aim at a structure and not a symptom; and any quick fix must be paired with a fundamental fix on a fixed date. The gates earned their keep. Two bundles of concepts both scored 79 per cent on the weighted scoring, and only one passed the fourth gate, because a weighted score cannot see sequencing.

The test slice was one line on one day of the week: Kadamba's vegetarian line on Sundays. On Sunday 19 April 2026, 817 students were registered and 179 ate. The other 638 were charged Rs 48 each, Rs 30,624, for a breakfast no one ate. The team replayed 23 April days from the scan records, arrival by arrival, against four kitchen rules.

Only the last rule keeps both surplus and shortage low. Together, calibration and a late batch cut the surplus from 362 to 91 portions a day, about 75 per cent. The flat cut leaves 150 portions of surplus and runs a line short on seven days out of 46, because one ratio cannot suit two lines that are sixteen points apart. The calibrated single batch cuts surplus most, and moves the cost to the final crowd.

The result for students is the honest counterweight. The kitchen rules leave the bills exactly where they were. Only a skip that returns money changes them. If a quarter to a half of the Sunday no-shows used a skip, April's charge would have fallen by Rs 0.40 to 0.80 lakh, and an every-day skip would take off Rs 2.65 to 5.29 lakh. The uptake is an assumption; a skip that returns nothing, as the portal's does today, changes nothing, which is why Skip Meal fails now.

The recommendation, in a sentence, is to route and read the gap together, open the Sunday exit on a fixed date, and calibrate the kitchen to buy time. The team fixed the Sunday skip at week 6, whatever the kitchen pilot showed, and replaced an earlier opt-in with a skip declared the evening before, because with the ratio kept the opt-in would have left the final crowd short and charged the forgetful.

Over twenty weeks the plan runs in three stages: a ledger that records the gap with its charge line, co-design tests and a joint reading at week 4; a Sunday skip proposed at week 6 and a Kadamba vegetarian pilot with a 9:00 late batch; then the skip in force from about week 10 if adopted, a decision on every-day skipping at week 14, and the team's own handover and exit by week 20. The team planned its own departure on purpose, so that the system does not come to depend on a student project. The pilot stops if two Sundays in a row run short, if the late batch fails twice, if any allowance meal is missed, or if anyone uses a figure against a member of staff.

Two of the principles behind the plan are worth stating here. Measure without blame. And never remove an absorber that protects someone in order to create a signal; build the route instead.

One thing must be said plainly. How twelve groups of people would respond to the concepts was reasoned from what they had said, not collected from them, and six co-design tests are planned, none of which has run. Everything in this section is projected or planned.

## What the team does not know {#s-unknown}

Three questions are still open, and the case leaves them open.

The first is who heads the dining services body. Two different people are on record for the post, one named by the operator and one from an earlier interview. The case does not choose between them. The second is whether Skip Meal reaches Kadamba's kitchen. The institution's account says that declarations are passed on; the portal says that staff are told so that they do not prepare food for the student, and that the student will still be charged; Kadamba's operator says the kitchen cooks on registrations alone. No record settles it. The third is whether operators are paid on plates registered or plates served. The Bakul manager says served. A remark from Kadamba's operator suggests registered. No contract has been seen. That fact decides who loses from a no-show and therefore who has a reason to close the gap, so the team held back the part of the plan that depends on it and kept the rest, which stands either way.

Other things could change the answer. How many students would actually take a Sunday skip is assumed at 25 to 50 per cent of no-shows, and the team tested 10 to 75. Whether a late batch at 9:00 can land by about 9:15 without delaying the crowd is untested. Kadamba's own figure of 5 to 10 per cent waste has no stated base. And the April pattern has not been checked in any other month, or at the halls that keep no records.

The study itself is small: six student voices, one visit to each kitchen, two halls in two different months, a model resting on one hall's scans, and responses that were reasoned and not heard. It is a careful look at a real system, not a measurement of the whole campus.

## What the team would do differently {#s-differently}

Go to the kitchens first, and ask for the records sooner. They existed all along, and the first honest look inside a kitchen changed more than weeks of analysis done from the student side.

State how sure the team is in the first document and not the last. Every finding in the project carried a statement of how it was known, and that habit let findings change cleanly when the evidence moved.

Be slower to name a fix and an owner. The first reframing did it, and the first bundle of concepts made the Sunday skip wait on the kitchen. The team reversed both when it saw that it had built the burden-shifting pattern into its own plan, at small scale.

And remember that measuring and routing are different things. The system already measures its problem daily. It cannot see it, because the measurement is not delivered.

The institution's own poster states the aim as minimising food wastage through advance meal planning. The planning that matters happens at the registration, not at the pot where the quantity of food is decided.

## Questions for discussion {#s-questions}

1. The operator at Yuktāhār keeps the best records in the system and still believes about half the students come to breakfast, while his register says about 28 per cent. Why might people hold a belief above their own record, and how would you present the number so that it is used and not feared?
2. A complaint about menu fatigue became a fortnightly rotation within a term, while the registration gap, far larger, has persisted. What distinguishes a problem that reaches the people who set the rules from one that does not? How would you make a quiet cost audible without removing the outlets that absorb it?
3. The kitchen bundle cuts Kadamba's projected daily surplus from 362 to 91 portions and does nothing for students' bills. Should the team have proposed it? What would change your answer if it were the only thing the institution adopted?
4. Billing on attendance ran during a cooking-gas shortage and in holidays, and was then switched off. Three explanations are possible. Which would you test first, and what evidence would settle it?
5. If operators are paid on registered plates, a Sunday skip costs the operator something; if on served plates, it does not. The contract has not been seen. How should a student team proceed with a proposal whose fairness depends on a fact it cannot get?
6. The team kept a deliberately weak concept in every test and let four gates overrule a weighted score. What does that say about testing only the ideas you believe in? Where in your own work would a foil be useful?
