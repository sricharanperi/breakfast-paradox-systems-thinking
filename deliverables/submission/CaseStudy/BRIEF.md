## 0. What the research says (short, and what it means for us)

Sources: teaching-case guidance (Emerald, Ivey, Univ. of Vienna "Ulysses" guidelines, Uniandes CBCC), Stroh and Goodman's homelessness case, the Goodman/Karash "six steps" course tool, Design Council Double Diamond material, plain-language writing on machine vs human prose. Searches did not surface a published HBS length rule, so length norms below are inferred, not quoted.

What good cases share:
1. **The opening does the work.** One to three paragraphs that stage a place, a person or a decision and plant the tension. The issue is "discovered" by the reader assembling pieces, not announced.
2. **Mostly chronological, more than one side.** The body tells what happened in order and lets competing accounts sit side by side (here: operator vs institution, student vs record).
3. **Exhibits are separate from narrative** and numbered in order of appearance; they should read in black and white, with labelled axes. The text refers to them but does not re-describe them.
4. **Analysis is a layer on the story, not a replacement.** Systems cases (Stroh) show the pattern through one community or institution and let the iceberg or loop appear when the reader is ready for it.
5. **A short close**: restate the issue or leave a live decision, then questions. Teaching cases pair with discussion questions and a teaching note; a student capstone case can carry questions and a short reflection.
6. **Length.** Teaching cases are a handful to about 15 pages with exhibits; legal and project-style cases run far shorter. Our 14 to 22 target is the upper end, justified by the figures.

How a case differs from the project documentation: documentation is complete and ordered by method (everything done, every decision, every table). A case is selective and ordered by what happened and what it meant: story, analysis, lessons. It leaves out whole activities. The reader should finish knowing one argument, not having seen one pass over every activity.

What reads as machine-written (weak signals alone, strong in combination): uniform paragraph length and sentence length; lists of three and parallel bullets everywhere; every section opening with a purpose statement and closing with a recap; recurring "In short" boxes; stock hedges and signposting ("Notice that", "It is important to note"); a section per activity because the process is the outline; the same dozen labels repeated after every claim; stat tiles as decoration. The fix is rhythm and selection, not only removing dashes.

## A. Title, subtitle, hook

**Title (recommended):** The Breakfast Paradox
**Subtitle (recommended):** What a hostel kitchen knows, and why no one who sets the rules ever hears it
Alternates: "Cooked for Everyone, Eaten by a Third"; "The Count Nobody Reads".
Running line on page 1, small caps: A systems thinking case from IIIT Hyderabad, September to October 2026.

**Opening hook (about 150 words; every fact is traceable):**

> The course handed the team a short story. A student's alarm goes off four times at 7:45 after a late night, he chooses sleep, and by 11:15 he is hungry and cannot think. The hostel dining hall serves breakfast until 9:30, and barely a third of the hostel shows up. The rest of the food is thrown away. The story has one villain, the clock. In April 2026 the counter at Kadamba, the largest hall, scanned 11,941 breakfasts against 32,162 registrations. Every one of the 20,221 that nobody scanned was still billed. On 26 September the head of another kitchen told the team that about half his students came to breakfast. His own September register said about 28 per cent. Neither number had ever reached the people who decide how many students are booked in.

Sources: story `02-ch2-framework.md` (and draft p.3); 11,941 and 32,162 `05-p1-3a5.md` (C); 20,221 `05-p1-1a1.md` (C); 50 vs 28 `05-p1-2a3.md`, chain 7 in `06-p2-2a7.md` (C, register; belief is the site head's own statement). ## D. The analytical spine (five insights, phrased as claims)

1. **The system measures the gap daily and cannot see it.** Scan records at Kadamba and the paper books at Yuktāhār both record the shortfall; neither reaches anyone who sets registration, billing or menu rules. Measuring and routing are different things. (`05-p1-3a5`, `06-p2-2a7`, `10-discussion`)
2. **A default nobody chose looks like a choice nobody honoured.** Registration is automatic and billing follows it, so "not showing up" is not a decision about a meal anyone picked; lunch under the same rules mostly works, so this is a breakfast problem, not a student problem. (`05-p1-1a1`, `06-p2-2a7`)
3. **Whoever has the knowledge has no authority, and whoever has the authority has no knowledge.** The operator holds the best data and no rule-making power; students carry the cost with almost no say; the body behind the 8:30 timetable has no channel to mess governance. Power and leverage are different maps. (`05-p1-2a3`)
4. **A quick fix can hide the real one.** Kitchens that top up and reuse make the gap quiet, so pressure on the booking never builds. Every loop that closes sits inside a kitchen. The kitchen work is worth doing (students eat the food saved), but it must be labelled the quick fix and paired with a fundamental fix on a date that does not wait for it. (`07-p3`, `08-p4`, `10-discussion`)
5. **Loud problems get fixed; quiet ones persist.** Menu fatigue reached a committee and became a fortnightly rotation; the registration gap is silent and was absorbed by eight informal outlets, most of which help people and should stay. The design rule that follows: never remove an absorber to create a signal; build the route instead. (`06-p2-2a7`, `06-p2-2b-a8`)
6. (Optional, one sentence in section 7) **A fix that worked twice was switched off with no review**: attendance-tracked billing ran in a gas shortage and in holidays; nobody evaluated it. (S, `06-p2-2a7`)

Recurring through-line to repeat at most three times: "the count does not reflect who will eat, and the record that could correct it goes nowhere".

## E. Teaching content

**Questions for discussion (six):**
1. The operator at Yuktāhār keeps the best records in the system and still believes about half the students come, while the register says about 28 per cent. Why might people hold a belief above their own record, and how would you present the number so it is used and not feared?
2. A student complaint about menu fatigue became a fortnightly rotation in one term; the registration gap, far larger, has persisted. What distinguishes a problem that reaches the rule setters from one that does not, and how would you make a quiet cost audible without removing the outlets that absorb it?
3. The team's kitchen bundle cuts Kadamba's projected daily surplus from 362 to 91 portions, and does nothing for students' bills. Should the team have proposed it? What would change your answer if the kitchen fix is all the institution adopts?
4. During a cooking-gas shortage and in holidays, billing on attendance ran and was then switched off. Three explanations are on the table (payment per plate may count registrations, a default is less work, the model reverts with the calendar). Which would you test first, and what evidence would settle it?
5. If operators are paid on registered plates, the Sunday skip costs the operator something; if on served plates, it does not. The contract has not been seen. How should a student team proceed with a proposal whose fairness depends on a fact it cannot get?
6. The team kept a deliberately weak concept (a flat cut of the first batch) in every test and let four gates overrule the weighted score. What does that tell you about testing only the ideas you believe in? Where in your own work could a foil be useful?

## F. Voice guide

**Person.** The team, third person, as house rules require: "the team", "Team Invictus", "the three students", "the students", then "they". Vary the subject; do not start 20 sentences with "The team". Where the draft has "we" (about 170 uses), recast to people and things: "Phase 1 showed", "the records showed", "the team went back to the kitchen". Names: use the three team members' names once in the fact box; in the text refer to roles ("a team member", "the site head at Yuktāhār", "the operator at Kadamba", "the Bakul manager", "the head of facilities").
**Tone.** Observational and a little dry; a sentence or two of surprise or irony when the facts offer it ("The record that would correct him was in his own book."). Short sentences next to long ones. Specific nouns (a register, a steel table, a 9:29 crowd), not method nouns.
**Concreteness.** One scene per section where the sources allow: the display plate at tasting, the registers laid out on the day, the 9:20 run-out of idli, puri and bhatura, the Yuktāhār staff first fearing "they are catching us".
