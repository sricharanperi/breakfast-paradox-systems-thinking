This report covers Phases 3 and 4 of the Breakfast Paradox project: why IIIT Hyderabad students get billed for breakfasts most of them never eat, and what could change that. Activity 9 works out where and how the system can be changed, producing the Leverage-Point / System Intervention Map and the Design Principles. Activity 10 takes it further. It designs and tests interventions against the system as a whole rather than any one stakeholder’s interest, and delivers the intervention concepts and prototypes, the scenario model and evaluation framework, and the recommended intervention.

We start from the Phase 2 reframe. The Breakfast Paradox isn’t a broken system; it’s a stable equilibrium, and three structures hold it there. Registration is an automatic default with a narrow way out: five cancellations per meal type per month, each made at least four days ahead, with no way to undo the charge beyond that (confirmed). Billing fires at the moment of booking, not at the moment of eating (confirmed). And the gap between the two is measured but never routed anywhere. The CDS scan record and the operators’ own books both capture it, but neither reaches whoever sets the registration, billing or menu rules (confirmed).

Underneath the kitchen side sits one intermediate mechanism: turning registrations into a cooked first batch. Kadamba cooks a fixed 70 percent of registrations per line (single-sourced). Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) cooks 70 to 80 percent by item, a figure its manager sets each week from his read of past attendance, never letting it drop below about 70 percent even on the quietest weekends (single-sourced). Yuktāhār plans from its own books. Every kitchen can top up a batch, but none will shrink one. Whatever the gap produces gets absorbed long before it becomes a signal anyone notices: through resale, top-ups, reuse, the meals of outside diners and staff, late service, holding and reheating, and finally the bin. Each actor is responding rationally to the incentives and information it has; the outcome nobody wants is what happens when those responses connect. The design opportunity, then, is to give the system a way to notice its own cost, by routing the records that already exist to the people who set the first batch and the rules, and by testing the beliefs that keep the working goal fixed on the registration count.

Claims here carry the project’s confidence vocabulary. *Confirmed* means read directly off a record (the Kadamba April 2026 breakfast scan export, the photographed Yuktāhār registers, posted rates) or seen on site. *Single-sourced* means it rests on one account: the Kadamba operator’s figures are first-hand, so the numbers themselves aren’t in doubt, but they remain his account of his own practice; the Bakul manager’s figures come from a team member’s synthesis of an interview with him. There are no Bakul records of any kind, so no Bakul effect is sized anywhere in this report. *Candidate* means an inference built by combining sources, and *assumed* means a judgement offered without direct evidence. Three more labels apply only to Activity 10. *Projected* marks every scenario-model output: arithmetic run on real records under stated assumptions, not an observed result. *Simulated* marks every stakeholder response: a reasoned expectation, not feedback from the actor itself. *Planned* marks every co-design test, none of which has run yet. Loop and chain IDs (L1 to L17 and L3b) and their verdicts carry over from the Phase 2 causal loop analysis. Leverage depth is written as a position on a twelve-point scale, counted from the deepest: place 2 is mindset, place 3 the goals of the system, place 5 its rules, place 6 its information flows, and place 12 its parameters.

# 1 Activity 9: Define Design Principles and Intervention Opportunities

Activity 9 works out where and how the breakfast mess system could change. It produces two deliverables, the Leverage-Point / System Intervention Map and the Design Principles, and moves through the activity’s four methods in order: identifying leverage points and reading them against archetypes and rank; exploring changes to rules, roles, incentives, information flows and processes; setting the design objective against the case for the status quo; and locating fourteen intervention points. The map and the seven principles close out the section.

## 1.1 Identify Leverage Points

Each leverage point sits on the twelve-point leverage scale, counted from the deepest, so place 1 is the power to transcend paradigms and place 12 is parameters. Place 5 (rules) is used only where a rule itself changes: the default and its exit, who decides, what a booking costs, or the standing rule that keeps another lever in place. Place 9 (delays) is reserved for flattening the closing crest, which no lever here attempts. Places 11 and 12 only appear as settings inside a deeper lever. Lowering Kadamba’s 70 percent ratio by decree would simply move the cost from surplus into run-outs at the crest, and the cancellation cap, raised from five to ten during a gas shortage and now back at five (single-sourced), only changes how many exits the default allows.

A lever is ruled out if it works by removing a mechanism that protects someone: resale on Mess Cell, reuse of leftovers, the meals of 30 to 50 outside diners and about half of Kadamba’s own staff, the walk-in buffer, late and back-door service, outside venues, informal accommodation. The over-cooked first batch, repeated holding and reheating, and the bin protect no one, so they are fair targets. Feasibility is judged for a student team, which can propose, pilot and evidence a change but not enact a rule. High means a prototype or pilot with an actor already engaged; medium, an evidenced proposal to an existing forum; low, a proposal only, because either the holder can’t be reached or the lever depends on a fact nobody yet knows.

**LP1: Build the missing routes.** Kadamba recorded 32,162 breakfast registrations in April 2026, of which 37.1 percent were eaten, and Yuktāhār’s September breakfast ran at 17.7 to 40.2 percent against lunch at 56 to 89 percent (confirmed). No route from any record to any rule owner has been found, a confirmed absence, and Yuktāhār’s two books even disagree on 3 September: 77 eaten against 110 (confirmed). The fix is a standing breakfast view per hall, weekday and line (vegetarian and non-vegetarian) of booked, cooked, eaten and left over, call it the ledger, sent to the rule owner and back to the operators, built on one agreed definition of “ate”. It also carries a weekly note of exams, events and holidays into the day-before planning meeting, since today these reach the Yuktāhār kitchen only by word of mouth (confirmed). The ledger has to accept paper, because Yuktāhār already dropped a commercial ERP system once (single-sourced), and it has to be blame-free. Delivery waits on a receiver, because two different people are on record as head of CDS. A monthly bill line showing breakfasts paid for and not eaten is a student-facing variant, conditional on LP2: a signal with no available response would only confirm that the charge can’t be avoided.

**LP2: Change the breakfast default and its exit.** Registrations sit at about 1,070 a day on every weekday, while turnout ranges from 42.1 percent on Tuesdays down to 27.3 percent on Sundays (confirmed). Only dry goods are bought more than four days ahead at Kadamba (single-sourced), and Yuktāhār takes milk daily (confirmed). The Kadamba team has already pushed back on the four-day cut-off (single-sourced), so a student-operator coalition for a shorter lead is the first evidenced shared interest found so far (candidate). The lever here is the default and its exit, not the cap. Going from lighter to heavier, the options run: a one- or two-day lead, an evening-before confirm-or-skip that lowers the count, Sunday opt-in, and no registration while off campus. At Kadamba, which cooks a fixed share, a lower count shrinks the first batch directly, but only if it’s the count the kitchen reads, and whether Skip Meal already lowers it is an open conflict. A skip must never cost a student a hall slot, because a Kadamba respondent books the whole month precisely because the hall fills up (L3b, reinforcing, candidate). The payment basis is unconfirmed for every operator: the Bakul manager says plates served, while Kadamba’s remark points to registrations (both single-sourced). If Bakul’s account holds, its position on LP2 is likely neutral or even favourable (candidate). Resale may fall, but that’s a need disappearing, not an absorber being removed.

**LP3: Operator goal: surplus is a cost.** Purpose, moving from “cook for every booking” to “feed whoever comes, at least cost”, sits at place 3; the numeric target inside L11 sits at place 8. Operators state different purposes under the same rules. Yuktāhār names quality, less waste, lower cost, standardisation and profit; Kadamba cooks on registrations “because it is a responsibility and they are getting paid for it”; the Bakul manager’s rule is “there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs” (all single-sourced). At Bakul, a run-out routes a complaint straight up to the managing director while surplus stays silent, so the rule is really the kitchen-level form of a system that only reacts to what’s loud (candidate). The fix is a wastage board, a target and a purpose stated by the operations manager, with the target anchored to the hall’s best recorded performance, Yuktāhār’s rate, or the institution’s own goal, never last month’s average, which just drifts down. At Bakul it can only hold as a cut to surplus with no rise in run-outs. If operators are paid on registered plates, cutting surplus lowers cost while revenue holds, though the rate may get renegotiated once savings show, and the cheapest cut is smaller portions, so the target always has to be paired with grams per plate, taste and run-outs (P7). The team can put a cost-per-line case to Prism’s operations manager, who hasn’t yet been interviewed.

**LP4: A standing operator forum that runs its own trials.** The three operators already run three different planning logics: Yuktāhār a matching-week lookup and weekday register, Kadamba a fixed 70 percent, Bakul 70 to 80 percent by item set weekly from past attendance and never below about 70 percent, topped up by phoning the Hafizpet kitchen (single-sourced; Yuktāhār’s books confirmed). Yuktāhār’s books are photographed (confirmed), and its waste fell between August and December once weighing was made blame-free (single-sourced, no before-and-after figures). Kadamba keeps gram standards but no learning record, and the Bakul operator believes Kadamba fills 90 to 95 percent when the records actually say 37 percent at breakfast (confirmed). The fix is a forum convened by CDS and hosted by Yuktāhār’s two project managers, where each hall runs its own trial and the ledger shows the results. Simply exchanging books would sit at place 6; an empowered forum with the ledger as the selection mechanism is place 4. What has to transfer is the preconditions: blame-free weighing, daily discussion, and a purpose that treats waste as cost. Kadamba’s layered chain of command carries a reprimand risk that Yuktāhār’s flat team doesn’t have.

**LP5: Record-calibrated first batch with a daily learning record.** L11 closes at Yuktāhār, runs as a candidate instance bounded by a floor at Bakul, and is simply absent at Kadamba. Creating it at Kadamba sits at place 6; strengthening it where it already runs sits at place 8. Kadamba cooks 70 percent of registrations per line, but biryani lunch runs at 100 percent (single-sourced), so the ratio is following expectation, not a record. April turnout came in at 32.4 percent veg and 48.5 percent non-veg (confirmed), meaning the batch is roughly 2.2 times what was eaten on the veg line and about 1.4 times on non-veg (candidate arithmetic). The fix sets the batch as the gram standard multiplied by expected turnout per hall, weekday and line, plus a named allowance for outside diners and staff and a float for bonda, puri, vada and idli, starting on the veg line, with a one-page daily paper record read the next day. Kadamba already cooks about 30 extra portions for outside diners, and about half its staff eat there too (single-sourced), so their meals and the raw reserve need to be budgeted in. The lever can’t yet be sized precisely: a 70 percent batch against about 398 eaten implies roughly 350 surplus portions (candidate), which sits oddly against Kadamba’s own stated 5 to 10 percent waste figure. At Bakul, which cooks 70 to 80 percent by item and keeps food for about 70 percent even on very low weekends against a turnout the manager puts at most around 60 percent (single-sourced), item-level cooked-and-eaten figures would let us check his weekly adjustment; his floor stays a named constraint regardless. It is a quick fix, paired with LP1 against the L17 risk and with LP6 against run-outs.

**LP6: Crest-aware top-up and a staged late batch.** At Kadamba, 26.5 percent of April scans land between 9:15 and 9:30, rising to about nine a minute by 9:29, and 9.2 percent, about 37 a day, arrive at or after 9:30, the latest at 10:07 (confirmed). The operator’s own estimate of about 150 diners in the last ten minutes (single-sourced) overstates this, and is a candidate driver of why the hedge exists. Yuktāhār projects from the first half hour (single-sourced), when only 15.3 percent of Kadamba’s scans have even arrived (confirmed). The fix has three parts: a weekday arrival card telling the supervisor at 8:00 what share of the day has already shown up (place 6); a late batch started shortly after 9:00 based on it (place 10, since it changes when stock gets created); and a grace period after 9:30, always paired with the late batch. Place 9 isn’t claimed here. The share seen by 8:00 runs from 13.5 percent on Sundays up to 22.5 on Wednesdays, and the share after 9:15 runs from about 32 percent (Tuesdays, Wednesdays) to about 46 (Sundays) (confirmed; used as factors, candidate), so a single flat factor would misread Sunday and Wednesday by about a quarter. None of this transfers to Bakul. The manager places his rush at 8:00 to 8:30 and aims to have food ready for about 50 diners at 9:20 (single-sourced), and a top-up there needs a phone call, cooking at the Hafizpet kitchen and a drive of about 8 km, roughly 20 minutes if called early and 40 otherwise, with the end-to-end lead time never actually measured. The Bakul version of this lever is really about the size of that held reserve and the latest call that can still land, set from measured data. The reserve is a hedge, not an absorber (candidate). LP6 is part of the quick fix.

**LP7: A cost trigger for attention, carried on the oversight line.** Loud menu fatigue got fixed through the Student Council, Parliament and CDS Committee (confirmed, L4); the much larger breakfast gap never entered that pathway at all. The facility team tastes every Kadamba meal alongside two Prism staff (confirmed, observed), but never checks quantity. The fix adds a standing threshold, say, cooked-against-eaten beyond a set level for several weeks running, that sends the case to the Committee alongside the existing complaint trigger. It is anchored to the stated goal, lunch turnout or Yuktāhār’s rate, never the hall’s own past, and it carries run-outs alongside surplus. The cheapest way to carry it is one extra line item at the tasting. Because an inspector writing down quantity can look like an audit, a breach triggers a review, never a deduction, and the line adds no delay to safety.

**LP8: Operator voice in the menu.** At Kadamba, uttapam, set dosa and upma waste the most while puri, bonda, vada and idli move well (single-sourced), yet it is students or the committee who choose the menu, not the kitchen. The Bakul manager wants menu input and already knows what sells, like pav bhaji, and which pairings he thinks work, like kachori with chole (single-sourced); that students would eat more of familiar pairings is his view, untested with residents. The fix is a standing operator slot at each two-week menu cycle, bringing item waste and run-outs to the table, which builds the missing route L16 as the quiet counterpart to L4. Input gets read alongside consumption records and checked against residents’ actual views; it is a voice, never authority, and no pairing view enters the record as student evidence on its own.

**LP9: A named owner of booked, cooked and eaten (held).** Decision rights are split at least five ways, across registration, billing, menu, cooked quantity and the academic calendar (confirmed). The actor who sets registration and billing rules bears none of the cost those rules produce, so a named owner, not a committee, would put the consequence where the decision actually gets made, and would inherit LP1. Yuktāhār already has a lighter version of this in its Wastage Register keeper. The lever stays held until the head of CDS is identified.

**LP10: What a booking costs: billing and payment basis (held).** Attendance-tracked billing ran during an LPG shortage and some holiday and fest periods, and it worked (confirmed). Operators are paid monthly as a lump sum on plates, with deductions (single-sourced, noisy audio). The Bakul manager says plates served (single-sourced), where “served” is the normalised wording of one remark; Kadamba’s “paid for it” is a hint, not a contract term. No contract has been seen, and the basis may differ by operator. The lever stays held because a billing change made without knowing the payment terms could push the operator incentive the wrong way, and student reliance on resale income needs checking first.

**LP11: Surface and test the governing mental models.** The beliefs holding the default, the fixed ratio and the unrouted gap in place sit at the base of the Iceberg chains, and nobody can change them on someone else’s behalf. Eight of them run into a record that doesn’t fit; the four Bakul beliefs meet no record at all, so the table below names the record that would actually test them.

Every session starts from the ledger, so the question on the table is what the record shows, not whose view wins. Sessions pair advocacy with inquiry and point at specific anomalies, such as 50 against 28, though at Bakul the record itself has to come first, since there isn’t one yet. Effort goes to the actors already open to the data, with the holders of tested views made visible.

**LP12: Change the goal the system actually seeks.** The CDS poster states the institution’s goal as minimising food wastage through advance meal planning (confirmed). But the goal the system actually seeks is the registration count: students are billed on it (confirmed), Kadamba cooks on it (single-sourced), and reading these together as the real working goal is a candidate reading. The measure of the stated goal is already being recorded and read by no one (confirmed). The fix makes booked-against-eaten and cooked-against-eaten, per hall and line, the real indicators, read alongside quality and run-outs, since a wastage goal read alone could just as easily be met by running short (candidate). CFS and CDS carry this by restating their own goal and asking for its indicators. LP12 is the move from the registration count to cooked-against-eaten; the other levers are what make that move possible.

The first archetype orders the whole set. LP5 and LP6 are the quick fix, worth doing because students eat the food that gets saved and it buys time, but it needs to be named as such in the ledger, since a better-run kitchen presented as the answer would stop anyone looking for the fundamental fix (the L17 risk). LP1, LP2, LP11 and LP12, with LP10 once released, make up the fundamental fix. The system is also chasing the wrong goal, the sharpest finding of the whole problem analysis, and that’s what LP12 answers. One past change already pushed the wrong way: the cap was tightened in December 2024 to ease Mess Office workload, narrowing the exit (single-sourced; effect on no-shows candidate). An earlier reading, that Bakul raised its ratio from 70 to 80 percent after run-outs, is withdrawn; the figures simply describe variation by item and day.

### Ranking: Depth and Feasibility

Depth combines place with role, so a fundamental-fix lever outranks a quick fix sitting at the same place; feasibility is judged for a student team in the weeks remaining.

*Table 4. Ranking of leverage points*

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | LP1 (6) | Fundamental, enabling | High / high to prototype | A named receiver |
| 2 | LP11 (2) | Fundamental | Very high / medium | The ledger |
| 3 | LP2 (5) | Fundamental | High / medium | Lowering the kitchen’s count |
| 4 | LP12 (3) | Fundamental | Very high / low to medium | CFS and CDS |
| 5 | LP3 with LP4 (3, 8; 4) | Links both | High / low to medium | Access to Prism management |
| 6 | LP5 with LP6 (6, 8; 6, 10) | Quick fix | Medium / high to low by hall | Waste base; Bakul records |

| 8 | LP8 (6) | Fundamental | Medium / medium | Records and resident views |
| Held | LP9 (5) | Fundamental | High / low | Head of CDS |
| Held | LP10 (5) | Fundamental | High / low | The plate basis |

LP1 ranks first even though it’s shallower than LP11, LP12 and LP2, because every other lever needs its input to work. LP11 is the deepest lever but depends on the ledger existing first. LP2 is the deepest rule lever that isn’t gated on the plate basis. LP12 comes fourth because nobody who could carry it has engaged yet, and a goal without indicators or an exit doesn’t do anything on its own. LP3 and LP4 are what carry the goal into the operator’s own routine: Kadamba isn’t missing information, it’s missing a reason and a routine. LP5 and LP6 are the fastest physical levers, but they are the quick fix, unsizeable until the waste conflict is settled, and at Kadamba they only land through LP3 and LP4. LP7 outranks LP8 because it gives governance an actual response to cost. LP9 and LP10 are held, not dropped.
