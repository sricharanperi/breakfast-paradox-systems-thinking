### Prototypes

**Prototype A: the ledger sheet.** One A4 page per hall per week, filled from the CDS export and the operator’s daily sheets. It can be written by hand and typed up later, just as Yuktāhār already does with its books (single-sourced).

*Table 12. Prototype A, the ledger sheet, filled for Kadamba on Sunday 19 April 2026*

| Weekday | Line | Registered | First batch and top-ups | Eaten (scans) | Left over, and where it went | Run-out time | Charged, not eaten |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday | Veg | 817 | From the sheet | 179 | From the sheet: outside diners, staff, reuse, bin | From the sheet | 638 × ₹48 = ₹30,624 |
| Sunday | Non-veg | 252 | From the sheet | 94 | As above | From the sheet | 158 × ₹66 = ₹10,428 |

Registered and eaten are the recorded figures (confirmed); the charge applies posted rates to April (projected, since using the rates this way is an assumption). Four rules sit at the foot of the page: “ate” means a scan against a registration plus any hand-logged scanner failures, shown separately; on a run-out day, eaten is a floor, not a count; the uneaten charge is printed right beside the surplus; and no figure on this page may ever ground a deduction.

**Prototype B: the plan card and daily sheet.** The plan card is one line per dish per line, filled in at the evening planning step. Here it’s worked through for the Kadamba veg line on Sunday 19 April 2026, using only the records that would have been available by Saturday evening:

*Table 13. Prototype B, the day-before plan card*

| Step | Rule | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Expected eaters | Mean eaten on the same weekday in earlier weeks (5 and 12 April: 168 and 159, confirmed) | 164 |
| Share arriving before the late batch lands (9:15) | Earlier Sundays, both lines pooled (confirmed) | 54.7 percent |
| First batch | Expected eaters × share before 9:15 × 1.5 cover | 134 portions |
| Named allowance | About 30 portions for outside diners (single-sourced) and a staff allowance (headcount unknown, assumed) | Added by name, whatever the batch |
| Float | Extra for bonda, puri, vada and idli (single-sourced) | Per item, on the card |
| Raw reserve | Kept ready for the late batch and the ordinary top-up | Kept |

Portions convert to grams using the existing standard (four idlis a person from 120 grams of batter, 100 grams of sambar; single-sourced). The baseline cooked 70 percent of 817, or 572 portions; with the late batch, the design would have cooked 173 against 179 eaten, leaving just 6 diners to the ordinary top-up (projected). The daily sheet records first batch, late batch, other top-ups, run-out time, what was left over and where it went; at Bakul, a per-item sheet is the whole prototype.

The rule: divide diners so far by the day’s usual share by 9:00, add 10 percent, subtract the first batch, and start cooking that many portions. On 19 April, 62 veg diners by 9:00 divided by 39.4 percent (the two earlier Sundays) projects about 157, a late batch of about 39. The grace period gets withdrawn if scans after 9:30 stay above April’s 9.2 percent (confirmed) for four weeks running.

**Prototype D: the Sunday exit on the portal.** One control on the existing Skip Meal screen for Sunday breakfast, open until 8 pm on Saturday, offered in one of two forms.

*Table 15. Prototype D, the two forms of the Sunday exit*

| Variant | What the student sees | Charge | Count the kitchen reads on Saturday evening |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opt-in (first draft) | “Confirm Sunday breakfast: you will be charged only if you confirm” | Only on a confirmation | The confirmed count |
| Evening-before skip | “Skip Sunday breakfast: you will not be charged, and the kitchen will cook less” | Removed on a skip | The registered count minus skips |

Whether Skip Meal already lowers the kitchen’s count is an open conflict, so the prototype requires that either form actually does.

**Prototypes E and H.** The menu note is a half page per operator per cycle, listing the three items most left over, the three that ran out earliest, and items above the gram standard, with residents’ views set beside any proposed pairing. The shared reading itself lasts an hour: each participant writes their own figure first, the page gets read together, and the session records, in the participants’ own words, what would show the belief has actually shifted. Nothing said is attributed outside the room.

### Co-design and User Testing (Planned)

These tests are planned for Stage 1, before any pilot begins; none has been run yet. They’re designed to catch a design nobody would actually use, which no model can do on its own.

*Table 16. Planned co-design tests (none yet run)*

| Test (planned) | With whom | What it checks | Result that would change the design |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday skip wording | 8 to 12 students: skippers, resellers, daily eaters | Understood at once; fear for slot or resale; preferred variant | Misreading: rewrite. Slot fear: guarantee on screen. Preference for confirming: opt-in later |
| Plan card and 9:00 walk-through | Kadamba supervisor and cook | Card fits the evening step; 9:00 batch without delay; 15-minute items; crest guess | Cannot land by 9:15: 8:45 trigger, higher cover. Too slow: three main veg items only |

The Bakul tests are preparing a later extension, and don’t gate the Kadamba pilot in any way.

### Scenario Model and Impact Evaluation

The model explores how the system might respond; it doesn’t predict. Every output is projected, meaning arithmetic run on real records under stated assumptions. It replays Kadamba’s April 2026 breakfast records (confirmed, 32,162 registration rows with scan times), comparing what each rule would have cooked against what was actually eaten, in the real order of arrivals. Kitchen forecasts use only earlier records, so the backtest covers 23 out-of-sample days, 8 to 30 April; student-side scenarios use the whole month. No food cost or waste weight is used; money here is simply the posted rate times counted uneaten registrations.

*Table 17. Model assumptions*

| Input | Central value (tested range) | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Kadamba first-batch ratio | 70 percent per line (60 to 80) | Single-sourced; a sensitivity band |
| Rates | ₹48 veg, ₹66 non-veg at Kadamba; ₹53 at Yuktāhār | Confirmed as posted; use for April assumed |
| Forecast of eaters | Mean of earlier same weekday and line (× 0.75 with an exam note) | Model rule; records confirmed |
| Arrival profile | Earlier same weekdays (crest 5 lighter to 10 points heavier) | Records confirmed; use candidate |
| Top-up time | 15 minutes (30 in a gas shortage) | Single-sourced; stress assumed |
| Named allowance; flat projection | About 30 outside diners plus 0 to 30 staff (0 to 60); 25 percent in by 8:00 | Single-sourced and assumed |
| Skip uptake | Charge removed: 25 or 50 percent of no-shows (10 to 75), 10 to 25 percent of skippers still eating (assumed). Nothing returned: zero | Direction confirmed (L2) |
| Opt-in turnout | 60, 72 or 85 percent; 5 percent walk-ins | 60 candidate (Yuktāhār June Saturdays); rest assumed |
| Eaters under a rule change | Held at records (±10 percent; ±25 for exams or a favourite item) | Assumed |
| Adoption and timing | Kitchen weeks 5 to 16; skip proposed week 6, in force week 10 | Assumed |

Three limits apply throughout. The records cover one hall, one meal and one month, and rest on the operator’s own ratio. Kadamba’s stated waste of 5 to 10 percent (base unknown) only fits a surplus of several hundred portions if most of it is eaten by others, reheated or never actually cooked, so surplus here means portions cooked beyond what registered students ate, not waste in the strict sense. And demand is censored: on a run-out day, “eaten” is a floor, so an unadjusted forecast would drift downward over time, which is exactly what the ledger’s run-out rule is there to answer.

None of this transfers to Bakul, which has no records of any kind. Its manager’s account (single-sourced) differs on every kitchen input: a rush at 8:00 to 8:30 with 10 to 15 arrivals in the last ten minutes, cooking about 8 km away from around 3 AM for some items, an unmeasured top-up lead time, and turnout at most about 60 percent, possibly itself an upper bound (candidate). The arrival card, the 9:00 batch, the 1.5 cover and the Scenario 4 thresholds all belong to Kadamba alone.

*Table 18. Kitchen rules on 23 backtest days (projected)*

| Rule | Cooked a day | Surplus a day | Short a day | Line-days short (of 46) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline: 70 percent, one batch | 759 | 362 | 0 | 0 |
| G: ratio cut to 50 percent | 542 | 150 | 4.5 | 7 |
| B alone: forecast plus 10 percent, one batch | 440 | 60 | 16.8 | 16 |
| B with C: calibrated batch plus 9:00 late batch | 485 | 91 | 2.6 | 7 |

The baseline cooks far ahead of demand (veg runs about 525 a day for 237 eaten). The ratio cut fails on both counts: 150 surplus still remains (138 on veg), and non-veg still runs short on 7 of 23 days, since its turnout reaches 59 percent on busy days. One flat ratio simply can’t serve two lines 16 points apart. A calibrated single batch cuts surplus down to 60, but it just moves the cost into the crest instead, 16.8 short a day (13.7 after 9:15), and on 26 April the veg line would have run out at 9:24, 45 short: L14 running in reverse. With the late batch added, 274 fewer portions get cooked, leaving 91 surplus and only 2.6 short, which the ordinary top-up covers; the effective ratio becomes an output ranging from 29 percent (Sunday veg) to 67 percent (Friday non-veg). Across true ratios of 60 to 80 percent, the reduction runs 165 to 382 portions a day: about 105 to 382 net of an allowance of up to 60, and about 214 to 274 at 70 percent.

**Scenario 2. Projecting the day from the morning.** Three ways of projecting the day’s diners from the morning count were tested against what the day actually brought.

*Table 19. Scenario 2, error in projecting the day from the morning (projected, on confirmed records)*

| Projection | Mean error | Mean absolute error | Range of error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat, from the count at 8:00 | Under-reads by 26.9% | 34.8% | −56% to +54% |
| Weekday profile, from the count at 8:00 | Over-reads by 15.6% | 28.6% | −35% to +113% |
| Weekday profile, from the count at 9:00 | Over-reads by 2.6% | 13.4% | −23% to +70% |

A flat projection under-reads because only 13.5 to 22.5 percent of diners have actually arrived by 8:00, not the quarter it assumes. The weekday profile removes most of the bias but not the noise, since the early count is still small, and reading the count at 9:00 instead halves the error again. Hence the 9:00 trigger, with the card rebuilt monthly.

**Scenario 4. The student side and the default.** In April, Kadamba students were charged for 20,221 breakfasts they didn’t eat (confirmed): about ₹7.4 lakh veg and ₹3.2 lakh non-veg, ₹10.6 lakh in all (projected). Kitchen rules don’t touch this number at all.

*Table 20. April charges for uneaten breakfasts (projected)*

| Scenario | Charges | Change |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | ₹10.58 lakh | None |
| Skip returning nothing, any day | ₹10.58 lakh | None; unused (L2) |
| D: Sunday skip, 25 to 50 percent uptake | ₹9.78 to ₹10.18 lakh | ₹0.40 to ₹0.80 lakh less |

| Shorter lead, cap of five kept | At most 26.5 percent of no-shows removable | Bound only: 1,072 students × 5 against 20,221 |

A skip that returns nothing changes nothing, which is exactly why Skip Meal fails today. One that actually removes the charge is the smallest rule change that returns real money, and it scales cleanly from Sunday to every day. The lead-time bound shows that the cap limits any exit, no matter how it’s framed.

*Table 21. Skip effect a week, by uptake among no-shows (projected)*

| Uptake | Sunday: returned | Every day: returned | Sunday: fewer registered plates | Every day: fewer registered plates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 percent | ₹4,010 | ₹24,692 | 78 | 472 |
| 25 percent | ₹10,025 | ₹61,729 | 194 | 1,180 |
| 50 percent | ₹20,050 | ₹1.23 lakh | 389 | 2,359 |
| 75 percent | ₹30,075 | ₹1.85 lakh | 583 | 3,539 |

Fewer registered plates only cost the vendor if it’s paid on them, which itself is unconfirmed. And if the kitchen keeps its ratio unchanged, opt-in starves the crest: 70 percent of a confirmed count leaves the two lines about 23 short each on Sundays at 72 percent opted-in turnout, and about 65 short at 85 percent, nearly all of it after 9:15, because the ratio silently assumes 30 percent of bookings go uneaten. A skip is less exposed to this than opt-in.

*Table 22. Lowest uptake at which a kitchen keeping its ratio runs short (projected)*

| Ratio | Line | Safe, Sundays | Safe, every day | Days short at 50 percent, every day (of 30) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 percent | Veg | 72.5% | 47.2% | 1 |
| 60 percent | Non-veg | 49.0% | 3.6% | 25 |
| 70 percent | Veg | 82.3% | 66.1% | 0 |
| 70 percent | Non-veg | 67.2% | 38.0% | 7 |
| 80 percent | Veg | 89.7% | 80.2% | 0 |
| 80 percent | Non-veg | 80.9% | 63.8% | 0 |

A Sunday skip is unlikely to cause a crest shortage below about two-thirds uptake on non-veg at 70 percent, or about half at 60 percent, even with a quarter of skippers still eating. An every-day skip at 50 percent uptake runs non-veg short on 7 of 30 days, about 1.4 a day, rising to 3.0 if a tenth of skippers still eat, and 8.9 if a quarter do. So the Sunday skip can go on its own fixed date, while the every-day skip has to wait until the kitchen actually reads eaters on every line.

**Scenario 5. Yuktāhār’s belief against its register.** Across nine legible September breakfasts, Yuktāhār registered 228 to 325 and served 41 to 105 (confirmed). Planning on the site head’s “about 50 percent” would cook about 63 portions a day beyond what was eaten, a gap the register itself removes (Sundays 18 to 24 percent, Tuesdays 31 to 40), with uneaten charges of about ₹10,500 a day at ₹53 (both projected). Yuktāhār’s real lever here is a change of belief, not a new routine.

Line-days short (of 46) run 2, 7, 13 and 15 from the lightest to the heaviest crest. The design fails safe toward fewer eaters and loud toward more. The late batch is load-bearing here: if it fails, about 44 diners are short and 33 wait, so the raw reserve is mandatory, and two failed Sundays running should stop the pilot outright. A gas shortage hurts mainly through waiting, and on such days the plan reverts to a larger first batch. A 10 percent shift in eaters keeps surplus between 89 and 100, so the no-adaptation assumption isn’t decisive either way, while the baseline’s surplus is almost pure ratio, running 254 to 471 across 60 to 80 percent.

Nothing physical actually moves in Stage 1; it’s spent building routes and testing designs. Surplus then falls over roughly three months, perhaps optimistically, since Yuktāhār’s own routine took about four (August to December, single-sourced). The charge only moves when a rule does; a kitchen that improves under a flat charge line is exactly the signature of a quick fix standing in for the fundamental one.

Beyond these headline numbers, every kitchen scenario except G names the staff allowance and reheats less, while supervisor workload rises at first and the share arriving after 9:30 (9.2 percent today) may creep up within its own stop rule. The bundle adds a weekly page and a portal toggle for CDS, and eases pressure on Mess Cell resale; G, by contrast, risks the allowance and risks trust. Only rule scenarios move the student cost, and only kitchen scenarios move the surplus, so the bundle needs both working together.
