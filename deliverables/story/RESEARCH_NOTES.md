# Research notes: telling Phases 1 to 4 as one book

Question: how do we turn four stages of stand-alone reports into one story book of 40 to 60 pages that a stranger can read and understand, with the problem, our approach, our trade-offs and our reasons visible at every step?

## 1. What the reference book does (structure only)

The reference is a 304-page graduation-project book (Tools of Joyful Operations). We take its structure and rhythm, not its words or pictures.

- **It is built as a journey of questions.** The preface says in one short paragraph what the book is and who it is for. Part 1 is "Intent and Inquiry", where each section is a question ("Why aren't products fun anymore?"). Later parts turn the answers into a framework, then into products, then a reflection. The arc is: why I care, what I asked, what I learned, what I made from it, what it did to me.
- **Pages are square and quiet.** One idea per page or spread. Large type for the idea, small type for support. Wide margins, a narrow text column beside a big picture.
- **Dividers carry the rhythm.** A chapter opens on a full colour-block page or a giant numeral, then a short intent box. A reader always knows where a part starts and ends.
- **Small repeated devices do the teaching.** "Ex 02" example cards (a photo, a one-line label, a tiny callout of what to infer), numbered "lens" pages with a why and a significance box, boxed "thumb rule" headings, pull quotes in oversized type on a plain page, margin notes for intent.
- **Personal first-person voice with plain words.** Short paragraphs, anecdote before theory, honest about doubt ("I'm not sure I liked it at first").
- **Decisions are shown as direction changes.** A "Direction" section sits between inquiry and tools, so the reader sees where the project turned and why.
- **It ends with reflection.** A short section on what the work did to the author, then the way forward, then sources. No new facts at the end.
- **Costs of the format:** it is image-led, so page counts balloon. We keep the devices but cap length.

## 2. Principles from outside (principles only, with sources)

- **Case-study structure.** Common templates run overview, challenge, process, solution, impact, reflection, and advise showing the journey through sketches, iterations and annotated decisions, not only the end result ([skills.sh case-study framework](https://skills.sh/owl-listener/designer-skills/case-study), [Miro holistic case study](https://miro.com/templates/holistic-case-study-by-carolina-poll/)). A design-research paper on narrative in engineering documentation notes that stories help carry design reasoning that diagrams alone lose ([Design Society](https://www.designsociety.org/publication/26729/the_role_of_narrative_in_evolving_engineering_design_documentation)).
- **Decision records.** Nygard's lightweight format has title, status, context, decision, consequences. Context lists the forces in tension in neutral language; the decision is a plain active sentence; consequences list the bad and neutral outcomes too; superseded decisions are kept and marked, not deleted ([Nygard](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions), [UK GDS guidance](https://gds-way.digital.cabinet-office.gov.uk/standards/architecture-decisions.html)). Their purpose is exactly ours: a newcomer should not have to blindly accept or blindly reverse a past choice.
- **Explorable explanations.** Victor argues a document should be an environment for thinking, not a fixed argument; the reader's reasoning should be given something to push against ([Explorable Explanations](https://worrydream.com/ExplorableExplanations/)). In print this means: show the number next to the picture, let the reader compare "what we expected" with "what we saw", and name the open questions.
- **Story spine.** A story needs a want, an obstacle, a turn and a consequence. For a research project: we wanted X, the evidence said Y, we changed Z. The turn is the point of the chapter.
- **Gap noted:** no source gave a ready template for systems-thinking storytelling or book layout. The blueprint below is our own synthesis.

## 3. Blueprint for our book

### 3.1 Working title and promise
"The Breakfast Paradox: how we found out why a hostel registers for breakfast it does not eat." Promise on page 1: in about an hour you will know the problem, what we did, what we got wrong, what we recommend, and what nobody knows yet.

### 3.2 Chapter list (about 13 chapters, 3 to 5 pages each)
0. How to read this book (promise, the one-page answer, the labels, plain-words box)
1. The question: the puzzle we were given, why breakfast, our first guess
2. Drawing the edge: what is inside the system and what is not (context brief)
3. Who is in the room: stakeholders, and who was missing
4. Who can change what: power, interest, leverage
5. Following one breakfast: the real process, the visits that overturned our first picture
6. Watching it over time: the scan data, the crowd curve, the weekly rhythm
7. One big picture: the system map, three kitchens
8. Where the loops close: causal loops, and the ones that do not
9. Why it keeps happening: root causes, the iceberg
10. Asking a better question: the reframe and the design opportunity
11. Where we can push: leverage points and design principles
12. Trying ideas on paper: interventions, model, scoring, the foil
13. What we recommend: sequence, decision points, pilot
14. What we got wrong, and what nobody knows yet (reflection, open questions)
Back matter: glossary, label key, ID key (L, LP, IP, P), evidence list.

### 3.3 Repeatable chapter template
Every chapter has the same six beats, each one to three short paragraphs, so readers learn the pattern:
1. **The question we asked** (one sentence in a pull quote)
2. **What we did** (method in plain words, who, how long)
3. **What we saw** (3 to 5 findings, one visual)
4. **The trade-off we faced** (two options, what each cost)
5. **The decision and why** (one sentence, then the reason)
6. **What changed in our mind** (the turn, with a pointer forward)

### 3.4 Six recurring devices
1. **Experiment card:** a boxed card with Try, Result, Surprise. One per chapter.
2. **Turning point:** a full-width coloured strip when a finding overturned an earlier belief.
3. **We were wrong about:** a short boxed correction (old claim, current fact, why). This replaces scattered "revised" notes.
4. **Trade-off:** a two-column strip, option A against option B, with the choice ticked.
5. **Plain words:** a small glossary box the first time a term appears (loop, leverage point, absorber).
6. **Decision log strip:** a one-line marker at the foot of a page, "Decision 14: breakfast only, because lunch works", numbered so the reader can find them in the back-matter list.

### 3.4b Evidence labels as icons
Keep confirmed, single-sourced, candidate, assumed (and projected, simulated, planned in the design chapters) but show them as small coloured tags in the margin, not as bracketed text in every sentence. Explain them once in chapter 0.

### 3.5 Visual system
- Page: square, 8.5 in. One idea per spread; a text column of about 4 in beside a figure or a pull quote.
- Type: serif or humanist sans body at 10.5 pt, chapter titles 30 pt, pull quotes 18 pt. Chapter number as a giant numeral on the divider page.
- Colour: one colour per stage (context, people, process, system, root cause, design, test) used on the divider, the tags and the strips; neutral grey for the rest. Blue for evidence, amber for open, red for corrections.
- Figures: about one per page, 50 to 60 total. Prefer simple pictures of what happens (a day in the life of one booking, a river from booking to bin, three kitchens as three kitchens) over node-and-arrow diagrams. Keep data charts for the few numbers that carry the argument. Each figure appears once in the whole book.
- Captions: one sentence saying what to notice, not what the figure is.
- Photos: field photos as full-bleed pages at chapter openings to anchor the story.
- Length cap: 55 pages plus back matter.

### 3.6 No-repetition rule
Every fact has one home chapter. Later chapters point back by name ("the 37 percent from chapter 6"). Numbers appear in full once, with the label. The stand-alone reports repeat on purpose; the book never does. The back-matter lists collect IDs and open questions once.

### 3.7 Tone guide
First person plural. Short sentences, one idea each. Say what happened before what it means. Name doubt plainly ("we are not sure"). No term without a plain-words box on first use. No stacked qualifiers, no triads for rhythm, no em dashes, no moral closers. People by role only.

## 4. Decisions this implies
1. Write a new book, not a merge: reuse evidence and findings, rewrite every sentence for the story.
2. Fourteen short chapters on one fixed six-beat template.
3. Square 8.5 in pages, divider page per chapter, one idea per spread.
4. Six recurring devices, used consistently (experiment card, turning point, we were wrong about, trade-off, plain words, decision log strip).
5. One home per fact; cross-references by chapter name.
6. Corrections are collected into "We were wrong about" boxes, not inline notes.
7. Evidence labels become margin tags; explained once.
8. Replace repeated node-and-arrow figures with plain scene-style pictures; each figure used once; keep only a handful of data charts and field photos.
9. Total about 55 pages plus glossary, ID key and open-question list.
10. Maintain the process log so another model can continue: every decision and its reason, with the file paths.
