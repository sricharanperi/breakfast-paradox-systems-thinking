# PART 1 — Exhaustive Account of the Shock-Adaptive Mechanism (Loop B4)

## 1. What this is and how it entered the project's evidence base

This mechanism is recorded in the sources as a **direct, verbatim user statement made mid-project, on 2026-09-13** (`MASTER_CONTEXT.md` line 486-489; `02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 105: "direct user statement, 2026-09-13"). It is not something the research team inferred, discovered in a document, or reconstructed from interview fragments — it was told to the team directly, by someone with first-hand knowledge of how the mess system actually operated under these conditions, and then formalized into the system map and root-cause analysis. This is why every source tags the *existence and shape* of the mechanism **confirmed**, while reserving the **assumed** tag specifically for the *interpretation of why* it doesn't stay standing (see §7 below) — a distinction the sources are careful to preserve and that this document preserves as well.

The mechanism is named **loop B4 — "Shock-Adaptive Registration Relaxation"** across all three deliverable documents, and is independently and repeatedly described as **"the headline finding," "the single most important addition to the system map," and "the strongest single finding of this entire expansion pass"** (`06-system-map.md` lines 126, 205, 209; `07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 10, 62-66; `MASTER_CONTEXT.md` line 489-491).

## 2. The two triggering conditions, named precisely

The sources name **two distinct triggering conditions**, and are explicit that they are separate events, not one:

### 2.1 Trigger A — the LPG/cooking-gas shortage in India

- Described plainly as **"a period of LPG/gas shortage in India"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128) and, elsewhere, as **"LPG-shortage conditions"** / **"the LPG shortage"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` lines 10, 26; `MASTER_CONTEXT.md` line 487). **confirmed** that this shortage occurred and that it drove operational changes at the mess.
- **Not found anywhere in the four sources checked: any characterization of the shortage as "war-related," geopolitical, or tied to any named international event.** A targeted search of all four source documents for "war," "war-related," "Russia," "Ukraine," "geopolitic," and "global/international shortage" returned zero hits. If this characterization exists, it was not recorded in any of the documents this analysis was instructed to read — it should be treated as **an external detail not present in this project's evidence base**, not silently inserted here. This is flagged explicitly per the task's own instruction not to invent specifics that aren't in the sources.
- **No exact date range, start date, or end date for the LPG shortage is given anywhere in the sources.** This is a genuine, explicit gap — not evaded, just not present in the material.
- A **separate, adjacent effect of this same shortage** is also recorded and is worth stating precisely because it is easy to conflate with the registration/billing change but is mechanically distinct: **"during a period of LPG/gas shortage in India, the mess reduced to serving only one item for the same cost"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128) — i.e., a **menu-simplification response** (fewer cooked items per meal, same price to the student), **confirmed**, but this is a *supply-side production* adjustment, not a registration or billing mechanism change. The sources present it as occurring during the same shortage period, immediately preceding their description of the registration relaxation, but do not claim the menu-reduction and the registration relaxation are the same lever — they are two separate operational responses to the same shortage.

### 2.2 Trigger B — holidays and long weekends, specifically including fest periods such as Felicity

- Described as **"during holidays and long weekends (e.g. Felicity fest, when a large share of students leave campus)"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128). **confirmed.**
- The rationale embedded in the source's own phrasing is explicit and mechanical, not left implicit: a "large share of students leave campus" during these periods, which is precisely the condition that makes standard T-4-day-ahead, registration-count-based forecasting unreliable — demand craters sharply and unevenly, in a way the standing compulsory-registration model isn't built to track in real time.
- **Felicity** is IIIT-H's annual techno-cultural fest (named directly as the example instance); the sources reference it here as one concrete instance of the "long weekend/fest" trigger category, not as a one-off unique to that specific festival — i.e., the mechanism is described as generalizing to holidays/long weekends as a category, of which Felicity is a cited example, **confirmed**.
- **No exact dates for any specific instance of this relaxation being triggered by a holiday/fest period are given.** Again, an explicit gap.

### 2.3 A reconciliation note on how the two triggers relate to each other across documents

The three documents describe the *combination* of the two triggers with slightly different granularity, and this is worth stating precisely rather than flattening into one clean story:

- `06-system-map.md` (the most granular account) separates the triggers into **two paragraphs with an explicit "Separately, and more consequentially" transition** (line 128): it attributes the *menu-reduction* specifically to the LPG shortage, then introduces the *registration-non-compulsory* change as occurring "during holidays and long weekends," and then states the *cancellation-cap raise* occurred "during the same gas-shortage period" — i.e., in this account, the registration relaxation is tied primarily to holidays/fests, while the cap raise is tied specifically to the gas shortage.
- `07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` (line 26) and the CLD template in `02_causal_loop_diagram.md` (lines 100-105) instead describe **both** measures — non-compulsory registration *and* the cap raise — as occurring under a combined "LPG shortage and past holidays" / "holiday/fest/LPG-shortage" umbrella, without separating which specific measure was triggered by which specific condition.
- **This is flagged here as a minor internal granularity difference across the project's own documents, not a contradiction of fact** — every document agrees, without qualification, that (a) both triggers are real and confirmed, (b) both mechanism changes (registration relaxation and cap raise) happened, and (c) they happened in the same general shortage/holiday window(s). What is *not* fully pinned down across all sources is the precise one-to-one mapping of "this specific relaxation measure fired because of this specific one of the two triggers, and not the other." Treat the finer-grained system-map.md version as the more precise account, but do not treat the other two documents' more combined phrasing as an error — they are simply less granular on this one point.

## 3. What changed about registration — spelled out precisely

The core phrase used across all three documents is that registration **"became non-compulsory."** Every source is explicit that this term has a specific, structured meaning — it is not a vague loosening, but two concretely named alternative pathways to eating breakfast, replacing the standing model's single mandatory path:

### 3.1 Pathway 1 — walk in and scan a QR code at the point of service

- Verbatim: **"students could simply walk in and scan a QR"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128); **"walk-in QR scan"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 101); **"walk-in QR, no billing penalty for non-attendance"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 26); **"actual consumption tracked at point of service instead of pre-committed intent"** (`06-system-map.md` line 209).
- This means: a student under this model needed **no advance registration whatsoever** to eat. They could arrive at the mess unannounced, present/scan a QR code (the same personal QR access credential the standing model already uses for identity/billing — see `06-system-map.md` line 20) at the point of actually being served, and be let in and billed on that basis alone. **confirmed**, stated identically and consistently across all three documents.

### 3.2 Pathway 2 — register 1-2 days in advance, at any mess (not locked to one)

- Verbatim, from the most detailed source: **"1-2 day advance registration at any mess"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 101); consistent with `06-system-map.md`'s phrasing, **"register one to two days ahead at any mess"** (line 128).
- Two distinct relaxations are bundled into this second pathway, and both matter on their own terms:
  1. **The lead time collapsed from the standing model's T-4-days (four days ahead) down to 1-2 days ahead.** This is a direct, quantified loosening of the same lock that governs the standing model's vendor-sourcing cadence (`06-system-map.md` line 16, "registration ... T-4 days," "this governs ingredient sourcing specifically").
  2. **The mess-lock was lifted: registration was not tied to one specific mess.** In the standing model, registration is mess-specific — a student is registered at (and billed through) a particular dining hall/kitchen system, and the four physically distinct messes (Kadamba, Yuktahar, Bakul, Palash) run separate registration pools tied to separate kitchen systems (`06-system-map.md` line 57, "registration is mess-specific, per ST6/ST7's structural separation of Kadamba vs. Bakul/Palash kitchens"; also cited as the reason loop B2, cross-mess capacity redistribution, does not function as designed). Under the shock-adaptive model, a student could instead register 1-2 days ahead **at any of the messes**, not just their normally assigned one — a genuine cross-mess flexibility the standing model does not otherwise offer at all.
- **confirmed** — this level of detail (the specific "1-2 day," and specifically "any mess," phrasing) comes from the `02_causal_loop_diagram.md` template, which is the most granular of the three sources on this exact point; the other two documents state the substance ("register ahead," "no lock to one mess" implied by the broader "non-compulsory" framing) without repeating the exact "1-2 day"/"any mess" wording verbatim, but nothing in any source contradicts it.

### 3.3 What "non-compulsory" explicitly did NOT mean

To be precise and leave nothing to assumption: registration was not *removed* as an option — a student could still choose to register (via the shortened 1-2-day/any-mess pathway). What changed is that registering **stopped being a precondition for eating.** Under the standing model, registering (or not) is the sole determinant of billing and, in principle, of being provisioned for; under the shock-adaptive model, a student who skipped registration entirely could still walk in, scan, and eat.

## 4. What changed about billing — the critical mechanism, spelled out precisely

This is identified across all three sources as the single most consequential part of the whole mechanism, so it is stated here in full, not compressed:

- **Standing model (for contrast):** billing is **"calculated from registration count, not attendance"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 16) — a student is billed for having registered, regardless of whether they actually show up and eat. This decoupling of billing from attendance is independently named, across the sources, as **"the single structure behind the largest share of symptoms in this project"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 41) and **Root Cause 1** of the entire diagnosis.
- **Shock-adaptive model:** billing was instead **tied strictly to actual consumption/attendance at the point of service.** The precise mechanic, stated directly: **"no billing penalty for non-attendance"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 26); **"no auto-billing penalty for non-use"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128); **"no auto-billing for non-registrants"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 101); **"actual consumption tracked directly at point of service instead of pre-committed intent"** → **"billing/waste risk realigned with true demand"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` lines 102-103, the B→C and C→D causal links, both individually tagged **✅ CONFIRMED**).
- **The critical, plainly stated consequence:** a student who did not register under this model, and who then also did not show up, **was simply not billed at all** for that meal. There was no default charge, no assumed slot, nothing to cancel. Billing existed only where consumption existed. This is the exact inversion of the standing model's logic, and every source frames it this way explicitly — e.g., `06-system-map.md` line 130: **"a completely different demand-matching model — attendance-tracked, walk-in-based billing — that solves the exact registration-attendance gap this whole project is diagnosing."**
- Confidence: **confirmed** at the level of "this billing logic existed and operated this way" (per the A→B→C→D causal chain in the B4 loop template, all four links individually confirmed at lines 105-107 of `02_causal_loop_diagram.md`).

## 5. The parallel change to the cancellation cap: raised from 5 to 10 per month

- Verbatim: **"the cancellation cap was also raised from 5 to 10 per month as a workload/cost-relief measure"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 111, tagged **✅ CONFIRMED** in the same sentence); consistent with **"the cancellation cap was simultaneously raised from five to ten per month"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 10) and **"the cancellation cap was also raised from five to ten per month"** (`06-system-map.md` line 128).
- **What problem this specifically addressed, stated directly in the sources:** it is named explicitly as a **"workload/cost-relief measure"** (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 111) and, in `MASTER_CONTEXT.md`'s own summary, as addressing **"workload/cost relief during the shortage"** (line 489, echoed in the task prompt itself). The standing model already caps cancellations at **five per meal type per month**, requiring the same four days' notice as registration (`06-system-map.md` line 22). Doubling that cap to ten meant students facing a genuinely disrupted month (fewer viable meals to attend because of the shortage, or campus-wide absence during a holiday/fest) could formally cancel twice as many registrations as usual without hitting the administrative ceiling — reducing both the volume of billed-but-unattended meals the office would otherwise have to process/reconcile, and the corresponding kitchen-side over-preparation and waste, during a period when the whole system was already under abnormal strain.
- This is presented in the sources as a genuinely **separate lever** from the registration-non-compulsory change, not a restatement of it — the non-compulsory pathway removed the *need* to cancel at all for a walk-in/short-notice student, while the cap raise gave *students who still used the standard registration channel* more room to formally back out without penalty. Both operated in the same direction (reducing billed-but-unconsumed meals) but through different mechanisms.

## 6. Duration: how long the arrangement ran, and what ended it

- **What is confirmed:** the arrangement was **condition-triggered, not standing** — it "exists, fires reliably under the right trigger, and retires once the trigger passes" (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` line 112-113); the institution **"reverts to the standing model once the trigger passes"** (`07-systemic-problem-analysis.md` line 66); `06-system-map.md`'s own B4 loop description closes with **"reduced institutional appetite to keep the compulsory model suspended once the acute uncertainty period ends (closes back toward the normal, standing state)"** (line 209).
- **What is explicitly NOT specified anywhere in the sources: exact start dates, exact end dates, or the exact duration (in days/weeks/months) of any specific instance of this arrangement being in effect.** No source gives a calendar window for either the LPG-shortage instance or any specific holiday/fest instance. This is stated here as a clean, acknowledged gap rather than estimated or invented.
- The reversion is framed consistently as automatic and reliable — tied to the trigger condition itself lapsing (the shortage resolving, the holiday/fest period ending and students returning to campus), not to any separate administrative decision process being described in the sources. Confidence on "it reverted, reliably, once the trigger passed" is **confirmed**; confidence on the precise administrative mechanics of *how* the switch-back was executed (e.g., whether it required a fresh announcement, a system toggle, a specific date) is **not specified** anywhere in the sources.

## 7. Why it reverted rather than becoming the standing model — the full T-4/procurement-certainty reasoning, laid out step by step

The sources are explicit that this specific piece — *why* the institution reverts rather than keeping or partially keeping the relaxed model — is tagged **assumed** (🔷 in the CLD template), distinguished sharply from the **confirmed** fact of the mechanism's existence and operation. It is described as **"structurally coherent"** reasoning, i.e., a plausible and internally consistent inference the project's own analysis constructs from the pattern of how and when the relaxation is used — not something any interviewed source stated directly as their own reasoning. Laid out in full, the argument runs as follows:

1. **The standing model's vendor relationship depends on a fixed, four-day-ahead (T-4) lead time.** Registration under the standing model locks four days before serving, and this specific lock is what is "communicated to the outsourced caterer on that same four-day cadence — this governs ingredient sourcing specifically" (`06-system-map.md` line 16). The vendor plans and sources ingredients against a number known four days out.
2. **Under the standing model, individual-level attendance is unpredictable, but aggregate registration is still a usable forecasting proxy.** Even though any single registered student may or may not show up, the *total registered count* is stable and knowable four days ahead, which is exactly what the vendor's sourcing process needs — a known, "aggregate-forecastable" number, even if it doesn't match true attendance (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` lines 120-121: "The institution likely accepts a known, aggregate-forecastable attendance gap under the compulsory model").
3. **The shock-adaptive/walk-in model breaks that forecasting basis by design.** Because a walk-in student can show up unannounced and be billed and served on the spot, and because even the "register ahead" pathway only requires 1-2 days' notice rather than four, the number of people who will actually eat on a given day is no longer knowable four days in advance under this model. Attendance becomes a same-day (or 1-2-day) signal instead of a T-4 one.
4. **This is tolerable, and does not break anything, specifically because the relaxed model is rare and short.** The sources state this directly: **"The exception works specifically because it's rare and short — the vendor can absorb one-off uncertainty it couldn't absorb as a daily standing condition"** (`06-system-map.md` line 132). A vendor's supply chain can flex to accommodate an unusual week (a shortage, a holiday) as an occasional deviation without needing to fundamentally restructure how far ahead it commits to sourcing.
5. **Generalizing the walk-in model to every single day, permanently, would remove the T-4 lock's lead-time guarantee entirely** — not just relax it occasionally, but eliminate the fixed-forecast basis the vendor's whole procurement cadence is built around (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` lines 119-120: "a walk-in/QR-at-point-of-service model, generalized to every day, would remove the T-4 vendor lock's lead-time guarantee entirely").
6. **The institution, under this reasoning, is trading demand-matching accuracy for procurement certainty as its default choice** — accepting a known, quantified, but real registration-attendance gap under the compulsory model, rather than accepting fully unpredictable day-of demand every single day, "which the vendor's lead-time contract may not support as a permanent condition" (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` lines 121-123).
7. **This is presented as a genuine, structurally coherent trade-off — not inattention, not simple bureaucratic inertia, and not evidence that "nobody has thought about this."** The project's own framing is explicit on this point: this reframes the whole diagnosis "from 'the institution has never solved this' to 'the institution has solved this before ... and chooses not to run it as the default'" (`06-system-map.md` line 130) — and separately, that this "does not contradict Task 7's fragmented-decision-rights finding — it sharpens it: the fix is not unknown, it exists, has precedent, and works, but the institution is not currently willing to trade away vendor-side procurement certainty to run it as a standing policy" (same line).

**What would falsify this specific explanation, per the sources' own stated falsification criterion:** "Evidence that the vendor contract could, in fact, absorb permanent day-of demand variability without a lead-time guarantee — this would mean the standing model persists for a different, currently unidentified reason (e.g. simple inertia, or [the] unowned-gap dynamic alone, without a genuine vendor-side constraint behind it)" (`02_causal_loop_diagram.md` lines 126-129).

**The single most consequential open question flowing directly from this**, named explicitly and repeatedly as the top unresolved priority in `MASTER_CONTEXT.md` (line 819) and `06-system-map.md` (line 285): **whether the vendor's actual contract terms would allow a partial or permanent version of the shock-adaptive model** — e.g., a smaller, permanent cancellation-cap increase, or a partial walk-in option for a subset of meals — without fully sacrificing the vendor's lead-time certainty. This question is explicitly **not answered anywhere in this project's evidence base**; it is flagged as the top item for any future intervention design, and this diagnosis-only document does not (and per its own stated scope, should not) resolve it.

# PART 2 — Systemic Problem Diagnosis

### Short statement (2-3 sentences)

The Breakfast Paradox at IIIT Hyderabad is not a technology gap or an unsolved design problem: the institution has already built, deployed, and successfully run an alternative registration-and-billing model — during LPG-gas shortages and holiday/fest periods such as Felicity, registration became non-compulsory (walk-in QR at the point of service, or 1-2-day advance registration at any mess) and a student who neither registered nor showed up was simply never billed, with the cancellation cap simultaneously doubled from 5 to 10 per month. This confirmed precedent (loop B4) proves attendance-based billing is fully operational at IIIT-H, which reframes the standing model's core defect — billing calculated from registration count rather than attendance — from "an unsolved problem" into a deliberate policy choice, made in exchange for the outsourced vendor's four-day (T-4) procurement lead-time guarantee. The paradox persists not because no fix exists, but because a proven fix is kept exceptional rather than becoming the default, and no actor in the fragmented governance structure has ever been forced to weigh that trade-off explicitly.

### Detailed statement

The central diagnostic claim of this analysis is not that the IIIT-H mess system has failed to find a way to match billing to actual attendance — it is that it has already found one, used it successfully, under exactly the kind of demand uncertainty that breakfast produces every single day, and chosen not to keep it running. The precedent is specific and mechanically detailed, not a vague "they could do something different": under two named triggering conditions — an LPG/cooking-gas shortage in India, and holiday/long-weekend/fest periods such as Felicity when a large share of students leave campus — the institution suspended its standing rule that a student must pre-register up to four days ahead and is then billed for that registration regardless of whether they eat. In its place, students could walk in unannounced and scan a QR code at the point of service, or register with only 1-2 days' notice at any of the four messes rather than being locked to one, and in either case a student who did not register and did not attend incurred no bill at all — billing tracked real consumption, not pre-committed intent. The cancellation cap was simultaneously doubled from five to ten cancellations per month, an explicit workload- and cost-relief measure for the same period. This is not a proposal or a hypothesis: it is a historical operating fact, confirmed directly. What makes it diagnostically decisive is what it rules out. It rules out "the institution doesn't know how to decouple billing from mere registration" — it has done exactly that. It rules out "attendance-based billing is technically impossible under an outsourced-catering, fixed-lead-time vendor contract" — it has run that exact model, successfully, under real supply-side stress. What remains is a genuine, structurally coherent trade-off: the standing model's registration-based billing gives the vendor a fixed, four-day-ahead number it can source against, while the walk-in model — if generalized beyond a rare, short exception — would remove that lead-time guarantee entirely, forcing the vendor to source against fully unpredictable day-of demand as a permanent condition rather than an occasional one. The institution appears to judge that trade not worth making standing, reverting to the compulsory model the moment the acute uncertainty period ends — but this judgment has, per this project's own evidence, never been evaluated or interrogated as an explicit trade-off, because decision rights over registration/billing policy, menu, kitchen execution, and academic scheduling are split across disconnected actors, none of whom owns the outcome end to end. The paradox, restated with this precedent as its centerpiece, is this: a demonstrably working alternative sits inside the institution's own recent operating history, unexamined for partial or permanent adoption, while the standing model it displaced only during emergencies continues to generate the very registration-attendance gap this entire project set out to explain.

# PART 3 — Key Findings and Conclusion

## Key Findings

1. **A proven fix already exists inside the institution's own history — it is not hypothetical.** During LPG shortages and holidays/fests (loop B4), attendance-based, walk-in billing ran successfully at IIIT-H. **confirmed.** *Why it matters:* every future intervention proposal for this system can point to a real precedent instead of an untested idea — the burden of proof shifts from "would this work" to "why isn't this the default."

2. **Billing decoupled from attendance is the single largest structural root cause in the system — and it is optional, not inherent.** The standing model bills on registration count regardless of consumption; B4 proves this is a choice, since the alternative has already been run. **confirmed.** *Why it matters:* this collapses the excuse that no-shows are an unavoidable cost of doing business at scale — the institution has already demonstrated it can bill differently.

3. **The mechanism of non-compulsory registration is specific and dual-pathed: walk-in QR at point of service, or short-notice (1-2 day) registration at any mess.** **confirmed**, per the CLD template's precise wording. *Why it matters:* this is not a vague "looser rules" story — it is a concrete, replicable design (real-time point-of-service billing plus a shortened, mess-agnostic advance option) that any future proposal can reference directly rather than reinvent.

4. **The reversion is real and repeatable, but the reason for it is inferred, not confirmed.** The mechanism reliably retires once its trigger passes (**confirmed**); the explanation — that a generalized walk-in model would strip the vendor's T-4 lead-time guarantee — is the project's own structurally coherent inference (**assumed**), not a quoted institutional rationale. *Why it matters:* treating a plausible inference as settled fact would misdirect any redesign effort; the actual vendor contract terms still need to be checked before assuming this trade-off is truly binding.

5. **The parallel cancellation-cap raise (5→10/month) shows the institution has more than one lever for demand-matching, and can pull more than one at once under stress.** **confirmed.** *Why it matters:* a future intervention need not choose between "billing model overhaul" and "administrative tweak" — the precedent shows the institution is willing to combine both under pressure, which argues for a phased or partial adoption path rather than an all-or-nothing redesign.

6. **The core trade-off — procurement certainty versus demand-matching accuracy — has been directly, historically demonstrated as achievable on both sides, just never simultaneously as standing policy.** **confirmed** that both states have existed; **assumed** that they cannot coexist permanently. *Why it matters:* this is the sharpest, most concrete tension in the whole diagnosis, and it names exactly what any future intervention design has to either resolve or accept.

7. **Fragmented governance is why a demonstrated success has never been evaluated for permanent or partial adoption, not just why the original problem persists.** Decision rights over registration/billing policy, menu, kitchen execution, and academic scheduling are split with no single owner (**confirmed**, per the root-cause analysis). *Why it matters:* B4 is not merely evidence of a technical fix — it is evidence that the institution's own governance structure is failing to learn from its own operating history, which is a distinct and arguably more serious systemic finding than the billing mechanism alone.

8. **The single highest-priority open question is whether a partial or permanent version of B4 is contractually possible — and it remains completely unanswered.** **Explicit gap**, named as the most consequential open question for any future intervention. *Why it matters:* without this answer, no redesign proposal building on B4 can be evaluated as feasible rather than merely appealing.

9. **Not all of the registration-attendance gap is dysfunction, and B4 does not erase that caveat.** A meaningful share of low breakfast turnout reflects legitimate cultural, religious, and individual variation (**assumed**, but deliberately preserved rather than overridden across the project's own documents). *Why it matters:* the diagnosis should not be read as "100% of the gap is solvable by billing reform" — B4 addresses the structural/incentive-driven share of the gap, not the whole of it.

10. **Exact dates and durations of every known B4 instance are unrecorded.** No source specifies when any shortage or holiday relaxation began, ended, or how long it lasted. **Explicit gap.** *Why it matters:* without this, it is not possible to independently verify frequency, scale, or the vendor's actual demonstrated tolerance window — a real evidentiary limit on how far this precedent alone can carry a feasibility argument.

## Conclusion

The Breakfast Paradox is often framed, implicitly, as a problem the institution has never managed to solve — a gap between registration and attendance that persists because no one has found the right mechanism. The shock-adaptive precedent overturns that framing directly and specifically: not as a suggestion that a fix might exist somewhere, but as a confirmed, detailed, previously-run operating model — walk-in QR billing, short-notice mess-agnostic registration, zero billing for non-attendance, and a doubled cancellation cap — that the institution itself deployed successfully under real supply-side and calendar-driven stress, and then deliberately let lapse once the stress passed. This is the single fact that most changes the shape of the diagnosis: the paradox is not that the institution cannot align billing with attendance, but that it can, has, and chooses not to as a matter of standing policy, most plausibly to preserve the procurement lead-time certainty its outsourced vendor relationship depends on — a real, structurally coherent trade-off, not mere inertia, though one that has apparently never been explicitly weighed against the now-quantified cost of leaving the standing model as is. That failure to weigh the trade-off, rather than the absence of a technical solution, is what fragmented governance across menu, kitchen, academic scheduling, and billing policy actually explains: a proven fix sits inside the institution's own recent operating history, and no actor in the system currently holds both the authority and the incentive to ask whether it should be run more than rarely. Until that question is asked — and until the one fact that would settle it, the vendor's actual contract tolerance for permanent day-of demand variability, is checked rather than assumed — the Breakfast Paradox will keep reproducing itself not for lack of a known answer, but because the known answer has never been put back on the table.
