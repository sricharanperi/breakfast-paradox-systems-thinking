# Overview

This is the second of the two deliverables that come out of Activity 8. The first, the Reframed Systemic Problem Statement, diagnoses what the problem actually is. This one converts that diagnosis into a design opportunity, where in the system a change would actually do something, what any intervention has to respect, and what should explicitly not be attempted.

It stops short of designing the interventions themselves. That's Activity 9's job. What this document does is hand Activity 9 a shortlist that is already filtered against the evidence, along with the reasons certain obvious-looking directions have been ruled out, so that Phase 3 doesn't spend effort rediscovering them.

This version has been revised against the project's operations evidence: interviews with the three catering operators, the Yuktāhār operator's own books and registers, the institution's scan records for every Kadamba breakfast in April 2026, and a team member's first-hand account of both mess visits. That evidence confirms the core of the opportunity and overturns several of its earlier specifics, each of which is corrected where it appears below. A clearer account of the Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) manager's interview then confirmed the opportunity again from a third hall and revised three specifics: Bakul's first batch is a weekly judgement with a floor, not a fixed ratio raised from 70 to 80 percent; its manager places the main rush earlier than the closing crest seen at Kadamba; and its stated rule, that there should be no shortage even if some food is wasted, turns an implicit constraint of this document into an explicit one. It also points to three secondary opportunity areas: the quality of food as eaten after hot holding, demand by item, and the menu's pairings. There are no Bakul records of any kind; every Bakul figure below is the operator's own statement.

# 1. Where the Leverage Is, and Isn't

The reframe showed the problem living at the structure and mental-model layers of the Iceberg. That immediately tells us something about where intervening is worth the effort.

**Not at the event layer.** Persuading individual students to attend, reminders, nudges, campaigns, targets the most visible layer and the least movable one. A meaningful share of non-attendance is legitimate, and the rest is rational given the incentives. Changing behaviour without changing what produces it means fighting the structure permanently.

**Not at the parameter layer either**, which is where the institution does act when it acts at all. The cancellation cap has already moved from five to ten and back, and currently allows five cancellations per meal type per month, each made at least four days ahead (confirmed). That kind of change alters where the pressure escapes, into formal cancellation, informal resale, or silent no-shows, without altering whether the pressure exists, because the cap limits the exits from a default without changing the default. The same holds on the kitchen side. The parameter doing the most physical damage is each operator's first-batch ratio, the share of registrations cooked before service opens: about 70 percent at Kadamba, planned separately for the vegetarian and non-vegetarian lines (the operator's single-sourced statement), and 70 to 80 percent at Bakul, varying by item and set each week from past attendance, with a floor of about 70 percent even on low weekends (single-sourced), against a recorded breakfast turnout of 37 percent at Kadamba and a Bakul turnout its own manager puts at most at about 60 percent (single-sourced, no records). The same Kadamba kitchen cooks its biryani lunch at 100 percent of registrations (single-sourced), so the ratio is a per-meal choice, not a fixed property of the kitchen. Simply lowering it would move the cost from surplus into run-outs at close. What makes the ratio wrong is the missing feedback that would set it, which is an information-flow problem, not a parameter one. At Bakul some feedback already reaches the ratio every week, and the floor still holds it up, because the operator's goal puts avoiding a shortage ahead of avoiding waste (single-sourced); there the ratio is held by a goal as well as by thin feedback.

**At the structural layer:** what a registration is and what triggers a bill. Together with the missing route described at the next layer, these are the three structural causes the diagnosis names: registration as an automatic standing default, billing at the booking, and a gap that is measured but unrouted. What triggers institutional attention is the condition that keeps all three unaddressed, and it is taken up as an intervention point below. The first cause was missing from the earlier version of this document, and its description has since been revised again. A breakfast registration is not a daily decision to eat, and it is not a choice either. Every student is registered automatically, whether or not they are on campus. The only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month, each made at least four days ahead, and beyond that there is no way to undo the charge (confirmed). The earlier description, a booking the student makes and that closes about four days ahead, was revised to this: the booking is a default, and its exit is narrow and capped. At Kadamba in April 2026 registrations held flat at about 1,070 a day on every day of the week, Sunday included, while turnout moved from 42 percent on Tuesdays to 27 percent on Sundays (confirmed from scan records). A default fixed that far ahead cannot carry the morning's demand, whatever the billing rule does. Yuktāhār's breakfast registrations are not flat (228 to 404 a day in September 2026, confirmed from the operator's register), so the flat pattern is established for Kadamba only.

**At the information-flow layer, between those rules and the kitchen:** the gap is already measured, but the measurement travels nowhere. The institution's scan system records every breakfast eaten against its registration (the records in hand cover Kadamba). The Yuktāhār operator records turnout by weekday and cooked, eaten and wasted quantities per item. At Kadamba the supervisor watches the counters during service and tells the kitchen how much more to cook, and the kitchen keeps a gram standard for every dish (single-sourced), but no outcome record was seen there, and no recorded turnout history by weekday reaches the first-batch decision at Kadamba. At Bakul the manager reviews past attendance week by week before setting the batch (single-sourced), which revises the earlier statement that nothing reaches the off-site decision; whether that review is written down is not known, so the measurement may live in one person's head. Neither the scan records nor any operator's books reach whoever owns the registration, billing or menu rules. Routing what already exists is the highest-yield change available.

**And underneath that, at the mental-model layer:** at the institution, the belief that an aggregate registration count adequately stands in for demand, and that certainty days ahead, for registration and therefore for billing, is permanently worth more than a same-week demand signal rather than a trade-off worth re-making. The earlier version of this document framed the second belief as procurement certainty. That was revised by the operations evidence: the four-day lock applies to registration, while Kadamba buys dry goods a week ahead, most perishables, including meat, eggs and milk, the day before, and some vegetables two to three days ahead (the operator's single-sourced statement), and Yuktāhār takes vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily (confirmed). Only non-perishables are committed earlier than four days, so the kitchens' own ordering does not depend on the four-day lead (candidate). The binding physical constraint is the cooked first batch, not raw procurement: raw surplus is largely recoverable, cooked surplus is not (candidate). Two beliefs sit on the operator side. The first is that each operator's picture of breakfast turnout is private and uncorrected: the Yuktāhār site head describes breakfast turnout as at most 50 percent, while his own September register averages about 28 percent (confirmed). The Bakul manager's estimate of at most about 60 percent is another private picture, with no records to correct it (single-sourced). The second differs by operator. Kadamba treats the registration count as the obligation it is paid to meet, Yuktāhār treats waste as cost it can cut, and Bakul holds that there should not be a shortage of food even if some wastage occurs (all single-sourced). Changes at this layer are the hardest and the most durable. The encouraging detail is that this institution has already demonstrably suspended its two beliefs, during the gas shortage and during fest periods, so neither is as fixed as it looks.

# 2. The Design Opportunity Statement

> **The opportunity is to give this system a way to notice its own cost.**
>
> The Breakfast Paradox persists not because the fix is unknown, it has already been run twice, and not because the gap goes unmeasured. The gap is measured daily, in scan records and in the operators' own books. It persists because none of those measurements travels to anyone with the authority to change the rules that produce it. Every consequence is absorbed before it becomes visible: the resale market takes the financial loss, the operators' own discount, top-ups and leftover reuse take the day-to-day mismatch, the meals of outside diners and Kadamba's own staff, the walk-in buffer, service to late arrivals, food held and reheated into later service at a cost to quality, and finally the dustbin take the physical surplus, outside venues take the dissatisfaction, and informal workarounds take the cultural mismatch. The institution's attention triggers on complaint intensity, and this problem is quiet.
>
> The design opportunity is therefore not to build a new solution, and not to start measuring, but to build the missing routes: from the records that already exist to the kitchen decision that fixes how much is cooked, and from the same records upward to a decision about registration and billing. Four intervention points follow. One sits in the kitchen, at the moment a registration becomes a cooked quantity. Three sit in governance, at decreasing depth: the registration and billing rules, the ownership gap, and the attention mechanism. The last is the cheapest and the most under-considered, and it is the one that would have surfaced the CFS Chair's own figures years ago.

# 3. Validate the Reframing with Evidence: What the Opportunity Rests On

The opportunity rests on three claims, and each can now be checked against records rather than recall.

**The gap is large, breakfast-specific and not a fault of the platform.** Kadamba breakfast turnout in April 2026 was 37.1 percent, a no-show rate of 62.9 percent across 32,162 registrations (confirmed from scan records). Yuktāhār breakfast turnout in September 2026 ran between 18 and 40 percent, averaging about 28 percent, while lunch on the same days ran between 56 and 89 percent (confirmed from the operator's register). The same platform and the same operator produce very different turnout at the two meals, so the problem is breakfast's, not the platform's.

**A private conversion step turns an inflated registration count into physical surplus.** Each operator reads a registration count from the portal and converts it into a cooked quantity by its own rule. Yuktāhār's planning meeting looks up the matching week in its Wastage Book and its weekday attendance register (confirmed), weighs the month's turnout pattern and any holidays, special weeks or campus events, and then, during service, uses the first half hour as a sample to project the rest of the meal and decide top-ups (single-sourced). Kadamba cooks about 70 percent of registrations in the first batch, separately for each line, portions it by a gram standard per dish (four idlis a person, 100 grams of sambar), names bonda, puri, vada and idli as items diners take more of than the standard, and tops up within 10 to 15 minutes; its manager said of forecasting, "We don't have that idea... it was wasted many times" (single-sourced). Bakul's manager sets the first batch each week at 70 to 80 percent of registrations depending on the item, never below about 70 percent even on low weekends, checks food left against expected demand about every 30 minutes, and phones the Hafizpet kitchen, about 8 km away, when more is needed (single-sourced); the end-to-end lead time of a top-up is not known, and the drive alone takes about 20 minutes early in the morning and about 40 otherwise. The earlier reading of the same interview, a ratio raised from 70 to 80 percent with extra food within about 30 minutes, is revised: the two figures describe variation by item and day, not a change, and the 30-minute top-up is not used. On the manager's own figures Bakul's first batch and floor sit above the highest breakfast turnout he expects, so it exceeds turnout by design (candidate). Set against recorded turnout, Kadamba's first batch is roughly 1.9 times what is eaten before any top-up, about 750 portions against about 400 eaten (candidate). The operator puts its own waste at about 5 to 10 percent a day, with no stated base (single-sourced). As stated, the two do not add up. They are compatible only if most of the surplus is eaten by outside diners and staff, or held and reheated into later service, or offset by top-ups smaller than the ratio implies, or if the percentage is measured on a different base, and which of these closes the gap is not known. It matters for the opportunity: to the extent outside diners and reheating absorb the surplus, its cost is paid partly in food quality and partly in other people's meals, not in bin weight. Once cooked, a batch can be topped up but not shrunk, and cooked food stays usable for three to four hours (single-sourced).

**The measurement exists and is stranded.** The institution's scan export holds one row per registration with an availed flag and a timestamp (confirmed). Yuktāhār runs on a set of books, each seen and photographed (confirmed): a Wastage Book recording, per item, raw quantity issued, quantity cooked, quantity left over, percentage wasted, run-out time and re-cook quantity, alongside registered and eaten counts and a daily meal wastage defined as plate waste plus leftover; a weekday attendance register; a wastage board; grocery, store, stock and vegetable books; utility registers; and fridge logs. Its rule is that the book of what was issued must match the book of what was cooked, and any mismatch is raised at the daily meeting (single-sourced; a cross-check of one item across two books held, confirmed). Kadamba states a waste share of about 5 to 10 percent a day and keeps per-dish gram standards (single-sourced), but no outcome record was seen. For planning, operators take only the registration count from the portal, and exams and events reach Yuktāhār's kitchen only when someone raises them at the planning meeting: "Until we sit in the meeting, until we discuss that, we will never come to know" (confirmed). The CFS Chair can state the breakfast gap from memory. Bakul's manager carries a weekly reading of past attendance into each first batch, with no record known (single-sourced). Four holders, no route between them, and no route from any of them to a rule owner.

## Compare Stakeholder Perspectives: the Operator's View

The earlier diagnosis held that the vendor experiences nothing of the gap. The operations evidence revises that. The operators experience the gap every morning, as food cooked for students who do not come, as a crush at closing time that forces re-cooking and late arrivals who cannot be turned away, or at Bakul as a mid-window rush with a small late tail that it covers with food held for about 50 people at 9:20 (single-sourced), and as guesswork about the first batch. They are the actors with the most outcome data and the least rule authority: they decide how much to cook but not how registration works, how it is billed, or what is on the menu. The Kadamba team challenged the four-day registration close directly, and also called last-minute registrations a pain; the Bakul manager asked for a say in the menu, on pairings rather than dishes (single-sourced). Items the operators see wasted repeatedly, uttapam, set dosa and upma at Kadamba (single-sourced), have no route back to the menu decision.

The operators do not all read the gap the same way, and the earlier version of this section treated them as one. Yuktāhār's management states its goals as quality, less waste, lower operating cost and higher profit, so the gap reaches it as cost it already acts on (single-sourced). Kadamba's operator says it cooks on registrations because that is its responsibility and it is paid for it (single-sourced), so the gap reaches it as the institution's concern rather than its own. The same registration and billing rules produce opposite stances, which points to management culture and incentive as part of the opportunity space (candidate). Bakul adds a third stance: a shortage is the failure to avoid, and waste is the accepted price of avoiding it (single-sourced). For Yuktāhār the opportunity reads as a voice and a better input, not a new obligation. For Kadamba a better input alone may not be wanted. For Bakul it reads as a check on a judgement it already makes, acceptable only if it does not raise the chance of a run-out. Bakul's manager says his operator is paid on plates served, against Kadamba's remark about registrations (both single-sourced); the tension is recorded in section 7 and not resolved.

# 4. The Four Intervention Points

These are stated as opportunities, not designs. Activity 9 works out what each would concretely look like. The first acts closest to the physical waste; the other three act on the governance that sets its conditions. Three secondary opportunity areas, food quality as eaten and demand by item under 4.1 and the menu's pairings under 4.3, rank below the four and do not change their order.

## 4.1 On process: how a registration becomes a cooked quantity

The first batch is the point of no return. At Kadamba it is set by a fixed ratio that is not calibrated to breakfast turnout; at Bakul by the manager's weekly judgement from past attendance, bounded below by a floor of about 70 percent (single-sourced); at Yuktāhār from the kitchen's own books, anchored on a headline turnout belief well above its records. The earlier version grouped Bakul with Kadamba as a fixed ratio; that is revised. Once cooked, it cannot be reduced. The data that could calibrate it already exists: the institution's scan records by weekday (the records available cover Kadamba), and at Yuktāhār the operator's own books. Yuktāhār shows the loop can work in-house, and that it can work inside the current rules: the same automatic registration and billing apply there, and the operator still runs a before-service estimate and an in-service projection from its own records. Waste tracking started there in August of last year, staff first resisted it ("they are catching us"), the managers made the weighing blame-free and discussed it daily, and by December staff themselves reported that waste had fallen (single-sourced). Kadamba described no equivalent loop, and its manager said it has no forecasting method (single-sourced). Bakul described a weekly loop from past attendance, with no written record known and a floor it keeps even on low weekends (single-sourced).

The opportunity is to feed recorded turnout, by hall, by weekday and by line, into the decision that sets the first batch, the evening before or at the planning meeting, while keeping each kitchen's top-up path intact. At Kadamba this can plug into what the kitchen already uses: a per-person gram standard multiplied by expected turnout on each line in place of 70 percent of registrations, with its own allowance for the items diners take more of. Recorded April turnout differs by line, 32.4 percent vegetarian against 48.5 percent non-vegetarian (confirmed), so one factor would not serve both. An in-service projection of the kind Yuktāhār uses is useful but exposed: a first-half-hour sample will understate a breakfast that crests at close (candidate). At Bakul the opportunity is narrower and starts from what the manager already does: item-level records of what was cooked and eaten could let the weekly adjustment Bakul already makes be checked against outcomes, provided the chance of a shortage does not rise. This is a candidate cause of the physical-waste leg of the problem rather than a confirmed root cause, since the Kadamba ratio is the operator's own statement of its practice and Bakul's weekly rule is its manager's own account, with no records behind it.

**A secondary opportunity in the same process: the quality of food as eaten.** Bakul's food is cooked in the Hafizpet kitchen, with preparation of labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha starting around 3 AM for arrival around 7 AM, and held at about 60 to 72 °C in insulated boxes set in hot water (single-sourced). The chain is built to keep food hot, and it can lose texture on the way: paratha softens in its own steam (single-sourced). The food held longest is the reserve kept for late diners, so they likely get the softest of it (candidate). The opportunity is to count holding time and texture alongside temperature and taste wherever food quality is judged, and to treat any change that holds more food for longer, a bigger reserve or an earlier dispatch, as a trade against quality as well as against waste or shortage. Whether softer food changes what students eat or whether they come has no student evidence; it is an open question, not a loop.

**A secondary opportunity in the same process: demand by item, not by head.** Every kitchen already plans breakfast by item in some form: Kadamba by gram standard per dish, Yuktāhār per item in its Wastage Book, and Bakul with a ratio that changes by item, pav bhaji drawing more students and muesli and Chocos going easily (single-sourced). Average turnout hides that variation. The opportunity is for the item-level consumption each kitchen already sees to reach the first-batch decision and the menu decision in a form someone can check. Whether item-level patterns would predict better than a single ratio is a hypothesis to test, not a finding.

## 4.2 On the rule: what a registration is and what triggers a bill

The institution has already operated attendance-tracked billing successfully during the gas shortage and during holiday and fest periods such as Felicity. That precedent now sits beside a confirmed fact that narrows it: registration is otherwise automatic, including over ordinary holidays (confirmed). Which holiday periods ran without compulsory registration is therefore an open question, and the precedent may cover only shock and fest periods. Either way, the opportunity is a standing, partial version of a model the institution has already run, rather than a novel mechanism. It also already records consumption at the level such a rule would need: one scan per registration, with a hand-written ID fallback when the scanner fails (the fallback is single-sourced). The export held by the project carries no student ID, so whether scans are retained per student is a candidate, not a confirmed fact.

The rule has three parts that the earlier version of this document treated as one. The first is what a registration is: a default applied to every student, not a choice any student makes. The second is how a student leaves it: five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead, plus a Skip Meal toggle that the portal presents as telling the kitchen not to prepare but that does not remove the charge (confirmed). Whether a Skip Meal declaration actually lowers the count the kitchen cooks to is unresolved (section 5). Neither is needed that early for the operators' perishables. The third is what a registration costs: a charge fixed at registration rather than at eating. Changing the default or widening its exit would make registration carry more of the morning's demand; changing the billing trigger alone would change who bears a no-show without improving the count.

Breakfast specifically is the natural scope. It has the worst recorded turnout of any meal: about 37 percent at Kadamba in April 2026 and about 28 percent at Yuktāhār in September 2026, against 56 to 89 percent at Yuktāhār's lunch on the same September days (confirmed from records). The earlier version stated a range of 35 to 45 percent from the CFS Chair's recall; that was revised by the records, which support the lower end at Kadamba and fall below the range at Yuktāhār. There are no records yet for Bakul or Palash. The case for scoping to breakfast is not that the volume at risk is small, since Kadamba alone registers about 1,070 breakfasts a day of which about 670 go uneaten (confirmed). It is that lunch shows the same platform working, so the problem and any trial can be confined to the meal where it lives.

This opportunity is gated on how each operator's contract counts a plate, covered in section 7.

## 4.3 On decision rights: who owns the outcome

Decision rights over the registration-attendance outcome are split at least five ways. The Mess Office sets registration and cancellation rules (the operators call the institution's side the "CDS team"; whether that is the same body as the Mess Office is unconfirmed). The institution sets billing. Students and the Mess Committee choose the menu; the Bakul manager disputes some of the combinations students select and has no stated power to change them (single-sourced). Each operator privately decides how much to cook. The academic administration sets a calendar that reaches the kitchens only by word of mouth. No actor holds both the knowledge and the authority, and the operator is the sharpest instance: it holds the most outcome data and the least rule authority. That is the confirmed reason a proven fix was never picked back up.

At Kadamba the chain runs from CFS to CDS to Prism, the tendered vendor that runs all of Kadamba's operations, and down through Prism's operations manager, manager and supervisor to the service staff; the supervisor is the one who decides, within a meal, how much more to cook (single-sourced). Who heads CDS is itself unresolved: the project has been given two different names for that role, and whether they are two roles or one name is wrong is not yet known. The only standing oversight inside the kitchen, a facility team tasting every meal with two Prism staff, weekly quality inspection and daily water testing, covers safety and taste, not quantity (single-sourced). Even the actor already present at every meal has no remit over the gap, which sharpens the finding that it belongs to nobody. At Yuktāhār there is no hierarchy of this kind: two project managers, staff who share every job, and records cross-checked by peers (single-sourced).

The opportunity is a single named owner accountable for the booked, cooked and eaten outcome end to end. Not a new committee, the system already has escalation structures that demonstrably work when something reaches them. What's missing is an actor for whom this specific gap is their problem, since the diagnosis established that currently it is nobody's, and a standing route by which the operators' data and their view of which items waste reach that owner.

**A secondary opportunity in the same decision rights: the menu's pairings.** The Bakul manager says the problem with the breakfast menu is pairings rather than dishes: kachori, in his view, belongs with chole, and Raj Kachori with aloo curry rather than chana (single-sourced). He describes students choosing for balanced nutrition, protein and preference, and himself choosing from traditional combinations, taste and what people actually eat, from about 20 years with North Indian food (single-sourced). That the combinations reduce what is eaten, and that residents prefer familiar pairings, are his views and are untested with Bakul residents. The opportunity is for what operators see eaten and left, item by item and pairing by pairing, to reach the forum that sets the menu, and to be checked there against what residents say they want. It is a voice in the menu, not operator authority over students' choices.

## 4.4 On information flow: what gets noticed

The escalation design routes high-intensity feedback to a committee and low-intensity feedback to the CFS Head. Both branches respond to how loudly a problem is felt. Neither responds to how much it costs.

The opportunity is an escalation trigger that fires on measured cost alongside the existing one that fires on complaint volume. This is the cheapest of the four interventions by a wide margin and the one with the broadest reach, because it would also catch the next quiet-but-large problem, not just this one.

The earlier version of this document named a precondition: that waste was never measured by volume, only handled by category, so measurement had to come first. That was revised by the operations evidence. Waste and turnout are already measured, by the institution's scan system and by the operators' books, but locally, in non-standard formats, and without a route to anyone who owns registration, billing or menu. The unlocking step is routing and a shared definition, not new measurement. The definition matters: Yuktāhār's two registers disagree for the breakfast of 3 September 2026, 77 eaten in the Wastage Book against 110 in the attendance register (confirmed from records), so any cost trigger needs one agreed meaning of "ate". Yuktāhār already reconciles its books on the input side, issued against cooked, so an agreed definition of what was eaten extends a practice it already runs rather than imposing a new one. Records exist for two halls only.

# 5. What Any Intervention Must Respect

These are constraints the evidence imposes, not preferences.

**Do not remove an absorption mechanism to manufacture a signal.** Making resale harder, or ending anyone's access to leftovers, would make the problem more visible by making real people worse off. The absorption layer is revised from the earlier version, which named composting as a surplus route: the Kadamba team said surplus goes to a dustbin collected by a garbage contractor and that there is no composting there (single-sourced). This conflicts with the institution's own account of composting, and the two are not yet reconciled. The evidenced routes are Yuktāhār's reuse of leftovers (idli turned into idli upma or idli poha for lunch, batter refrigerated, surplus milk set as curd, confirmed); the 30 to 50 outside diners, such as night security staff who do not want to leave their post to eat, and about half of Kadamba's own staff, who all eat from the same food (single-sourced); the operators' top-ups; the 30 to 40 extra portions Kadamba prepares for walk-in and paying diners, which may be the same allocation as the portions for outside diners (candidate); service to late arrivals; and the bin. The figure for staff and outside diners revises the earlier one of 20 to 30 cooking and security staff. The reuse, the meals for staff and outside diners, and the top-ups are locally beneficial, students genuinely recover money through resale, the people eating the surplus are genuinely fed, and none of these should be removed. An intervention that shrinks the first batch takes food from those diners unless it provides for them by name. Two routes are not worth protecting. The bin benefits no one. Holding surplus and reheating it into later service, which the team judged to be Kadamba's main quality problem (candidate), absorbs surplus at the cost of what students eat. The signal the institution needs can be read from records it already holds. Any proposal that works by increasing someone's pain to create institutional visibility is the wrong kind of fix, and the diagnosis is what makes that identifiable in advance.

**Act before the first batch is cooked.** Cooked food is usable for three to four hours and a batch can be topped up but not shrunk (single-sourced, consistent with Yuktāhār's single-batch practice). Breakfast cooking starts at 5:30 at Kadamba and at Yuktāhār, and Yuktāhār's is ready by 7:30 (single-sourced). Bakul's first batch is decided earlier still: labour-heavy items can be started at the Hafizpet kitchen around 3 AM to arrive around 7 AM (single-sourced). At Kadamba the batch then passes a food-safety sequence before any diner arrives: a cooking temperature check, a sample kept for 72 hours, a wrapped display plate at the entrance (confirmed, observed), serving temperatures per item, and tasting with corrections (single-sourced). No same-morning turnout signal can reach that batch, so any change that reduces surplus has to act the evening before or at the planning meeting, and it must not trade any safety step for speed. Surplus is not inert once cooked: food held and reheated loses quality (candidate), so topping up instead of over-preparing protects quality as well as waste. Kadamba already cooks live items such as dosa in front of diners from 7:00 (single-sourced), a just-in-time element inside the current operation. Any change must keep a top-up path, and the top-up lag differs by hall: 10 to 15 minutes on site at Kadamba (single-sourced), and at Bakul a phone call, cooking and a drive of about 20 to 40 minutes whose total has not been measured (single-sourced). The earlier figure of about 30 minutes from the off-site kitchen is not used. No single ratio fits every hall.

**Reduce waste without raising the chance of a shortage.** The earlier version stated this constraint only as "do not trade waste for run-outs", tied to the late window at Kadamba. It is now explicit and applies to every hall, for two reasons. Students need food to be there when they come: the run-outs they describe, idli, puri and bhatura gone by 9:20 with promised restocks that do not arrive, are the failure they feel directly. And one operator states it as its rule: "There should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs," which is why Bakul keeps food for about 70 percent of registrations even on weekend mornings its manager calls very low (single-sourced). An opportunity that cuts waste by raising the chance of a shortage fails students, and an operator holding that rule is unlikely to adopt it (candidate). Where the risk concentrates differs by hall. Kadamba breakfast arrivals build steadily to a crest just before close: 26.5 percent of all April scans fell between 9:15 and 9:30, and on an average day the scan rate peaked at about nine a minute at 9:29 (confirmed from scan records). The operator's own rule of thumb agrees, about 150 diners in the last ten minutes of any meal (single-sourced), and Yuktāhār's site head describes the same rush at closing time (confirmed). The earlier version said all three operators describe it; that is revised, because the Bakul manager places his main rush at 8:00 to 8:30, with 10 to 15 people in the last ten minutes, and keeps food for about 50 people at 9:20 (single-sourced), a reserve that stands in for a top-up that cannot arrive late from a kitchen 8 km away (candidate). After 9:30, late arrivals come in through Kadamba's back door and cannot be refused (single-sourced). The closing crest is the one condition where surplus and shortage occur on the same morning, and it is also where any projection from the first half hour of service is weakest (candidate). Any opportunity that shrinks the first batch has to protect late-window service in the form each hall needs, or it moves the cost onto the students who arrive last.

**Do not treat turnout as the success metric.** A meaningful share of the gap is students eating the way they actually live, intermittent fasting, regional patterns, religious observance, and turnout varies by weekday while registrations do not. The Yuktāhār site head treats exam and event dips as something that "cannot be helped" (confirmed). Driving turnout toward a hundred percent would mean engineering away legitimate choices. The right measure is the gap between what is cooked and what is eaten, read together with run-out incidents, which Yuktāhār already logs per item, and with the quality of food as eaten, including how long it was held and its texture, not only its temperature; and whether that cost becomes visible and answerable, not whether the seats fill. Waste falling while run-outs rise is not success. Yuktāhār already steers by such a measure: its wastage board tracks the daily waste share against a target to bring it down (single-sourced), and its Wastage Book defines daily waste as plate waste plus leftover (confirmed).

**Do not treat the 8:30 class start as the lever.** The academic timetable almost certainly solves real problems of its own, faculty scheduling, room contention, sequencing. It should be treated as a fixed boundary condition the mess system works around, not a variable to pull. The scan records add a nuance: nearly 60 percent of Kadamba's April breakfast scans fall at or after 8:45, after classes have begun, so class timing is not the only driver of late arrival. The service-window end and the timing of batches sit inside the mess system and are available to Phase 3 without touching the timetable.

**Do not aim the fix at students.** They hold the least power and the most defensible behaviour in the entire system. Any intervention whose mechanism is "students should register more accurately" is asking the least-empowered actor to solve a structure they did not build, and given that registration is automatic, it is asking them to opt out of a default through an exit capped at five a month. The Kadamba manager's own account of no-shows, students who register "even if you are in the village" and let a friend eat in their place (single-sourced), mirrors the student-blame framing from the operator's side and corroborates the informal transfer of slots.

**Do not aim the fix at operators either.** Their ratios over-prepare, and they already run the system's only same-day balancing loop. Two reasons for the over-preparation are plausible. One is an asymmetry: a shortage at close is visible and complained about while a surplus produces nothing. The Bakul manager now states that asymmetry as his own rule, no shortage even if some food is wasted (single-sourced); whether what makes a shortage costly to an operator is a complaint, a deduction or a contract risk is not known, and the link to the system's response to loudness stays a candidate. The other is the Kadamba operator's own framing, that the registration count is what it is paid to cook for (candidate as a cause). An intervention that treats operators as the source of the waste would discard the actors who hold the data.

**Keep measurement blame-free.** Yuktāhār's waste measurement worked only after its managers made the weighing blame-free; before that, staff feared being singled out (single-sourced). Any new measurement, and any route that carries waste figures to a rule owner or a cost trigger, must not become a ground for deductions or penalties on the kitchen staff who record them, or the records will be defended rather than kept. This is a condition on both 4.3 and 4.4.

**Work with the records operators already keep.** Yuktāhār tried an enterprise software system to manage groceries and predict the rush and dropped it: the data was already in its books, the cost was not justified, and its staff are not digitally inclined, so office staff digitise records later (single-sourced). Any route has to start from paper books and the daily meeting, and must not depend on operators adopting a new digital system. At Bakul, where no records are known, a route would start from the manager's own weekly review, whatever form it takes.

**Preserve what already works.** The Student Council to Committee escalation pathway produced a real policy change on menu rotation, and the resulting two-week cycle, with weeks one and three identical and weeks two and four identical, is what makes Yuktāhār's matching-week lookup possible (confirmed from records). Yuktāhār's book routine, with staff buy-in, is a working in-house calibration loop. Each operator's top-up is the only same-day balancing loop in the system. Bakul's weekly adjustment from past attendance is a second working calibration, bounded by its floor (single-sourced). The earlier version stated as confirmed that Skip Meal declarations reach kitchen forecasting; that rests on the CFS side's account and is now revised to unresolved, because no operator named Skip Meal or cancellations as a planning input and all three plan from the portal's registration count. Kadamba's operator put the conflict more sharply: it cooks on registrations, whether or not people want to eat (single-sourced). The two accounts can both hold if Skip Meal lowers the count the operator reads and the operator then treats that count as its obligation (candidate), but neither account is yet preferred. None of these needs replacing: the escalation pathway needs a second trigger type, Skip Meal needs to be worth a student's time, and the kitchen loops need better inputs.

## Identify Contradictions and Tensions: What the Opportunity Must Hold

Several tensions run through the evidence, and the intervention points have to be designed with them in view, not around a guess at how they resolve. Kadamba's stated waste of 5 to 10 percent does not square with a first batch nearly twice what is eaten. Surplus that looks like waste is also the meal of outside diners and staff. The same billing rules produce an operator that cuts waste for profit, one that cooks to the count as its obligation, and one that accepts some waste to avoid a shortage. The kitchen that forecasts best projects from the first half hour, while at Kadamba the heaviest demand arrives in the last fifteen minutes. A reserve held for late diners protects them from a run-out and is also the food held longest, so the same hedge trades shortage against waste and against texture (candidate). A hot-holding chain keeps food at temperature and can soften it (single-sourced for aloo paratha). A menu chosen for nutrition and preference may not be the menu that gets eaten, in the Bakul manager's view, which is untested with residents. The safety sequence that protects diners also fixes the first batch before any turnout signal exists. And two accounts of Skip Meal, and two accounts of whether holidays were ever non-compulsory, remain unreconciled. Each of these is carried as open in section 7.

# 6. How the Four Interact

They are not alternatives, and the order matters.

The information-flow change (4.4) is the enabling one. Without a routed, agreed measure of cost there is nothing for an owner to act on and no evidence base for a rule change. It is also the one that needs no new data: the institution's side already holds the scan records and the operators already hold their books.

The process change (4.1) is the fastest to show a physical effect and draws on the same records. It can move without waiting on governance where the operator has a reason to: Yuktāhār's loop already runs without institutional involvement, driven by its own cost and profit goal and a blame-free routine. At Kadamba, whose operator treats the registration count as its paid obligation, the kitchen side may not move on its own, so 4.1 there is partly coupled to the plate-basis question in section 7. On its own it only reduces surplus within a registration system that still over-books.

The ownership change (4.3) is what converts a visible number into a decision. A signal with no addressee produces nothing, which is precisely the current state: the scan records, the operators' books and the CFS Chair's figures all exist and go nowhere.

The rule change (4.2) is the deepest and the one that actually closes the gap, but it is also the one gated on an external constraint. It is best understood as what the first three make possible, not as a standalone move.

A reasonable reading of this is that the sequence runs bottom-up in cost and top-down in impact: route the measurements already taken, calibrate the first batch to them, assign an owner, then change what a registration is, how a student leaves it and what it costs, with that last step scoped to breakfast and evaluated rather than assumed.

# 7. The Questions That Gate All of This

Two questions determine the shape of the deepest intervention point.

**What counts as a plate for payment.** Bakul's operator described winning a tender and being paid monthly as a lump sum on the number of plates, with deductions, and its manager says the plates counted are plates served (single-sourced); whether the other operators are paid the same way is not known. Kadamba's operator says it cooks to registrations because it is paid for them (single-sourced), which points to payment on registered plates there, but no contract term has been seen and the basis remains a candidate. The two statements pull in opposite directions. Contracts may differ by operator, "served" may be a normalized word for billed plates, or Kadamba's remark may be about duty rather than the payment formula; this is recorded as an open tension, not resolved, and one word from one operator is not enough to change the gate. Yuktāhār's profit framing would make sense under either basis. Whether "plates" means registered plates or plates actually served, and what the deductions are for, is not known. If operators are paid on registered plates, the no-show cost sits with students and the institution. Section 4.2 is then an adoption problem if the contract can absorb a consumption basis, since the institution would be formalising something it has already run, and a renegotiation of that specific term if it cannot, which is a different and harder piece of work. If operators are paid on served plates, part of the no-show cost already sits with the operator, and the incentive picture behind the over-preparation changes. For Bakul that would mean it already bears the cost of what it over-cooks and accepts it to avoid a shortage, and that a change to the breakfast default would not cut its revenue (both candidate).

**Who set the four-day lead and the five-cancellation cap, and what depends on them.** The kitchens buy perishables one to three days ahead and only dry goods a week ahead, and the Kadamba team has already challenged the four-day close. If nothing operational depends on four days, the lead is a rule held in place by habit or by billing convenience, and moving it, or widening the exit, is the nearest-term version of section 4.2. A shorter lead is not free for operators, though: Kadamba also finds last-minute registrations a pain (single-sourced).

Either way the other three intervention points stand unchanged, which is part of why they are worth pursuing first.

Other questions remain open and are carried forward:

- Is the portal count Kadamba cooks against already net of Skip Meal declarations and cancellations, given the operator's statement that it cooks on registrations?
- Who heads CDS? Two different names have been given for the role, and whether they are two roles or one name is wrong is not known.
- Does anyone with policy authority look at the scan-level records, and are they retained per student?
- Which holiday periods ran without compulsory registration, given that registration is otherwise automatic over holidays? This bears on June 2026 at Yuktāhār. Two Saturday breakfasts that month show 37 and 34 registered. The sheets write 89 and 91 percent eaten, but those totals include unregistered and paying diners; registered students alone numbered 22 and 21, about 60 percent (confirmed from records). If registration was non-compulsory then, these sheets suggest a smaller, closer-to-intent booking roughly doubles breakfast turnout, though still well below lunch. Automatic registration over holidays makes a non-compulsory June less likely, so this remains a candidate until the regime is confirmed.
- What is the base of Kadamba's 5 to 10 percent waste figure, and which route absorbs the gap between it and a first batch nearly twice what is eaten?
- Are the portions for outside diners the same allocation as the walk-in and paying portions, and is feeding outside diners and staff a written entitlement or an informal norm?
- How much Kadamba surplus is held and reheated into later service, and does the loss of quality feed skipping or complaints? This is an open chain, not an evidenced loop.
- Is over-preparation driven by an asymmetric penalty, shortage producing complaints or deductions while surplus produces nothing, or by operators treating the registration count as their paid obligation? One operator, Bakul, states the asymmetry as its rule; what makes a shortage costly to it is not known.
- Which account of disposal is current, Kadamba's dustbin or the institution's composting, and what is Yuktāhār's disposal route? At Bakul some leftovers are said to go back to the Hafizpet kitchen and the rest to be thrown (single-sourced, from an earlier reading of the same interview); how much goes each way is not known.
- Which definition of "ate" should a measured trigger use?
- Which body does the facility team that tastes each Kadamba meal belong to, and could its remit extend to quantity?
- How accurate is Yuktāhār's first-half-hour projection on breakfasts that crest at close?
- What do turnout and waste look like at Bakul and Palash, for which there are no records? At Bakul specifically: is anything in the weekly adjustment written down; how long does a top-up from the Hafizpet kitchen take end to end; is the reserve for about 50 at 9:20 set from data or judgement, and how much is left at close; which menu decisions can the manager override; and why do Bakul's own residents skip breakfast, asked of them rather than inferred from the manager's view?
- Which breakfast items other than aloo paratha lose quality in hot holding, and how often?

# 8. What Phase 3 Inherits

Activity 9 asks for leverage points and design principles. This document hands it four intervention points, one in the kitchen and three in governance already ranked by depth rather than by ease; a set of constraints that rule out whole categories of intervention before they are designed, including acting after the first batch is cooked, removing food from the outside diners and staff who eat the surplus, cutting waste in a way that raises the chance of a shortage, penalising the people who record waste, depending on a new digital system, and aiming the fix at students or at operators; three secondary opportunity areas, food quality as eaten, demand by item and the menu's pairings, stated as opportunities rather than designs; a stated sequencing logic; and two gating questions, the basis on which a plate is paid for and the reason for the four-day lead and the five-cancellation cap.

It also hands on the evidence limits. Record-level turnout exists for Kadamba breakfast in April 2026, Yuktāhār in September 2026 and two Yuktāhār Wastage Book sheets from June 2026 only. The Kadamba first-batch ratio and gram standards are the operator's own statements, heard directly by the team, and carry self-presentation bias. Bakul's conversion rule, floor, timings and payment statement are its manager's own account, taken from a team member's synthesis of the recordings, with no Bakul records of any kind; the other contract figures come from noisy recordings. Kadamba's only waste figure is a share with no stated base; earlier kilogram figures were withdrawn and are not used. Yuktāhār's per-item waste is in its own books. None of these should become headline numbers. Each hall was visited once, and the fullest account of both visits is a team member's synthesis of what operators said and showed, not a verbatim transcript.

The single most useful thing carried forward is the negative result, revised by the operations evidence: the reason this system has not fixed itself is not that anyone lacks capability, willingness or information. The system is not short of information; it is short of routes for it. The gap is measured every morning and absorbed every morning, and nothing carries that real, known cost to the kitchen decision that fixes the first batch or to the actors who set the rules. Designing those routes is the work.
