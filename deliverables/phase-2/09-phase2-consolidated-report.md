# Introduction

Phase 2 of this project covers three activities: building a System Map of the breakfast mess system, analyzing its causal loops and feedback mechanisms, identifying the systemic patterns and root causes behind the registration-attendance gap, and reframing the original operational question into a systemic problem statement with a matching design opportunity. This report condenses all five Phase 2 deliverables into one shorter document. The full version of each, with every table, every diagram, and every respondent quotation, is filed separately.

All five were revised three times against the project's operations evidence. The first revision drew on interviews with the three catering operators who run Yuktāhār, Kadamba and Bakul, Yuktāhār's photographed registers for September 2026, and the institution's scan record of every Kadamba breakfast registration in April 2026. The second drew on a team member's first-hand account of the Kadamba and Yuktāhār visits, which corrected several figures heard earlier through machine translation. The third drew on a clearer account of the Bakul manager's interview, replacing a machine-translated reading of it. Claims are marked confirmed, single-sourced, candidate or assumed. Figures read off a record, and anything the team saw on site, are confirmed. Kadamba operator statements that the team member heard directly are single-sourced: the figure is not in doubt, but it remains the operator's own claim. Bakul statements are single-sourced, and no Bakul records of any kind exist.

Two findings thread through all five documents. The institution has already run a working alternative, where billing tracks who actually shows up rather than who registered, twice, and switched it off both times once the trigger passed. And the gap is not invisible: it is measured every day, in the institution's scan records and in the operators' own books, and acted on inside the kitchen, but nothing carries that measurement to anyone who sets the rules.

# 1. System Map

Students are the registrant, the billed party, and the one eating. Campus Facilities Services sits above Campus Dining Services, which runs four dining halls, Kadamba, Bakul, Palash and Yuktāhār. Catering is outsourced by tender. Prism, a vendor that wins the Kadamba contract by tender, runs all Kadamba operations under a chain from CFS to CDS to Prism, then an operations manager, a manager, a supervisor and the service staff (single-sourced). Two different people are on record as the head of CDS, and whether they hold two roles or one name is wrong is open. ABC Hospitality Services runs Yuktāhār, a vegetarian mess with Jain service, through two project managers and a flat staff with no hierarchy and peer cross-checking. Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) is cooked for at the Hafizpet kitchen, about 8 km and 20 to 40 minutes away (single-sourced), revising an earlier candidate match; that Palash shares the kitchen is a candidate. An earlier version of this map recorded Kadamba's operator only as a floor team calling itself the Prism team; that has been revised. Suppliers, a garbage contractor and the institution's site team sit on the boundary. Academic administration sets the class schedule and has no formal role in mess governance at all.

Registration is an automatic default. Every student is registered, on campus or not and over holidays, and the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead, with no way to nullify the charge beyond those (confirmed). An earlier version described a standing booking taken in weekly blocks; that has been revised, and whether the operators' weekly description and the students' monthly one are the same rule is unconfirmed. The four-day lock applies to registration. Kadamba buys dry goods a week ahead, some vegetables two to three days ahead and perishables the day before (single-sourced), so the lock is longer than any perishable lead time. Kadamba's breakfast bookings held flat at about 1,070 a day on every day of the week in April 2026, while turnout moved from 42.1 percent on Tuesdays to 27.3 percent on Sundays; across the month 37.1 percent of bookings were eaten and 62.9 percent were not (confirmed). Yuktāhār's breakfast averaged about 28 percent in September against 56 to 89 percent for lunch on the same days (confirmed).

Kadamba cooks a first batch of 70 percent of registrations, planned separately for veg and non-veg, portioned by a gram standard for every dish, and its supervisor calls top-ups from what is left of each item at the counter, ready in 10 to 15 minutes (single-sourced). Bakul cooks for 70 to 80 percent of registrations, by item and day, from the manager's weekly review of past attendance, keeps food for about 70 percent even on very low weekends, and tops up by phoning the Hafizpet kitchen, lead time unknown (single-sourced); an earlier reading of a ratio raised from 70 to 80 percent is withdrawn. Yuktāhār uses no fixed ratio: a twice-daily meeting plans from the Wastage Book entry for the matching week of the two-week menu cycle, a weekday attendance register, the month's turnout pattern and any holidays or events, and during service it projects the rest of the meal from the first half hour (books confirmed; projection single-sourced). Its site head still believes breakfast runs at about 50 percent against the 28 his register shows. A cooked batch can be topped up but never shrunk, and at Kadamba arrivals crest at close: 26.5 percent of all April scans fell between 9:15 and 9:30, about 79 a day in the last ten minutes, and about 37 a day came at or after 9:30, when late-comers enter by the back door and cannot be refused (records confirmed; back door single-sourced). Curves differ by hall: the Bakul manager places his rush at 8:00 to 8:30, with 10 to 15 people in the last ten minutes, and keeps food for about 50 at 9:20 (single-sourced), a reserve standing in for a top-up that cannot arrive late (candidate). At Kadamba every meal passes a food-safety sequence before service, cooking temperature, a 72-hour sample, a wrapped display plate at the entrance, serving temperature and tasting by a facility team with two Prism staff; the plate and tasting are confirmed as observed. That oversight covers safety and taste, never quantity, and it fixes the first batch before any diner arrives. Surplus leaves through reuse at Yuktāhār, about 30 portions for 30 to 50 outside diners such as night security and about half of Kadamba's staff eating from the same food, food held and reheated into later service (candidate), the walk-in buffer and late service, and the bin; Kadamba's waste goes to a garbage contractor and is not composted (single-sourced). An earlier version gave the extra diners as 20 to 30 staff; that has been revised.

# 2. Causal Loop and Feedback Analysis

A causal loop only exists if the last variable in a chain actually causes the first one and that last link is evidenced. Eighteen mechanisms were tested, numbered L1 to L17 plus L3b, one scheme shared by every Phase 2 document. Three close with every link evidenced, all balancing: menu rotation governance (L4), Yuktāhār's Wastage Book learning across matching menu weeks (L11, confirmed at Yuktāhār only), and counter top-up within a meal (L12, which corrects only upward). Bakul's weekly review is a candidate second instance of L11, bounded by its floor, and its L12 waits on a phoned order driven 8 km. Yuktāhār's first-half-hour projection is a second, forward-looking form of L12; its sample comes from the calm start of service and will under-read a crowd that crests at close (candidate). Five are candidate loops whose closing link is assumed: resale (L1), Kadamba lock-in (L3b), waste absorption (L9), the soft close and back-door entry (L15), and kitchen competence shifting the burden (L17). Six are open chains, led by the core chain of the paradox, L13, in which an automatic booking keeps the count flat and a fixed ratio makes over-preparation worst on the days and the veg line where turnout is lowest. An unnumbered branch of L13 runs from surplus to food held, reheated and moved between vessels to lower quality, in the team's own judgement; the link back to skipping is not evidenced, so it stays an open chain. Bakul adds a second: food held hot for hours softens (single-sourced), with no evidence it moves turnout. The shock-adaptive exception (L6) is a calendar switch, not a loop. Skip Meal (L2) does not close, and whether it reaches Kadamba's kitchen is now in conflict: an earlier finding says declarations reach the kitchen, while Kadamba's operator says it cooks on registrations only. Capacity redistribution (L3) is contraindicated, vendor quality discipline (L8) is unresolved until the plate basis is known, operator waste to menu choice (L16) is absent, and the measured-but-unrouted gap (L10) is a static condition.

The split is itself the finding. The system is not short of loops or of measurement. Its working loops all act on the kitchen's conversion of bookings into food, and none of them touches the booking.

# 3. Systemic Problem Analysis

Seven chains were traced from a concrete event down to the belief holding it in place: registering and not showing up, food running out before close, complaints ignored until something forces a response, a working fix switched off, a menu rotation that worked and cast a shadow, a real share of the gap that is legitimate variation, and a kitchen that cooks to a guess and absorbs the difference.

Three structural causes recur. **Registration is an automatic standing default** with a narrow, early exit, so it cannot follow the morning decision to eat, which is why the problem is breakfast's while lunch mostly works. **Billing fires at the booking, not at consumption**, which makes an unused booking costless to the system; when billing followed attendance during the shortage and holidays, the no-show problem eased by the CFS Chair's account. **The gap is measured but unrouted**: menu, kitchen quantity, calendar, registration and billing sit with different owners, neither the scan records nor the operators' books reach anyone who sets the rules, and the one oversight line that reaches the kitchen at every meal checks safety and taste, never quantity. The operator is the data-rich, authority-poor actor. Two conditions keep all three unaddressed: an escalation design that responds to complaint intensity rather than cost, and an absorption layer that takes each consequence before it becomes a signal. The Bakul evidence leaves the causes unchanged and gives the first condition operator-side evidence: a Bakul shortage has a route to the managing director while a surplus is silent, and its stated rule is "there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs" (single-sourced); that the rule answers loudness is a candidate. Beneath the kitchen half sits one intermediate mechanism, the conversion ratio, in three regimes: a fixed first batch at Kadamba, the Bakul manager's weekly judgement down to a floor of about 70 percent, and Yuktāhār's book-based lookup that still anchors on a belief above its records, and in every kitchen a batch that can be corrected only upward. This revises an earlier grouping of Bakul with Kadamba.

The two on-site operators run under the same booking and presumably the same billing basis, yet hold opposite stances. Kadamba cooks on registrations "because it is a responsibility and they are getting paid for it"; Yuktāhār states lower cost and higher profit as goals and made its waste weighing blame-free before it worked (both single-sourced). Bakul holds a third, shortage first (single-sourced). The breakfast gap is about as large under both, which places the cause in the booking; operator culture decides only how much of the gap becomes surplus (candidate).

The sharpest tensions follow: individual sense against total cost, booking certainty against demand accuracy, operator knowledge against operator authority, the first batch against the late crest, Kadamba's stated 5 to 10 percent daily waste against a first batch near twice turnout, which is left unresolved, food safety against food held and reheated, cutting surplus against feeding the outside diners and staff who eat it, shortage against waste, temperature against texture, nutrition against familiar pairings, and one billing basis against three operator stances.

# 4. Reframed Systemic Problem Statement

> The Breakfast Paradox is a stable equilibrium. The system holds the gap between registration and attendance in place because every actor's behavior inside it is individually reasonable, and every consequence it produces gets absorbed before it can reach anyone who sets the rules.
>
> Three structures hold it. Breakfast is booked by default: every student is registered automatically, including when away and over holidays, and can leave only through five cancellations per meal type per month, each at least four days ahead, so the booking cannot follow the morning. Billing fires at that booking rather than at consumption, with no way to nullify the charge beyond the cancellations. And the gap is measured twice, in the CDS scan record and in the operator's own books, and neither is routed to the people who set registration, billing and menu rules, whose decision rights are split so that none of them holds both the knowledge and the authority to act.
>
> What follows gets quietly absorbed: resale takes the student's loss; the operator's discount, top-ups, reuse, the outside diners and staff who eat from the same food, the walk-in buffer and late-door service take most of the physical surplus, holding and reheating turns some of the rest into lower quality, and what remains leaves in the bin; outside venues take the dissatisfaction, and informal workarounds take the cultural mismatch. Most of these are genuinely useful, and none of the useful ones should be removed.

Five assumptions behind the original question do not survive the evidence. Non-attendance is not uniformly a problem. Students are not careless, and most are not choosing a booking at all. The fix is not unknown. The gap is not unmeasured: it is measured three ways and never reconciled. And it is not one problem. The statement was revised three times. The operations evidence overturned the claims that composting took the surplus and that the vendor felt nothing. The first-hand account then revised the booking from a block students choose to a default they are assigned, the extra diners from 20 to 30 staff to 30 to 50 outside people plus about half of Kadamba's staff, and the operators from one actor to two with opposite stances: one measures the gap per item and per weekday with no authority over any rule that produces it, the other cooks to the registration count as its paid obligation. The Bakul account then confirmed the hall, withdrew the 70 to 80 percent ratio change in favour of a weekly judgement, and made the stances three; the framing held, with Bakul a third hall showing the same pattern.

# 5. Design Opportunity Statement

> The opportunity is to give this system a way to notice its own cost. The gap is measured daily; it persists because none of those measurements travels to anyone with the authority to change the rules that produce it. The work is to build the missing routes, not to start measuring.

Four intervention points follow. **On process**, feed recorded turnout by hall, weekday and line into the decision that sets the first batch, the evening before or at the planning meeting, keeping each kitchen's top-up path; at Kadamba that can build on the gram standards it already uses. **On the rule**, a standing, partial, breakfast-scoped version of the attendance-tracked model already run twice, treating what a registration is, how a student leaves it and what it costs as three separate levers. **On decision rights**, one named owner of the booked, cooked and eaten outcome, with a standing route for the operators' data. **On information flow**, a trigger that fires on measured cost alongside complaint volume, which needs routing and one agreed definition of "ate", not new measurement.

Any intervention must respect what the evidence rules out: do not remove an absorption mechanism to manufacture a signal, and plan explicitly for the outside diners and staff who eat the surplus; act before the first batch is cooked and never trade a safety step for speed; reduce waste without raising the chance of a shortage, which Bakul states as its own rule, protecting late service in the form each hall needs; do not treat turnout as the success metric; keep any new measurement blame-free; work from the paper books operators already keep; do not treat the 8:30 class start as the lever; and do not aim the fix at students or at operators.

Three secondary opportunity areas rank below the four points, with no solution named: food quality as eaten, counting holding time and texture; demand by item rather than by head; and the menu's pairings, where what operators see eaten would reach the menu forum and be checked against residents, a voice rather than authority.

# What Carries Forward

The single most consequential open question is what a plate counts for the operator's monthly payment, booked or served. It decides who bears the cost of a no-show, whether L8 could close, and whether the deepest intervention point is an adoption problem or a renegotiation; Kadamba's "paid for it" is a hint toward booked plates, not a contract term, while the Bakul manager says plates served (single-sourced); the tension is flagged, not resolved, and no payment lever is released until each operator's basis is confirmed. The second is who set the four-day registration close and the five-cancellation cap, and what, if anything, depends on them. Three conflicts are stated rather than merged: whether Skip Meal declarations lower the count Kadamba cooks against, who heads CDS, and whether the Yuktāhār Wastage Book keeper named in the account is the person named by a form of address in the recording. Smaller items carry forward: the base of Kadamba's 5 to 10 percent waste figure; whether the outside-diner portions and the walk-in buffer are one allocation; direct observation of holding and reheating; how well Yuktāhār's projection reads the closing crest; where the garbage contractor takes Kadamba's waste; Palash's kitchen; whether Bakul's weekly review is written down, and its top-up lead time; Bakul scans and lunch, dinner and other-hall records; and interviews with CDS and the facility team. Bakul figures have no record behind them, and the Kadamba kilogram waste figures heard in translation are withdrawn; none of these should become headline numbers.
