\section{Overview}\label{overview}

This map asks what pattern emerges when the process repeats, across people and across time. It rests on four kinds of evidence. The first is six student respondents describing their own breakfast habits. The second is two sets of records: thirty days of Kadamba breakfast registrations and timestamped scans from April 2026, and twenty days of Yuktāhār breakfast and lunch attendance registers from September 2026, photographed on site with the rest of Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s daily books. The third is interviews with the operators of three messes (Yuktāhār, Kadamba, and Bakul, run by Vijayalakshmi Caterers). The fourth is a first-hand account by a team member present at both the Kadamba and Yuktāhār operator conversations. Numbers read off records are confirmed, and so is practice the team saw on site. Kadamba operator numbers, heard directly, are the operator\textquotesingle s single-sourced statements about his practice. Bakul numbers come from a team member\textquotesingle s structured synthesis of the recordings and are the manager\textquotesingle s own statements and estimates, since no Bakul records of any kind exist. No Bakul figure, and not Kadamba\textquotesingle s waste percentage, whose base is unstated, is used as a headline figure.

The timescales the evidence supports are these.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Within one morning:} measured. Every April scan at Kadamba carries a timestamp, the operator\textquotesingle s rule of thumb has the same shape as the records, and both kitchens described their morning from the 5:30 start to the close.
\item
  \textbf{Across a week:} measured. Kadamba registrations and turnout are recorded for every weekday and the Yuktāhār register covers nearly three weeks. Kadamba\textquotesingle s weekly quality inspection and one-week dry-goods purchasing run on the same cycle, and the Bakul manager reviews attendance weekly to set the next first batch (both single-sourced).
\item
  \textbf{Across a fortnight:} confirmed. The menu repeats on a two-week cycle and Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s waste records are organised around it.
\item
  \textbf{Across a month:} measured for Kadamba breakfast in April. Students are registered automatically and can cancel only five meals per meal type per month (confirmed). Yuktāhār reads the month\textquotesingle s turnout pattern when it forecasts (single-sourced).
\item
  \textbf{Across a semester:} recall only. Six respondents each give a semester-level trend for themselves, and no multi-month records exist yet.
\item
  \textbf{Across an operator year:} single-sourced for Yuktāhār in outline. The current operator took over the previous year, began per-item waste weighing in August on deliberately blame-free terms, and by December heard staff report lower waste. The switch to rock salt and cold-pressed oil dates from about six months before the visit, and software was tried and dropped at an unknown date.
\end{itemize}

No student records span multiple semesters, and no trend at that scale is claimed.

Bakul runs a longer morning, because its breakfast is cooked at Hafizpet, about 8 km away, and driven in. Every step is the manager\textquotesingle s description (single-sourced), and no Bakul record exists to time any of it. Labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha start about 3 AM and are dispatched in insulated boxes at about 60 to 72 °C, in about 20 minutes early in the morning (40 normally). They arrive about 7 AM and wait in a hot-water setup. Service peaks at about 8:00 to 8:30. About every 30 minutes the manager compares food left with registrations and expected demand and, if short, phones Hafizpet, which cooks and sends more (cooking plus 20 to 40 minutes on the road; lead time not stated). Around 9:20 he keeps food for about 50 people, with about 10 to 15 arriving in the last ten minutes. For labour-heavy items the timeline starts about four hours before the food reaches Bakul, and a top-up crosses the same road as the first batch. Hot parathas steam in the box and soften, so the food keeps its temperature and loses texture over the hours it is held (single-sourced; other items unknown).

The records add two features recall lacked. The curve opens with a burst: the first fifteen minutes after 7:30 carry more diners than any later slot until 9:00. It then runs as a flat plateau from 7:45 to 9:00 before climbing steeply, to about nine scans a minute in the final minute before 9:30. And roughly one scan in eleven falls after the nominal close, 1,100 across the month, most before 9:35, the latest at 10:07. The operator says latecomers come in by the back door once the front door closes and the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced), which the records match. The effective close is 9:35 to 9:45, set informally at the door. No bulk marking appears: never more than two scans share a second.

Kadamba\textquotesingle s rule of thumb is about 100 diners in the opening phase, about 100 through the middle and about 150 in the last ten minutes, "for any meal the highest flow is in the last 10 minutes" (single-sourced). That has the shape of the records. The records put the last ten minutes before 9:30 at about 79 scans a day, about 115 counting the minutes just after close, of about 398 a day in total. The rule slightly overstates the final surge and understates the total. The records remain the measurement.

The closing crest is recorded at Kadamba and described at Yuktāhār, where the site head says the rush always comes at closing and staff check the counter every thirty minutes from about forty-five minutes before (single-sourced). It is not described at every hall. Bakul\textquotesingle s manager puts the main breakfast rush at about 8:00 to 8:30, with a small late tail of about 10 to 15 in the last ten minutes, and keeps food for about 50 around 9:20 to cover it (single-sourced; his description, not a measured curve). (Revised: a noisy-audio reading placed the Bakul peak at 8:30 to 9:00 and later, arriving in waves, and that is withdrawn, as is the earlier claim that the closing crest is not particular to Kadamba.) Arrival curves differ by hall, and the Kadamba profile cannot be carried to another hall without that hall\textquotesingle s own scans (candidate).

The two timelines meet badly at one point. Yuktāhār projects the rest of the meal from its first half hour. On the Kadamba curve, the first half hour carries about 15\% of the morning\textquotesingle s diners and the last half hour about 40\% (confirmed for Kadamba). If Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s curve has the same shape, a straight projection from the first half hour reads low and pushes top-ups late, toward the crest (candidate; Yuktāhār keeps no arrival timestamps).

Zero of six respondents who addressed Skip Meal have used it, and the respondent who explained why did so in exactly the terms the incentive analysis predicts: it does not return the money. Four of six have used Mess Cell in some form.

The informal resale market is a symptomatic relief valve. It makes the underlying problem, billing that does not track attendance, tolerable enough that nobody is forced to fix the cause. Every link is directly evidenced except the last, that relief reduces pressure to fix billing, which is an inference no respondent stated. \textbf{B1: Kitchen waste control} (balancing; revised). The first reading was that because no respondent uses Skip Meal, the kitchen-side balancing loop might not close at all. Kitchen waste is balanced by the operator, not by Skip Meal, and each mess runs its own balancing loop with a different delay.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Yuktāhār} runs two balancing channels. The first sizes each item from registrations, the Wastage Book page for the matching day two weeks earlier, the weekday register and the month\textquotesingle s turnout, in a meeting held twice a day. Having seen 18.7\% breakfast attendance on Sunday 6 September, the site head said the quantity would be cut "not next month, next week" (confirmed). Its delay is two weeks and it acts on surplus as well as shortage. The second works within the meal: the first half hour projects the rest (single-sourced), and items are re-cooked or not by a written run-out-time rule (confirmed). Its delay is minutes and it acts upward only.
\item
  \textbf{Kadamba} cooks 70\% of registrations, veg and non-veg separately, by gram standard. The supervisor watches the counters and orders top-ups in ten to fifteen minutes (single-sourced). The delay is minutes, and nothing recorded shrinks the next first batch.
\item
  \textbf{Bakul} also runs two channels. The manager\textquotesingle s weekly review of attendance history sets 70 to 80\% by item and day, but on his account not below about 70\%, which he keeps even on low weekends under his rule that "there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs" (single-sourced). Its delay is about a week and it acts on surplus only down to that floor. The second channel is the 30-minute check and phone top-up, upward only, with cooking plus a 20 to 40 minute drive in its delay and the lead time unstated.
\end{itemize}

Whether Skip Meal reaches Kadamba\textquotesingle s kitchen at all is now an open conflict. The earlier finding was that Skip Meal declarations reach the kitchen for same-week preparation. The operator\textquotesingle s account is that the kitchen cooks for the number registered, "because it is a responsibility and they are getting paid for it" (single-sourced). The two fit together only if Skip Meal lowers the registration count the kitchen sees, which is unconfirmed.

\textbf{B2: Capacity redistribution} (weak or broken). One respondent registers for the whole month specifically because "Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full," direct evidence of scarcity-driven demand concentration on the popular mess, not organic redistribution to Bakul or Palash.

\textbf{Same meal, two failures} (open chain; revised). The first candidate was that preparation might be calibrated to the low attendance number, leaving real attendees under-served. That does not hold. At the aggregate, preparation runs well above turnout: Kadamba\textquotesingle s first batch is 70\% of registrations, about 750 portions a day in April, while 37.1\% of registrants ate, about 398 a day, so the first batch alone is roughly double what is eaten before any top-up (the 70\% is single-sourced and the doubling a candidate inference against the confirmed 37.1\%). Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s head believes about 50\% where his register averages about 28\%. What survives is that real attendees do meet run-outs, driven by timing, not volume. A quarter of Kadamba\textquotesingle s diners arrive in the last fifteen minutes, Yuktāhār cooks mostly in one batch, specific items draw longer queues, and the kitchen responds by re-cooking or serving latecomers at the back door. At Bakul the first batch exceeds turnout by design on the manager\textquotesingle s own numbers, and the gap is larger if Bakul resembles the two recorded halls (candidate).

Kadamba\textquotesingle s operator puts waste at about 5 to 10\% a day, without saying whether per meal or per day, by weight, or plate waste or kitchen leftover (single-sourced). That sits in tension with a first batch about twice what is eaten. Either the surplus is absorbed before it is counted, by 30 to 50 outside diners, about half the staff, reheating into later service and smaller top-ups, or the percentage uses a different base. The tension is recorded, not resolved. Over-preparation in total and shortage at the crest both come from fixed-ratio batch cooking against a curve that crests at close. No evidence links run-outs back to how students register, and none links reheated food to students skipping, so this is an open chain, not a loop.

\textbf{Recurring pattern: the weekly rhythm.} Turnout falls every Sunday at both messes with records (Kadamba 25 to 33\%, Yuktāhār 18 to 24\%) while registrations stay put. The system cycles weekly and registration does not cycle with it (confirmed). Bakul\textquotesingle s manager describes weekend breakfast as very low too, a third hall showing the pattern on his description alone, and he still keeps food for about 70\% of registrations on those days (single-sourced).

\textbf{Recurring pattern: three kitchens, three forecasting regimes} (revised from two). Kadamba prepares a fixed ratio and reacts at the counter, with no record carrying one morning\textquotesingle s surplus into the next first batch. Yuktāhār learns from its books across a two-week cycle and projects within the meal from its first half hour. Facing the same automatic registration, they state opposite stances: Kadamba cooks for registrations because that is what it is paid for, and Yuktāhār names lower cost and higher profit as goals alongside quality (candidate reading of the difference). Both are exposed to the closing crest, Kadamba through top-ups under time pressure and Yuktāhār through a projection window that sees only about 15\% of a morning\textquotesingle s diners on the measured curve. Bakul sits between them: its manager adjusts the share cooked, by item and day, from weekly attendance history, a calibration by judgement with no known record and a floor of about 70\% (single-sourced). Its stance is a third, stated outright: a shortage is the failure to avoid and some waste its price. He also says payment is on plates served, in tension with Kadamba\textquotesingle s remark about being paid to cook for registrations. Contracts may differ, and the basis is flagged here, not resolved. Bakul\textquotesingle s manager describes an earlier rush rather than a closing crest, so its late exposure is carried by the held reserve, not by top-ups (candidate).

\textbf{Recurring pattern: operator beliefs drift from operator records.} (Revised: Kadamba\textquotesingle s "300 to 400 by 8:30" came from a noisy recording and is superseded by his rule of about 100, 100 and 150 in the last ten minutes, which has the records\textquotesingle{} shape.) At Kadamba the belief about timing is close to the records, and the gap is in the level of turnout. Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s site head believes breakfast turnout is at most about 50\% beside his own register\textquotesingle s 18 to 40\%. The Bakul operator says Kadamba sees 90 to 95\% (single-sourced) against 37\% in Kadamba\textquotesingle s April records. Bakul\textquotesingle s manager estimates his own turnout at most about 60\% (single-sourced), and if Bakul resembles the two recorded halls at 28 to 37\%, that is an upper bound and a further instance of the pattern (candidate). The numbers are confirmed from records. The narrower pattern, that operators misjudge the level of breakfast turnout more than its timing, is a candidate.

\section{Key Findings}\label{key-findings}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{No single trend exists across individuals.} Respondents move in different directions at once. At the aggregate, Kadamba breakfast turnout was stable at 35 to 39\% in every week of April, with a Sunday trough that recurs weekly. Any claim that the paradox is getting worse or better needs multi-month records, not recall.
\item
  \textbf{The within-morning crowd is measured, not inferred.} It opens with a burst at 7:30, runs flat until 9:00, then climbs steeply: 40\% of diners arrive in the last half hour, a quarter in the last fifteen minutes, and about 9\% after the nominal close, many by the back door. Kadamba\textquotesingle s rule of thumb has the same shape. The curve is Kadamba\textquotesingle s: Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s head describes the same closing rush, but Bakul\textquotesingle s manager describes a main rush at about 8:00 to 8:30 with a small late tail (single-sourced; no Bakul records), so arrival curves differ by hall.
\item
  \textbf{Skip Meal\textquotesingle s student-side use is zero, but kitchen waste is balanced anyway,} by operator rules with delays from minutes to two weeks. Yuktāhār runs a two-week lookup against its books, which acts on surplus as well as shortage, and a within-meal projection that acts upward only and sees about 15\% of a morning\textquotesingle s diners if its curve matches Kadamba\textquotesingle s (candidate). Kadamba acts within minutes against shortage only. Bakul adjusts weekly by judgement, acting on surplus only down to about 70\%, and tops up by phone upward only (single-sourced). None is calibrated to actual breakfast turnout.
\item
  \textbf{The same breakfast fails in two directions at once:} too much food in total (candidate, from the single-sourced first-batch ratio against the confirmed 37.1\%) and item-level run-outs at the closing crest. Both come from a fixed first batch meeting a curve that crests at close. Part of the surplus is eaten by 30 to 50 outside diners and about half the staff, and the operator\textquotesingle s 5 to 10\% waste figure has no stated base and sits in unresolved tension with a batch about twice what is eaten. The earlier candidate, that preparation is calibrated to the low attendance number and under-serves real diners, does not hold at the aggregate.
\item
  \textbf{Registration does not track the daily decision to eat.} Kadamba registrations are flat at about 1,070 a day every weekday, Sunday included, while turnout swings from 27 to 42\%. Sunday is the lowest and latest-arriving day at both messes with records, and Bakul\textquotesingle s manager describes weekend breakfast as very low (no records). Registration is automatic, with five early cancellations per meal type per month as the only way out.
\item
  \textbf{Breakfast is the structurally worst meal.} At Yuktāhār about 28\% of breakfast registrants eat against 56 to 89\% at lunch on the same days, and at Kadamba 62.9\% of April breakfast registrations were no-shows. The "about 30\%" gap students cited is a lunch-sized figure, and the breakfast gap is 63 to 72\%.
\item
  \textbf{Every recent change sits on the operator side,} apart from the temporary non-compulsory registration during the LPG shortage (whether it also covered holidays is disputed), and none has touched registration or billing. The one reported waste reduction (Yuktāhār, August to December, reported by staff themselves) came from a paper routine made blame-free and discussed daily over about four months, alongside team training at a project manager\textquotesingle s farmhouse. Software was tried and dropped because it cost more than the data it would add, and staff are not digitally inclined. The switch to rock salt and cold-pressed oil came about six months before the visit, later than the takeover.
\item
  \textbf{The two best-evidenced kitchens run opposite forecasting philosophies:} a fixed ratio plus reactive top-up at Kadamba, and learning from its own books plus a within-meal projection at Yuktāhār. Both are exposed to the closing crest, one through top-ups under time pressure, the other through a projection window that sees about a seventh of the morning\textquotesingle s diners on the measured curve (candidate transfer to Yuktāhār). Bakul sits between, with a weekly judgement bounded by a shortage floor, and an operator who states outright that shortage is worse than waste (single-sourced).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Outstanding Data Collection}\label{outstanding-data-collection}

The new records, operator interviews and first-hand account answered several earlier items: the shape and timing of the crowd peak (measured for Kadamba); whether the gap sits near 30\% (not for breakfast: about 63\% at Kadamba, about 72\% at Yuktāhār); how stock and registration numbers become per-item quantities (answered for all three messes); whether any records-based time series exists (two now do); the wording of Kadamba\textquotesingle s 70\% batch (heard first-hand, still the operator\textquotesingle s own claim); the cancellation limit (five per meal type per month, at least four days ahead, confirmed); whether service after 9:30 is a formal extension (informal, by the back door); and which hall Vijayalakshmi Caterers serves (Bakul, single-sourced; whether Palash shares the kitchen remains a candidate). The earlier kilogram figures for Kadamba waste are withdrawn. Still open:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Multi-month records: Kadamba breakfast from May onward, any lunch and dinner exports, and exports for Yuktāhār, Bakul and Palash, so semester-level and exam-period patterns can be tested. Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s site head expects exams to push attendance down, but no record covers an exam window.
\item
  A Bakul breakfast scan export, to test the manager\textquotesingle s 8:00 to 8:30 rush, at most 60\% turnout and very low weekends; whether anything in Bakul\textquotesingle s weekly adjustment is written down; the Hafizpet top-up lead time; and whether the reserve for about 50 at 9:20 rests on data or judgement and how much is left at close.
\item
  Records carrying student identity, to test individual trajectories such as the reported three-to-six-week decline, and the Yuktāhār Wastage Book archive from June 2026, which would give a per-item history of waste and run-out times.
\item
  The base of Kadamba\textquotesingle s 5 to 10\% waste figure and how it reconciles with a 70\% batch against 37\% turnout; whether a Skip Meal declaration lowers the count Kadamba cooks against; and whether the first-batch ratio differs between veg and non-veg.
\item
  Whether the non-compulsory registration relaxation ever applied over holidays and fests; whether the CDS head named in the team\textquotesingle s account and the CDS Chair named earlier are one person or two roles; and whether the back-door service after 9:30 is known to or sanctioned by CDS.
\item
  Kadamba\textquotesingle s internal staff headcount, to size "about half the staff eat at the mess", and whether outside diners are counted anywhere; and whether breakfast service formally opens at 7:00 or 7:30, given the live counter from 7:00 and about eleven scans a day before 7:30.
\item
  Whether Yuktāhār keeps any arrival timestamps, so its first-half-hour projection can be tested against its own closing crest, and what waste target its wastage board shows, which was not captured clearly.
\item
  Dates for the Prism tender, the Kadamba LPG response, the non-compulsory registration precedent, the Kadamba dinner-sambar removal, any change to a standing Bakul ratio, each paneer switch, the Yuktāhār takeover year, the salt and oil switch and the farm training trip.
\item
  Whether the Wednesday electricity rise at Yuktāhār holds over more weeks; why Kadamba non-veg registrations roughly doubled in the first week of April; whether Yuktāhār\textquotesingle s Sunday pattern (lunch registrations fall, breakfast do not) holds across more weeks; and why Monday is a strong breakfast day at Kadamba and a weak one at Yuktāhār.
\item
  Why the Yuktāhār production sheet and attendance register disagree for breakfast on 3 September (77 against 110), and whether Skip Meal use, even if rare in a larger sample, reduces item-level run-outs.
\item
  When Skip Meal was introduced and why, why the cancellation cap is five, and whether breakfast hours have ever differed from 7:30 to 9:30. These are governance questions for CDS, whose head or Chair has not been interviewed, along with the vendor contract terms.
\end{itemize}

Not yet claimed: that breakfast attendance is declining or rising across the student population over a semester; that any one student\textquotesingle s trajectory matches the aggregate; that the Kadamba April profile holds for other months or messes without records; that Skip Meal has no effect on kitchen behaviour, as opposed to near-zero observed student use (now also disputed from the kitchen side at Kadamba); and that reheated food at Kadamba causes students to skip.
