# Use Case

The breakfast system at IIIT Hyderabad registers, bills and serves breakfast to resident students in four messes (Kadamba, Palash, Bakul and Yuktāhār) through one digital platform. Registration counts, not attendance, drive billing and the quantity each operator cooks. Three caterers run the four messes. Two cook on site, and Bakul's, Vijayalakshmi Caterers, cooks at the Hafizpet kitchen and trucks the food in. This map identifies every actor, institution and informal structure that takes part in the system, shapes it, benefits from it or is affected by it, and the relationships, dependencies and conflicts between them.

Operator visits, the Yuktāhār registers, Kadamba's scan-level April records and a later account of the Bakul manager overturned several first-version findings about the vendors, the four-day lead time and who places food orders. Each change is marked as revised where it appears. There are no Bakul records, so every Bakul figure here is the manager's own statement or estimate.

# Stakeholders

## Primary, Secondary, and Indirect Stakeholders

Thirty-two candidate actors were identified through seventeen lenses (student, social, food production, governance, money, technology, academic, sleep and time use, substitutes, campus infrastructure, waste, health, policy, informal systems, absent or sleeping actors, temporal, intervention). Seven became visible only once the operators' own practice was observed: operator site management, the CDS site team, suppliers, the garbage contractor, event and exam organisers, quality-check visitors and the food-safety regulator. One more, the Student Parliament's mess seats, came from the Parliament's published records. Twenty-four are in the final map.

Five were treated as minor. Cleaning staff and the juice canteens add no leverage beyond serving staff and Vindhya. Guest diners are counted by the operators themselves and stay too small for a full stakeholder: Yuktāhār's register shows none to twelve unregistered, paying or faculty and staff diners per breakfast on the legible September days (confirmed, records). Quality-check visitors, described by Kadamba's manager as IIIT faculty who inspect the store and by the operator as a weekly QC inspection, are read as a role of the Mess Committee's faculty, which is a candidate identity. The food-safety regulator certifies the campus but has no part in attendance. Three were excluded: a campus health office (its only link to the messes is food safety), senior-to-junior norm transmission, and a parental financial relationship (mess fees are bundled into hostel fees).

Revised: Kadamba's extra portions were first described as 30 to 40 for paying walk-ins, with security staff sharing a leftover meal. The operator's own account is that about 30 extra portions are cooked in advance for 30 to 50 people outside the student registration, such as night-shift security, and that about half of Kadamba's staff also eat from the same food (single-sourced). Together they are a large, uncounted sink for first-batch surplus (candidate), so they now count as a stakeholder: non-student diners fed at Kadamba.

## Formal and Informal Roles

Every institutional and commercial actor holds a formal, rule-defined or contract-defined position: students as registrants, the Mess Committee, the Mess Office, the CDS site team, the Warden, the three operators, serving and kitchen staff, suppliers, the garbage contractor, the canteens and the delivery platforms.

**Formal roles inside the operators.** Each operator runs its own organisation, which the institution does not see. The two on-site ones are opposites, and Bakul splits across two sites.

Kadamba sits at the end of a contract chain from CFS to CDS to Prism, and inside Prism from an operations manager to a manager, a supervisor and the service staff (single-sourced). A separate staff recording adds a director above the operations manager; reading that as Prism's ladder is a candidate. The supervisor is the in-service quantity decider: they watch the counters, track how much of each item is left at what time and tell the kitchen how much more to cook. The kitchen has about ten chefs with separate veg and non-veg cooks, a main vegetarian chef, and a head chef who joins on biryani days. Up to fifty staff work at the mess (single-sourced). Every meal passes a fixed safety sequence before service: cooking temperature checked, a sample kept 72 hours, a wrapped display plate set at the entrance (confirmed, observed), serving temperature per item, then a tasting by a facility team with two Prism staff (confirmed, observed) and corrections. Water TDS is read daily and QC inspects weekly. Who the facility team is (CDS site team, CFS, Facilities and Estate, or faculty visitors) is not confirmed and is kept separate from each.

Yuktāhār has no hierarchy: everyone does every job and peers cross-check. ABC runs the mess through two project managers, with about three site staff sharing the work (single-sourced; revised from a five-person team). The mess in-charge keeps the accounts and the Wastage Register, and colleagues check those records. A cross-verifier writes and checks the grocery register, and their signature is on the photographed fridge cleaning log (confirmed). A coordinator keeps the registrations, the weekday attendance register and a wastage board with a falling waste-share target. The store function pulls, sorts and cleans raw material but is nobody's dedicated job. A tasting pair checks every breakfast, with cutting and cleaning teams alongside, and sixteen kitchen staff start cooking at 5:30 to be ready by 7:30. A site head has run the mess since the takeover and writes in the registers daily; whether they are one of the two project managers is unconfirmed. Each role attaches to a book (Wastage Book, weekday attendance register and board, grocery issue book, store ingredient book, stock register, vegetable book and indent, milk, fruit, gas, electricity and water register, fridge logs), all photographed (confirmed), and each book is checked by someone other than its keeper. What was issued must match what was cooked, and a mismatch goes to the daily meeting; on 18 September the grocery book and stock register agreed on toor dal (confirmed). An office digitises the paper books later.

Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) splits two sites: Hafizpet cooks, and the mess holds, serves and decides. The operator describes a line from staff to the manager to a managing director, and a complaint route from the CDS team to the manager, the kitchen and the managing director (single-sourced). The manager is the single quantity decider at the mess. This puts Bakul between the other two, with a weekly judgement rather than Kadamba's fixed share or Yuktāhār's books and no book of its own that any source has seen (candidate).

**Informal roles** belong to the Mess Cell community, Ping and the tool developers. None has official standing and each does a real job: a resale market, a public-pressure channel and an alternative registration interface. The operators add informal structures of their own. Yuktāhār's twice-daily meeting is the system's real forecasting institution, and no institutional document names it. It also carries Yuktāhār's informal organisation. There is no leave policy, so absences are raised there and colleagues agree to cover. One project manager took all staff to his farmhouse for training on what good quality looks like, and staff describe a family-like culture (single-sourced). That informal layer holds the formal books together. The WhatsApp group between the CDS site team and each operator carries formal business informally. At Kadamba, meals for outside diners and half the staff, the door reopened for 5 to 10 minutes after close (25 students came after close on one biryani day) and the back door that, in the operator's words, the mess "can't say no" to, are accommodations that absorb surplus and lateness with no rule requiring them (single-sourced).

**Roles that are not separate stakeholders.** Attending regularly, skipping regularly, having an early class, selling on Mess Cell and using an unofficial tool are states one student moves through in a week. Peer influence is a relationship inside the Students node, not a separate "Friends" stakeholder. Guest diners have a different formal relationship (no registration, a higher spot rate) but too little volume for their own node. Students also act as menu selectors. In the Bakul manager's account, students choose combinations from balanced diet, protein, carbohydrate and preference, while he reasons from traditional pairings, regional habit and what is eaten (single-sourced; residents have not been asked). Whether residents differ in regional familiarity, diet and breakfast habit in ways that matter for menus is untested, so menu research at Bakul should sample along those lines (research implication, candidate).

## Dependencies

Students depend on operators for food quality and timing. Operators depend on no individual student, an asymmetry consistent with a walk-in price that needs no negotiation. Operators depend on the Mess Office for counts released four days ahead and on the contract for payment. Revised: operators do not depend on that count to procure food. Procurement is theirs and runs on its own cadence (Yuktāhār groceries fortnightly, vegetables two or three times a week, milk daily, confirmed from its registers; Kadamba dry goods a week ahead, most perishables the day before). What the count sets is how much cooked food is prepared, since every operator converts registrations into a cooked quantity and cooked food lasts only three to four hours. Kadamba uses a fixed first-batch share. Bakul uses 70 to 80% by item, adjusted weekly and not below about 70%. Yuktāhār uses its records, its meeting and a first-half-hour projection. The Mess Office depends on students to register accurately, but nothing in billing rewards accuracy: a student pays the same whether or not they attend and is registered automatically unless one of the capped cancellations is used. The weakest link in the chain has no incentive attached. The Mess Committee depends on the Mess Office to execute policy. The Office's one confirmed policy change, motivated by workload (candidate), did not visibly come from the Committee, which suggests operational pressure may drive policy from below as often as governance drives it from above.

Operators depend on suppliers for clean raw stock, on the garbage contractor to remove what is not eaten and, at Bakul, on the road. Revised: that leg was first given as 40 minutes from a kitchen near the airport, with top-ups arriving within 30 minutes. The kitchen is about 8 km away, a drive of about 20 minutes early in the morning and 40 in normal traffic, and a top-up needs a phone call, cooking and that drive. Total lead time is unstated, so the 30-minute figure survives only as a noisy-audio reading that transit alone makes doubtful (single-sourced). A top-up called late in the window would miss the close, which is a candidate reason the manager holds food for about 50 at 9:20, several times the 10 to 15 he says arrive in the last ten minutes. The same boxes keep food hot and soften it, so Bakul's quality depends on the transport link as well as the kitchen (aloo paratha only; other items untested). Kitchen staff depend on the QR scanner and portal for live counts. Operators also depend on one another without planning to: Yuktāhār's site head blames weak Wednesday paneer turnout on another mess serving something more attractive that day, so the messes substitute for each other as well as for Vindhya, the juice canteens and delivery.

A dependency runs the other way at Kadamba. The outside diners and about half the staff eat what the fixed first batch produces (single-sourced), so any cut to that batch takes food from people nobody has consulted unless their portions are planned explicitly. They are a latent opponent of waste reduction (candidate).

Revised: the first version said vendors and students have no direct relationship and everything routes through the Mess Office or portal. They meet daily at the counter. A student complained in person to Kadamba's manager about the dropped sambar and got a direct answer, operators absorb latecomers through a door and a back door they say they cannot refuse, and Yuktāhār weighs student plate waste. Only formal complaints route through institutional channels, running from a director's email or the CDS team to the manager and the kitchen, and at Bakul on to the managing director (single-sourced). No source describes the Portal and IT owner having any direct relationship with the Mess Committee, although the Committee sets policy that the system presumably enforces.

One contract dependency matters more than any other cell. Vijayalakshmi Caterers says it won a tender and is paid monthly on plates with deductions, with tender figures unclear on the recording, and that payment is on plates served (single-sourced). If plates mean registrations, an operator cooking for well under all of them keeps a margin on every no-show and has no financial interest in closing the gap. If plates mean meals served, the reverse holds. Bakul's "served" and Kadamba's "we cook on registrations because they are getting paid for it" pull opposite ways. The contracts may differ, "served" may be a normalised word for billed plates, or Kadamba's remark may describe a duty rather than a formula. One word in one account does not settle Bakul, still less the others, so the question stays open for every operator until the contract wording or CDS confirms it. If Bakul is paid on plates served, it bears the cost of over-cooking and accepts it to avoid a shortage (candidate).

## Influence

Influence splits by what each actor controls. Operators hold high local influence over how much is cooked, when a batch is topped up and whether a second counter opens, and they respond within 10 to 30 minutes. At Kadamba that minute-to-minute decision sits with the supervisor, at Yuktāhār with the kitchen team reading the first half hour. At Bakul the manager decides, but every top-up adds a 20 to 40 minute drive to the cooking time. Latecomers are absorbed, not decided on, since students at the back door cannot be refused. Operators hold no influence over the registration cut-off, the billing rule or the service window, and their influence over the menu is contested. Bakul's manager is the clearest case: he holds pairing knowledge and sees what is eaten, yet in his account students choose the combinations, and which menu decisions he can override is unknown (single-sourced). The Mess Office holds the registration and billing rules and the only scan-level attendance data, and has no confirmed influence over how much any operator cooks. The Mess Committee holds menu authority and the final say on policy. Academic administration holds the class timetable, the variable most in conflict with breakfast hours, and exerts no influence on the mess at all. Students supply the input everyone else plans around and have no influence over any rule, except informally through Mess Cell and direct complaint at the counter.

# Findings

Thirty-two candidate stakeholders were narrowed to twenty-four on direct evidence. Three lenses that might plausibly have produced a stakeholder (student health, senior-to-junior norms, a parental financial relationship) produced none.

The Mess Office is distinct from the Mess Committee, separating who decides policy from who executes it. Every earlier account, including this team's first drafts, had collapsed the two. The Office owns the four-day registration rule, passes registration counts to operators, runs the platform and issued the one confirmed policy change on record, which was motivated by workload (candidate). Revised: it does not place food orders. Operators buy their own stock on their own cadence, and the four-day count sets only how much cooked food they prepare.

The operators are the largest part the first map left out, and each runs a different planning regime. Yuktāhār plans at a twice-daily meeting from the Wastage Book for the matching week (the menu repeats in weeks one and three, and two and four), the weekday register, the month's pattern, holidays and events, and a run-out rule ("if it runs out at 1:30 we re-cook; if at 2:20 we don't"). The books are confirmed; the rule is single-sourced. During service it samples the first half hour to project the rest. Kadamba cooks about 70% of registrations first against a gram standard for every dish, and its supervisor calls top-ups from a raw reserve. Its manager says there is no forecasting method, and the gram standard is its only standing record. Revised: Bakul was read as a fixed 70% raised to 80%, with top-ups inside 30 minutes. Its manager cooks 70 to 80% by item, adjusted weekly from attendance history, keeps about 70% even on low weekends because there should be no shortage even if food is wasted, and tops up by phone on an unstated lead time (single-sourced). Yuktāhār's matching-week learning against Kadamba's fixed ratio is the cleanest comparison in the map, and Bakul's weekly judgement with a floor sits between them (candidate). Every recorded planning figure sits well above actual turnout: 37.1% at Kadamba in April and about 28% at Yuktāhār in September (confirmed, records), against Yuktāhār's belief of at most about 50% and Bakul's manager's estimate of at most about 60%. His first batch and weekend floor both exceed even that, and if Bakul resembles the recorded halls the gap is larger and its size unknown (candidate). At the two recorded halls, then, the operators' conversion factors are miscalibrated upward.

The registration-to-attendance gap is already measured twice and reaches nobody who sets the rules. The CDS team holds a timestamp for every Kadamba breakfast scan (11,941 of 32,162 registrations availed in April, confirmed). Yuktāhār records registered against eaten by weekday and waste by item, in books each checked by someone other than its keeper. Kadamba keeps a gram standard per dish and watches its counters but records nothing of what was eaten. At Bakul the measurement is the manager's weekly review, and no source says whether any of it is written down, so it may live in one head (candidate). None of these measurements is confirmed to reach the owner of registration and billing policy, and they do not reach each other: no source shows CDS sharing scans with operators, or operators' registers reaching CDS or the Mess Committee.

Academic administration holds the lever most likely to affect the core problem, the overlap between breakfast hours and the 8:30 AM class start, and has no relationship of any kind to the three mess-governance actors. It is the clearest sleeping stakeholder in the map: total authority over the one variable in conflict, and zero engagement with the system it conflicts with. The operator evidence extends the gap downward. Exams, hackathons and campus events cut turnout, yet reach a kitchen only if someone raises them in a meeting.

Three feedback channels exist on the student side (an in-app per-meal rating, a public email address and Ping) and none has a confirmed instance of changing a policy. The operator side adds channels the first map missed: complaints from a director's email or the CDS team to the manager and kitchen (at Bakul on to the managing director), the WhatsApp group between the CDS site team and each operator, the facility team's tasting with Prism before service, and complaints made in person at the counter. It also adds the one working internal feedback loop anywhere in the system, Yuktāhār's Wastage Book, run-out times and weekday register feeding the next meeting, which took hold only once measurement was made blame-free (single-sourced). The fortnightly menu rotation that makes that loop possible is a confirmed policy change, and it came through the governance route from CDS to the Student Council to the Student Parliament (single-sourced, CFS Chair), not through any student feedback channel.

Revised: the first version found registered attendance markedly lower, against capacity, at every North Indian mess, read as three independently run messes pointing to cuisine. That no longer stands. Bakul is served by Vijayalakshmi Caterers from Hafizpet (single-sourced) and Palash from the same off-site kitchen (candidate), so the pattern does not come from three independent messes, and an off-site kitchen where food is held hot for hours and softens in transit is a competing explanation (candidate). The Bakul manager's view that North and South Indian eating patterns differ is a hypothesis from experience, not evidence for cuisine. Turnout records show cuisine and item driving attendance inside a single mess too: at Kadamba non-veg breakfast registrations were availed at 48.5% and veg at 32.4%. Cuisine is one factor among several.

The Mess Committee's chair, reported as a faculty member by a secondhand source we could not verify, is a second, subtler candidate sleeping stakeholder if that report holds: a person with cross-institutional standing that no student channel has and a plausible bridge to Academic administration, with no evidence the bridge has been used. Kadamba's manager describes IIIT faculty inspecting the store, and the operator describes a weekly QC inspection and a facility team that tastes every meal with Prism. These are the only signs of institutional contact with the kitchens, and whether any belongs to the Committee is a candidate only.

The Student Parliament has an elected Mess Secretary and a Mess Deputy Secretary for each mess (Kadamba, Palash, Yuktāhār), known from the Parliament's published records (single-sourced). It is a real, structured channel of student representation, and it sits in the internal ring beside the Committee, Office and Warden.

Surplus leaves the system through people and a bin, not a compost heap, at least at Kadamba. Revised: staff eating leftovers is replaced. Kadamba cooks about 30 extra portions for 30 to 50 outside diners, about half its staff eat from the same food (single-sourced), latecomers are fed through the reopened door and the back door, Yuktāhār turns leftover idli into idli upma or idli poha for lunch and sets surplus milk as curd, and the rest of Kadamba's cooked surplus goes to a dustbin the garbage contractor empties. Kadamba's 5 to 10% waste figure, on an unstated base, sits uneasily with a 70% batch against 37% turnout: either the extra diners, reheating and small top-ups absorb most of the surplus, or the percentage uses another base. The evidence does not say which. The first version's assumption that composting absorbs the physical surplus is revised for Kadamba, whose manager says there is no composting (single-sourced). The CFS Chair earlier said a collector takes waste to an outside compost facility, and the manager may mean only that nothing is composted on site, so the onward route stays open and the garbage contractor is the single point where all of Kadamba's waste leaves.

Revised: the first version named the four-day procurement lead time as a hidden actor capping the system's response to any same-day signal. Kitchens respond within 10 to 30 minutes and buy perishables a day ahead, so that finding no longer holds. Three other hidden actors carry outsized influence without being visible to any one respondent. The Portal and IT owner is used constantly and named by no one. The four-day registration lock is a Mess Office rule that fixes the student's commitment long before they know whether they will eat. The first-batch share is a rule of thumb held by each operator, fixed at Kadamba, a weekly judgement at Bakul that can fall only as far as its floor of about 70% (single-sourced), and it turns the four-day commitment into cooked food that lasts three to four hours.

Several questions remain open after the operator visits. The most consequential is whether operators are paid per registered or per served plate, and what the deductions are, since that decides who gains from no-shows. Bakul's "served" needs confirming from the contract or CDS, along with whether Kadamba's basis differs. Whether Palash is served by the same operator and kitchen as Bakul needs confirming. At Bakul it is open whether anything behind the weekly adjustment is written down, how long a top-up takes, which menu decisions students control and the manager can override, and why Bakul residents skip breakfast. Who heads CDS is open, as is whether Skip Meal changes the count Kadamba cooks from, which needs a direct answer. At Yuktāhār, whether the mess in-charge is the person the recording calls "Adit Amma", and whether the site head is one of the two project managers, are unconfirmed. Also open: who the facility team is, what the outside registration for night security is and who pays for those portions, what base Kadamba's waste figure uses, who decides the menu at Kadamba, whether any operator's item-level waste figures reach the Committee, whether CDS ever shares scans back, the onward route of Kadamba's waste, the identity of faculty quality-check visitors, whether staff meals are an entitlement, and whether any channel could carry the academic and event calendar to the kitchens. The identities between CDS, CFS and the bodies named earlier need a direct answer from CDS leadership, which has not been interviewed.
