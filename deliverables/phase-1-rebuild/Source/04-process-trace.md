# Overview

The Stakeholder Map says who is involved. This trace says what actually happens, step by step, workarounds included. It follows one breakfast from the student's registration through the operator's planning and cooking to the counter, the close, and what happens to the food nobody eats.

Three timescales are kept apart. Registration is locked about four days ahead, and because every student is registered automatically it works as a standing month-long default, not a choice. Cooked quantity is set the day before: as a share of registrations at Kadamba, from the mess's own past records at Yuktāhār, and at Bakul as an item-dependent share the manager adjusts weekly from past attendance. The decision to eat is made on the morning itself, and the operator responds live at the counter. At Yuktāhār it also re-forecasts the rest of the meal from the first half hour, so the morning holds an operator forecast as well as a reaction.

(Revised: the first version covered only the student half and stopped at the kitchen door, because no source then showed how a registration became a cooked quantity.) Operator interviews at three messes, one mess's photographed planning and attendance registers, a month of timestamped Kadamba scans, a walkthrough of the Kadamba kitchen and the team's first-hand account of two visits now make the operator half visible. The two best-evidenced operators run on different logics, so the trace follows each end to end. Bakul, cooked off site, is traced on its manager's account alone, since no Bakul records exist. What stays unseen is narrow and listed at the end.

# Trace Decisions and Interactions

Registration is locked about four days ahead. The CDS handbook requires registration and cancellation at least four days in advance, and Kadamba's manager calls it "weekly compulsory." Students say they register "for a month," three of six explicitly. Both hold: the portal locks on a four-day weekly rhythm and students set a standing booking once. A team member's own experience as a Kadamba student adds that registration is automatic and mandatory, off campus and over holidays, and that the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month made four days ahead: "we don't have any opportunity to nullify it." The booking is a default. Kadamba's April records confirm the result: registrations flat at about 1,070 a day on every weekday including Sunday (range 928 to 1,123) while turnout runs from 42.1% on Tuesdays to 27.3% on Sundays.

### Decision points: operators

The three operators forecast in three ways. Kadamba cooks a fixed share of registrations and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond per-dish gram standards. Yuktāhār learns from its own books, looking up the matching past week before service and re-projecting from the first half hour during it. Bakul sits between. (Revised: an earlier reading, from a noisy recording that gave only the ratio, had Bakul resembling Kadamba.) Its manager varies the share by item and adjusts it weekly from past attendance by judgement, never below a floor he keeps against shortage. Whether any history is written down is unknown.

Two readings follow from the manager's own numbers, both candidates. His first batch (70 to 80%) and weekend floor (about 70%) both exceed the highest turnout he expects (at most about 60%), so the batch exceeds turnout by design; if Bakul resembles the recorded halls at 28 to 37%, the gap is larger, and without records it cannot be sized. And the late reserve of about 50 is several times the 10 to 15 late arrivals he describes, the difference being insurance against a shortage a top-up from 8 km away could not fix in time.

**Menu choice, all messes.** The student committee chooses from an operator-supplied menu and the operator follows it. Operators see per-item waste (at Kadamba uttapam, set dosa and upma waste while puri, bonda, vada and idli move) and shift quantities between items, as Kadamba did in cutting ragi idli and increasing plain idli, but cannot change the menu. The Bakul manager asks for a say, and his objection is to pairings, not dishes: kachori belongs with chole, and he would serve Raj Kachori with aloo curry where the menu lists chana. He describes students choosing from balance, protein, carbohydrate and preference, and himself from traditional regional combinations, taste and what is eaten, with about 20 years in North Indian food. He also mentioned a protein target of about 30 to 40 g a meal, set by an unknown party, and cites pav bhaji as drawing more students and muesli and Chocos as eaten readily (single-sourced). This is the operator's view; whether Bakul residents share it is untested.

## Yuktāhār: the book system as a process

Yuktāhār runs on paper books, each photographed by the team and explained by the operator. Everyone does every job, so "storekeeper" is a function people rotate through, and each book is cross-checked by a peer.

**Informal norms** on the student side are unwritten and shared: registering for the whole month rather than day by day, which looks like the path of least resistance and which auto-registration makes the default, and one respondent's habit of checking the menu first. Going early to avoid queues is a minority behaviour, since only a third of Kadamba scans arrive before 8:30. At Yuktāhār the culture is described as family-like, and one project manager trained all staff at his farmhouse on what good quality looks like and what responsibility to take (single-sourced).

**Workarounds** compensate for a gap in the formal system. Mess Cell resale, used by four of six, compensates for Skip Meal's lack of refund and is mirrored on the operator side by Kadamba's manager describing students who register while away while a friend eats in their place. Eating snacks or a later meal compensates for skipping without a cost-recovery path. Early registration at Kadamba compensates for a popular mess filling up. Students enter by the back door after close. On the operator side the workarounds are a fixed ratio in place of a learned forecast at Kadamba, a weekly judgement with a shortage floor and a held reserve at Bakul, raw stock held ready for a quick top-up, holding and reheating each batch's surplus at Kadamba, leftover reuse into the next meal at Yuktāhār, and extra portions for people outside the registration.

# Track Exceptions, Workarounds and Dependencies

## Operator exceptions

- **Scanner failure.** When the QR system fails, Kadamba staff write down ID and name by hand. The April export shows no sign of bulk entry (never more than two scans in one second), so hand-logged diners may be missing from the records, a candidate undercount.
- **Gas shortage.** During the LPG shortage Kadamba cut quantities a little and bought some items outside. This is the first operator account of the shortage, and its cause is still single-sourced.
- **Equipment failure.** Kadamba's bread grill is broken and unreplaced, which removes an item from service regardless of demand.
- **Events, exams and holidays.** Nothing formal tells the kitchen. They reach Yuktāhār's meeting only if someone mentions them, and hackathons and exams lower turnout in ways the site head calls unavoidable.
- **Supplier quality.** At Yuktāhār, impurities above the norm are raised in the meeting and lead to a complaint or a new supplier. Bakul's paneer has moved between three brands after complaints.
- **Biryani days.** Kadamba cooks biryani lunch at 100% rather than 70%, with a head chef brought in, so the ratio follows the operator's expectation of demand for a dish, not a fixed rule. Portion leakage (three pieces for two) still causes late run-outs at full preparation.
- **Off-site transport.** Every Bakul top-up is a phone call, a cook at Hafizpet and a 20 to 40 minute drive against a fixed close. The true lead time is unknown. The 9:20 reserve of about 50 is, as a candidate reading, the consequence: late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals.
- **Latecomers.** When Kadamba's front door closes at 9:30, latecomers use the back door and demand food, and staff do not refuse. Last-minute registrations are called "a pain." The speaker's wording covers both the breakfast and dinner closes; scans after 9:30 on every April day support it at breakfast.
- **Records that disagree.** For breakfast on 3 September at Yuktāhār, the Wastage Book records 77 diners (28%) and the attendance register, on a partly obscured row, records 110 (40%). Which feeds planning is unknown. The two-book rule covers materials, not diner counts, and the two books have different keepers, which may be why the difference stands (candidate).

The operator workarounds alongside these (leftover reuse, outside diners and staff eating from the same food, holding and reheating surplus, late-door service, raw stock held for a quick top-up) are absorption mechanisms. Each soaks up part of the gap before it becomes visible, and each is locally beneficial. The outside diners and the half of Kadamba's staff who eat at the mess are real people fed by the surplus, so any change to the first batch has to plan for them. Holding and reheating is the exception among absorbers: the team judged Kadamba's food to be cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, so it degrades fast. That links over-preparation to quality as an open chain, and whether poorer quality feeds skipping is not evidenced. Bakul's hot holding carries a similar cost for a different reason: food is kept hot from an early start through transport, and steam softens parathas on the way. As a candidate reading, the food kept back for late arrivals is also the food held longest. Yuktāhār tried and dropped a commercial ERP for grocery management and rush prediction, because paying "lakhs of rupees" for prediction made no sense when the data already sits in its books ("prediction will be based on our data only… I want to remain close to the data"), and because staff are not digitally inclined and too few people exist to digitise, so the office digitises later.

## Dependencies

Eating breakfast at the mess depends on waking with enough time and alertness, the most cited constraint. It depends on the desired item still being available (run-outs confirmed by at least three of six, directly or by reputation), on no clash with class-attendance pressure (explicit for one respondent), and on the mess still being open, since the 9:30 cut-off is the most-requested change. The attendance-policy pressure depends on academic scheduling, which the governance analysis found disconnected from mess governance.

Item availability depends in turn on a chain this trace can now follow. (Revised: the first version said the Mess Office fixes each vendor's order four days out, so nothing on the day could change what is cooked.) The four-day lock applies to registration, not to cooking or buying. Yuktāhār buys groceries fortnightly by the stock register from many local shops and godowns, vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily. Kadamba buys in three tiers: dry goods a week ahead, perishables the day before, some vegetables two to three days ahead. Cooked quantity is set the day before, and adjusted live through top-ups and re-cooks. At Bakul the first batch leaves Hafizpet before any sign of the morning's turnout, and a top-up can only add food, slowly. The binding constraint on waste is therefore cooked food prepared ahead of demand, not raw procurement: raw stock left over is issued again, while cooked food lasts only 3 to 4 hours.

The operator side has its own dependencies. They include supplier cadence and quality, and gas, electricity and water, which Yuktāhār logs daily and Kadamba checks for water quality daily. They include equipment (Kadamba's roti line, multi-item cooker and tilting curry machine, and the broken grill), and one person running all of Kadamba's inventory, a single point of failure. They include the matching-week Wastage Book page, which depends on the two-week menu rotation, and the daily meeting as the only channel for event information and exceptions at Yuktāhār. Transport from Hafizpet, about 8 km and about 20 minutes early, 40 later, bounds every Bakul top-up. Yuktāhār chooses inputs for quality (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months, with visits to the mills), and staff numbers matter: Yuktāhār has 16 kitchen staff and two project managers with some site staff sharing the work, in separate tasting, cutting and cleaning teams without a hierarchy, and Kadamba about ten chefs under a main veg chef with a head chef for biryani days.

Where uneaten food finally goes is only partly settled. The CFS Chair described production and plate waste going via a garbage collector to an outside compost facility. Kadamba's manager, asked about composting, said there is none: the bin goes to the garbage contractor (single-sourced). (Revised: the first assumption that composting absorbs the physical surplus.) At Kadamba the surplus is absorbed by outside diners and staff, by holding and reheating, by latecomer top-ups and by the bin, and whether the contractor composts it off site is unverified. Yuktāhār's disposal route after reuse is not stated in any source. At Bakul some leftovers return to Hafizpet and the rest is thrown, with no onward route stated.

# Key Findings

1. **The registration-attendance gap has several mechanisms, not one.** Habitual monthly over-registration made the default by automatic registration, scarcity-driven defensive registration at Kadamba, automatic allocation and simple forgetting all feed it, and the Kadamba records show their combined effect. Registration is an automatic standing booking, flat across the week while turnout varies by weekday, and not a daily demand signal.
2. **Peer influence is disconfirmed as a decision-determining factor.** Six respondents place it at most as a motivational add-on.
3. **There are three forecasting logics, not one or two.** (Revised: the first version gave every operator a private ratio, and a later one grouped Bakul with Kadamba.) Kadamba cooks a fixed 70% and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond gram standards. Bakul varies an item-dependent 70 to 80% weekly from past attendance by the manager's judgement, never below about 70%, so its correction closes against surplus only down to that floor, and whether it rests on any written record is unknown. Yuktāhār has no fixed ratio: it learns from its books by matching week and weekday and re-projects from the first half hour, though its head believes about 50% where his register shows about 28%. None of the fixed ratios is set against actual turnout, confirmed at 37.1% for Kadamba in April. The gap is measured, locally and repeatedly, and what is missing is any route from that measurement into registration or billing rules. Even in the best-documented mess the fast correction samples the calm start of service, not the crest at the close.
4. **Broken restock promises.** Staff say an item will return before close and it does not, a trust and service finding first reported by students. The operator side offers a candidate mechanism: a re-cook cut-off that leaves late run-outs unfilled, top-ups of 10 to 15 minutes on site, an unmeasured 20 to 40 minute drive from an off-site kitchen against a close minutes away, equipment failures and items that float above their gram standard, together with a Kadamba chef's "wait 5 minutes" that matches what students describe hearing.
5. **Under-provisioning is not the problem.** Two respondents independently said only about 30% of registrants attend and the mess still struggles to serve even that smaller group, which early drafts read as under-provisioning. The attendance half is now confirmed: about 28% at Yuktāhār in September and 37.1% at Kadamba in April, a gap of 63 to 72%. The under-provisioning reading does not hold. Every stated first-batch share sits well above that turnout, and at Bakul the share and weekend floor sit above even the manager's own estimate (candidate, no records), and Yuktāhār's book records leftovers. The shortage students feel is a timing and item-mix failure at the close. 26.5% of Kadamba scans arrive in the last quarter hour, though the curve differs by hall, since the Bakul manager describes an earlier peak. The items students report running out (idli, puri, bhatura, fruit) include those Kadamba says float above gram standard (puri, idli), while the ones it says waste (uttapam, set dosa, upma) are left over, and late run-outs are not re-cooked. Over-preparation and run-outs happen on the same morning.
6. **The escalation-path absence is corroborated from the operator side.** An operational complaint path exists and works for same-incident fixes, but no path for changing rules or timing works for students or operators.

**A tension the operator's own figures leave open.** Kadamba's stated practice and stated waste do not yet add up. In April it averaged about 1,072 registrations and 398 scans a day (confirmed). A 70% first batch is about 750 portions against about 400 eaten, a surplus of about 350 a day before any top-up (candidate arithmetic from the ratio and the records). The operator reports about 5 to 10% daily wastage on no stated base (single-sourced). The two are compatible only if most of the surplus goes somewhere other than the bin: to the 30 to 50 outside diners and half the staff, to food held and reheated, to smaller top-ups than the ratio implies, or to a waste figure on a different base such as all meals or plate waste only. Which holds is not known, and this trace assumes none.
