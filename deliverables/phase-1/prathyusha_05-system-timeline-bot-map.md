# ACTIVITY 5A — Behaviour Over Time (established now)

## Timescales actually supported by evidence

- **Within one morning** — ✅ well evidenced, six respondents describe this directly.
- **Across a week** (early-class day vs. not) — ✅ evidenced (daily eater 2, class-
  attendance-policy tradeoff explicit).
- **Across a month** — ✅ evidenced indirectly: monthly registration is the universal
  pattern, and the 5-cancellation cap operates on a monthly cycle.
- **Across a semester** — ✅ evidenced, six respondents each give a semester-level trend
  statement (see below) — the strongest temporal evidence this project has.
- **Across multiple semesters/years** — ❓ no evidence exists at this scale; not
  claimed.

## Temporal evidence register

| Respondent | Behaviour | Frequency | Trigger | Time horizon | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Original respondent (2026-09-03) | Habitual skipper | Most mornings | Intermittent fasting, poor hostel mess quality (historical), late sleep | Longstanding habit | ✅ |
| Daily eater 1 | Near-daily attender | ~5-6/week | Habit; late-night work causes the rare skip | This semester specifically **more regular** than before | ✅ |
| Daily eater 2 | Most-days attender | Most days | 8:30 class + late sleep causes skips; class-attendance policy prioritized over breakfast | No explicit before/after comparison given | 🟡 (trend not stated) |
| Skip-1 | Light eater | ~1/week | Appetite-driven, menu-checked | Changed "since just before classes started" — direction implied but not explicit | 🟡 |
| Skip-2 | Non-eater | 0/semester | Not hungry that early; eats after 10-10:30 instead | Zero for the entire current semester | ✅ |
| Skip-3 | Declining attender | Rare, was more regular | Late sleep → not hungry | Declining specifically over the **last 3-6 weeks** | ✅ — the most precisely dated trend statement in the dataset |

**Cross-respondent finding, stated honestly:** the six real respondents do **not**
agree on a single direction. One reports attendance *increasing* this semester (daily
eater 1); one reports a specific *recent decline* (skip-3, last 3-6 weeks); one has been
at *zero* all semester (skip-2); the rest give no clear directional trend. **There is
no single "the paradox is worsening" or "improving" claim this dataset supports** — six
individuals are moving in different directions simultaneously, which is itself the
honest system-level finding at this evidence level, not a gap to be resolved by more
interviews of the same kind.

## Qualitative Behaviour-Over-Time maps (no invented numbers)

### Within-morning crowd/availability curve — real data, reconciled

| Window | What respondents say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 7:30-8:00 | "Very peaceful," can get anything | Daily eater 1 (on 8:30-class mornings only) |
| 8:00-9:00 | Names this as the peak, but goes deliberately early specifically to avoid a crowd | Skip-3 |
| 9:00-9:30 | Names this as the peak, with confirmed item run-outs (idli, puri, bhatura, fruit) | Daily eater 1, daily eater 2, independently |
| Around 9:15-9:20 | Specific run-out point; staff-promised restocks don't materialize | Daily eater 1 |

**Reconciled reading, informed by a teammate's independent synthesis of the same
transcripts** (`student_interview_summary_neha.md` §8): rather than two competing
peaks, the more parsimonious interpretation is a **single crowd that builds
continuously from around 8:00 and crests just before the 9:30 close** — skip-3, who
leaves early specifically to dodge a crowd, is describing the *rising* portion of the
same curve the daily eaters experience at its *crest*, not a separate, earlier peak.
This resolves what first looked like a contradiction into one consistent shape, and
is a better account than treating the two windows as genuinely competing claims. It
remains real interview recall, not measurement — direct observation or QR-scan
timestamps (Activity 4B/5B) would still confirm the exact shape, but "continuous
build, crests near close" should now be the working hypothesis rather than "disputed
between two candidate peaks."

## Candidate feedback loops — real evidence upgrades applied

**R1 — "Shifting the Burden" via Mess Cell** *(reinforcing — sustains rather than
grows the dysfunction)*. Status: **upgraded from 🟡 to ✅ on its central mechanism.**
Real evidence: 0 of 6 respondents who addressed Skip Meal have ever used it, and the
one respondent who explained why did so in exactly the terms the teammate's hypothesis
predicted — "it doesn't return the money." Four of six respondents have used Mess
Cell in some form. The loop (no-show → registration wasted → resold via Mess Cell →
partial cost recovered → less pressure to fix billing → cycle continues) is now
directly evidenced at every link except the final "reduces pressure to fix billing"
step, which remains 🟡 (structurally plausible, not something any respondent stated
directly).

**B1 — Kitchen Waste Control via Skip Meal** *(balancing, but incomplete — a Fix That
Fails)*. Status: **the "Fix That Fails" framing is strengthened.** Skip Meal's
zero real usage across six respondents means this balancing loop may not even be
closing on the kitchen side in practice, not just failing to address the student
side as previously argued — if literally nobody uses it, its kitchen-planning value
is close to zero regardless of design intent. This is a stronger, more concerning
version of the original finding.

**B2 — Capacity Redistribution (weak/broken)**. Status: **a new confirming
mechanism found.** Skip-3 explicitly registers for the whole month specifically
*because* "Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full" — direct, first-person evidence
of scarcity-driven demand concentration on the popular mess, rather than organic
redistribution to Bakul/Palash. This is a real behavioural mechanism, not just an
inference from capacity numbers as before.

**New candidate loop, surfaced this round — "Under-provisioning for real attendees"**
*(not previously identified)*: two independent daily-eater respondents converge on a
claim that the mess *cannot adequately serve even the ~30% who do attend*, because
preparation is calibrated to something other than actual peak-clustered demand.
```
Registration inflated by several mechanisms (habitual, scarcity-driven, random
allocation) → true attendance rate low relative to registration → 🟡 preparation
possibly calibrated toward the low true-attendance number, not peak-hour clustering →
peak-hour (whichever window it is) run-outs occur even though total food may be
"enough" on average → attendees who do show up have a bad experience → 🟡 possible
long-run effect of discouraging exactly the students who do show up, though no
respondent states this last step directly
```
This is presented as a hypothesis with two real, independent, converging respondent
statements behind its first half, and an unconfirmed second half — not as a settled
loop.

## Key findings — 5A

1. No single population-level trend exists — real respondents move in different
   directions simultaneously. Any future claim that "the paradox is getting worse" (or
   better) needs to specify *for whom*, not assert it for students generally.
2. What first looked like two competing peak-crowding claims (8:00-9:00 vs.
   9:00-9:30) reconciles into one continuously building crowd cresting near the 9:30
   close — still recall-based, not measured, so direct observation remains the way to
   confirm the exact shape, but this is now a single working hypothesis rather than an
   unresolved contradiction.
3. Skip Meal's real usage rate in this sample is zero — the strongest evidence yet for
   treating it as a non-functioning tool in practice, not just poorly incentivized in
   theory.
4. A new candidate systemic loop — under-provisioning for actual (not just
   registered) attendees — is independently corroborated by two respondents and adds a
   previously-missing half to the waste narrative: this isn't only about food cooked
   for no-shows, it may also be about food *not* cooked for real attendees.

# ACTIVITY 5B — System-Level Validation Plan

## Historical data needed

Realistic minimum given this project's timeline: **4-8 weeks of daily registration/
attendance data per mess**, if that's all that's obtainable; one full semester would
be materially better for anything involving exam-period or deadline-clustering
effects, which no current evidence speaks to at all.

## Variables to track over time

Registration, attendance (QR scans), no-show rate, cancellations, Skip Meal usage,
walk-ins, capacity, menu, price, day of week, early-class context — all aggregate,
none identifiable, consistent with Activity 4B's data request.

## Analysis questions to specify before any data arrives

- Does the true peak window match either respondent claim (8:00-9:00 or 9:00-9:30), or
  is it something else entirely?
- Does the registration-to-attendance gap actually sit near the "~30%" figure two
  respondents independently cited, or was that a shared folk-belief rather than a real
  number?
- Does Skip Meal usage, even if rare, correlate with any measurable reduction in
  specific-item run-outs — or is its real-world effect genuinely zero, as this round's
  0-for-6 usage rate suggests?
- Does attendance actually decline in the specific 3-6 week window skip-3 identified,
  and if so, does it correlate with any academic-calendar event (deadlines, exams)?
- Do early-class mornings show a measurably different attendance pattern than non-
  early-class mornings, beyond what interviews already qualitatively suggest?

None of these are claimed as answered — they are the hypotheses this round's
qualitative data makes worth testing quantitatively.

## Change/trigger history still needed

When was Skip Meal introduced, and why? When was the 5-cancellation rule set at
exactly five? Has breakfast timing (7:30-9:30) ever been different? These remain
entirely unaddressed by any source and are appropriate governance-interview questions
(Guide 3), not resolvable from student accounts.

## Feedback-loop validation plan

| Loop | Weakest link | Data required | Interview required | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 (Shifting the Burden) | "Reduces pressure to fix billing" | None available — this is a structural/political claim, not a measurable one | Governance contact, on whether Mess Cell's existence has ever come up as a reason *not* to reform billing | Still 🟡, likely stays qualitative |
| B1 (Skip Meal as Fix That Fails) | Whether kitchen planning uses Skip Meal data at all | Kitchen-side process data (Activity 4B) | Mess staff, kitchen role | Closest to resolvable — a single staff interview likely settles this |
| B2 (weak capacity redistribution) | Whether students who don't get Kadamba slots actually switch to Bakul, or just don't attend at all | Registration data cross-mess, cross-time | None additional needed if data obtained | Partially resolvable from data alone |
| New: under-provisioning loop | Whether preparation quantity is really miscalibrated, or the "~30%" figure is simply wrong | Preparation + attendance data together | Kitchen staff (forecast method) | Needs both data and an interview |

## What we are not yet allowed to claim

- That breakfast attendance is declining (or rising) across the student population as
  a whole — the real sample is split.
- That the mess is definitively under-provisioning relative to real demand — two
  respondents believe this; no kitchen-side data confirms or refutes it.
- That the 9:00-9:30 window is *the* peak — a real respondent places it earlier.
- That Skip Meal has zero effect on kitchen behaviour — only that no respondent in
  this sample uses it; the kitchen side of that mechanism is untested.
- Any numeric trend over time for any variable — no quantitative time-series data
  exists yet for this system.
