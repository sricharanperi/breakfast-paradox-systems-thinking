# Executive Summary

Power in this system is not concentrated in one actor and does not move as a single current. It is split across several governance bodies whose relationship to one another is only partly established. It is exercised informally by actors with no official standing at all. Its most consequential features are two absences rather than presences: there is no relationship between the one actor who controls the class schedule and the actors who control everything else about breakfast, and there is no route by which the kitchen's own records of the gap reach anyone who sets the rules that produce it.

The first central finding of this analysis: students control an input that nothing requires to be accurate. Registration is an automatic default rather than a daily decision to eat. At Kadamba in April 2026, 32,162 breakfast registrations produced 11,941 meals eaten, a turnout of 37.1%, and registrations stayed flat at about 1,070 a day on every weekday while turnout ranged from 42.1% on Tuesdays to 27.3% on Sundays (confirmed from the scan records). The mess operators, the only actors who plan food against this input, each correct it privately, in opposite spirits. Yuktāhār distrusts the registration count and replaces it with its own records. Kadamba treats the count as its paid obligation and cooks a fixed 70% of it, then tops up from the counter (single-sourced). Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers) sits between the two: its manager cooks 70% to 80% of registrations depending on the item, adjusts that share week by week from historical attendance, and keeps food for about 70% of registrations even on weekends when breakfast attendance is very low (single-sourced). An earlier version of this brief said every operator has learned to distrust registration; that was revised, because it held for Yuktāhār only. The institution's own scan records measure the inaccuracy, but nothing routes that measurement into a rule, and the institution never sees any operator's correction.

The second central finding: the mess operator is the data-rich, authority-poor actor in this system. The operator sets the parameter with the largest immediate effect on breakfast waste, which is how much of each registration actually gets cooked, and Yuktāhār in particular keeps the system's only per-item, per-weekday record of registered, eaten and wasted food, in a set of books it reconciles against one another every day (confirmed). Kadamba holds per-dish gram standards and live counter knowledge but no per-item waste record was seen. Bakul's weekly adjustment rests on its manager's reading of past attendance, and whether any of it is written down is unknown. None of the three operators has authority over the registration cut-off, cancellation rules, billing or menu, and neither has a route by which its records reach anyone who does.

Several findings in earlier versions of this brief were revised on the operations evidence gathered in September 2026 from three mess operators and from the institute's own scan records, rather than merely extended. Vendors were previously described as passive actors who produce to a forecast fixed four days ahead; that was revised, because no operator cooks the full registration count for breakfast and the four-day lead time turned out to be the students' registration cut-off, not a procurement lock. A second, first-hand field account of Kadamba and Yuktāhār then added three things: Kadamba's full command chain from the institute down to its service staff, the finding that the two operators take opposite stances toward registration under the same billing basis, and the finding that the institution's routine oversight of the kitchen covers safety and taste but never quantity or waste. The Mess Office was previously presumed to hold the system's evidence base; that was revised, because the evidence sits with the operators and, separately, with the CDS team's scan records. Students were previously described as having no direct channel to vendors; that was revised, because a weak, informal channel runs through menu selection and the counter. Two earlier revisions still stand: a confirmed student representative structure on mess matters (the Student Parliament's Mess Secretary and per-mess Deputy Secretaries, an addition first made in the Stakeholder Map), and a confirmed prior instance of organized student action aimed directly at governance.

What remains unresolved is stated as open, not guessed at, and is listed in full at the end of this document. The most consequential open question is whether operators are paid per registered plate or per served plate, because that decides whether the registration gap costs the operator money or earns it money. The Kadamba operator's remark that it cooks on registrations because "they are getting paid for it" leans toward the per-registered-plate reading but is not contract evidence. The Bakul manager, by contrast, says monthly payment is based on the number of plates served (single-sourced). The two statements sit in tension and are not resolved here, so the question stays open for every operator.

Power is treated throughout as issue-specific rather than a single score per actor: a stakeholder may hold high authority over one decision and none over an adjacent one. Every claim below states which domain it applies to and distinguishes formal authority from observed influence.

# Assess Decision-Making Authority

## Formal governance structure

Three separate institutional actors hold mess governance, a split not recognized in this project's own earliest drafts, where "who decides" and "who executes" had been collapsed into one actor:

- **Mess Committee**: a policy body that sets the menu approximately one month ahead of service, with student input. That this body is genuinely separate from the Mess Office is confirmed. That it is chaired by a faculty member rests on a single secondhand report and is unconfirmed, though the Committee/Office split itself is not in question. Its authority beyond menu content is unconfirmed.
- **Mess Office**: the operational and administrative arm. It administers the registration platform, which closes about four days before service, and issues operational policy changes. The one confirmed policy action on record, a December 2024 tightening of cancellation rules, was issued by this office alone, with its stated motive being reduced staff workload rather than a student-facing goal. An earlier version of this brief said the Mess Office places the food orders on a four-day lead time. That was revised by the operations evidence: operators buy their own food (confirmed at Yuktāhār, single-sourced at Kadamba), on cycles far shorter than four days, and the four-day figure is the students' registration cut-off.
- **Warden**: holds authority over vendor management and structural changes to the mess. No source describes the Warden taking a specific action; this authority is stated but not observed in any account gathered.

**Academic administration** sets the class schedule, including the 8:30 AM start that overlaps with the breakfast window, with total authority within that domain and zero formal relationship to any of the mess-governance actors above. No student, Committee or Office has ever raised the overlap with Academic administration; in the one direct account on this point, nobody has really tried. This resolves the question in favor of **inattention rather than refusal**: the absence of a relationship reflects that the bridge has never been attempted, not that it was tried and blocked. The same disconnection reaches the kitchen. Exams, events and holidays reach the Yuktāhār kitchen only if someone raises them in the operator's planning meeting, in the operator's words, "we don't come to know until we sit in the meeting" (confirmed). There is no formal calendar feed from the institute to any operator.

**A further actor, verified directly from source: the Student Parliament's elected mess-related roles.** IIIT Hyderabad's Student Parliament, elected via Single Transferable Vote at roughly one representative per 50 students, maintains a **Mess Secretary** and a dedicated **Mess Deputy Secretary for each individual mess**, confirmed for Yuktāhār, Kadamba and Palash from the Parliament's own published records. Student representation on mess matters is a dedicated elected role per mess, sitting within the Parliament, rather than a seat appointed by the Mess Committee itself. Parliament representatives also hold a standing meeting format with a **Mess Warden**, a title distinct from, and not yet confirmed identical to, the Warden named above.

**The CDS team** is the institutional counterpart operators actually name. At Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers), complaints arrive from the "CDS team" and pass from the operator's manager to the kitchen and on to the operator's managing director; at Kadamba, complaints arrive by email from a director and are relayed by the operator's manager into a WhatsApp group that a site or facility team also reports into (both single-sourced). The April 2026 Kadamba breakfast scan records came from the CDS team (confirmed). Whether "CDS" names the same department this brief calls the Mess Office is unconfirmed, and the two are kept separate here rather than merged. Institutional oversight at Kadamba is routine but narrow. Every meal passes a fixed food-safety sequence: the cooking temperature is checked, a sample is kept for 72 hours so the food can be tested if anyone falls ill, a wrapped display plate is set at the entrance, the serving temperature of each item is measured, and a facility team tastes the food together with two of the operator's staff before corrections are made and service opens. The display plate and the joint tasting are confirmed by observation; the rest is single-sourced. Quality control inspects weekly, including the store, and the TDS of cooking and drinking water is read daily, with any failure triggering a full check (single-sourced). Whether the quality-control visits can penalize or only inspect is unknown, and whether the "facility team" is the CDS site or facility team named in the complaint WhatsApp group is unconfirmed; they are not merged here.

**The Kadamba command chain**, as the operator describes it, runs from CFS to CDS, then to the contractor Prism, and inside Prism from the operations manager to a manager, a supervisor and the service staff (single-sourced). Prism is an outsourced third-party vendor that registers as a vendor, wins the contract by tender bid and runs all of Kadamba's operations. This is the first account that places CFS at the top of the chain and names the contract holder. The head of CDS is named in the team's site account, while an earlier interview names a CDS Chair; the two names differ, and whether they are two roles or one role misnamed is open, so they are not merged. A separate staff account of an operator's internal reporting chain, from staff to supervisor to manager to operations manager to director, matches the lower part of this chain, but which mess it describes is unconfirmed, so reading it as Prism's chain is candidate.

A separately named "Mess Council," raised earlier as the recipient of a public complaint email, is very likely a naming artifact rather than a real, separate IIIT Hyderabad body: a real Mess Monitoring Council exists at IIT Hyderabad, a different institution with a near-identical name, and IIIT Hyderabad's own site carries no "Mess Council" reference anywhere.

A sharper, still-unresolved ambiguity replaces it. Secondhand sources describe what may be two different things both called "Mess Committee": a Parliament-internal committee of elected representatives that negotiates toward the administration on students' behalf, and the institute-level, faculty-led policy body described above. Operators add a third name. The Kadamba and Bakul operators both say a "student committee" chooses the menu from options the operator supplies (single-sourced). Whether this student committee is either of the two Mess Committees, or the CDS Committee named elsewhere in this project, is unconfirmed, and the three are not assumed to be one body.

For several specific decisions this system makes, including breakfast timing itself, the registration cut-off, and the terms of the operator contract beyond the tender, no source describes who proposes, approves, or can veto a change. This absence, across every available source, is treated as a finding in itself.

## Operator-side authority

"Vendors" was a single box in earlier versions of this brief. The operators' own accounts show a layered actor whose internal structure decides who holds which power.

- **Contract and price terms** sit with the operator's owners or managing director. The Kadamba manager does not know the plate rate: "our owners are directly dealing with them" (single-sourced). The reporting chain inside an operator runs from staff to supervisor to manager to operations manager to director (single-sourced, mess unconfirmed); at Kadamba, Prism's chain runs from the operations manager through a manager and a supervisor to the service staff (single-sourced).
- **Quantity rules** sit with the operator, and no institutional approval of them is evidenced anywhere. At Kadamba the standing rule is Prism's: a first batch of 70% of registrations, planned separately for veg and non-veg, with a gram standard for every dish (four idlis a person, 100 g of sambar, and separate standards for poha, pulao and the rest), and the remaining raw material kept ready to cook in 10 to 15 minutes. Biryani lunch is the exception, cooked at 100% of registrations at two pieces of 150 to 200 g each with unlimited rice, with a head chef brought in (all single-sourced). The ratio is therefore set per menu item at the operator's discretion, with no institutional rule either way. At Bakul (Vijayalakshmi Caterers), food is cooked at the operator's Hafizpet kitchen, about 8 km away, and the manager sets the first batch at 70% to 80% of registrations, the share depending on the item, from historical attendance he reviews week by week: if Mondays have run at about 70% attendance, he cooks about 80% of Monday's registrations. He keeps food for about 70% of registrations even on weekends, when breakfast attendance is very low (all single-sourced). It is a judgement-based weekly calibration with a floor beneath it, not a fixed ratio like Kadamba's.
- **The in-service top-up decision** sits with a different person in each mess. At Kadamba the supervisor is in constant check at the counters and tells the kitchen how much more to cook; staff watch each dish's level against its gram standard, and the chef cooks against registrations and turnout so far (single-sourced). Items such as bonda, puri, vada and idli "float", with diners taking more than the standard. At Yuktāhār, run by ABC Hospitality Services, the same decision is collective: a planning meeting of the whole team, twice a day, discounts portal registrations using the matching week from the menu's two-week cycle in the Wastage Book, the attendance register grouped by weekday, the month's turnout and any holidays or campus events (confirmed), and during service the team uses the first half hour as a sample to project the rest of the meal (single-sourced). At Bakul the decision is the manager's alone: about every 30 minutes he compares the food left with registrations and the demand still expected, and if more is needed he phones the Hafizpet kitchen with an approximate quantity, which is then cooked and driven over (single-sourced). The drive alone takes about 20 minutes early in the morning and about 40 minutes normally, and the full lead time of a top-up is not stated; an earlier account that extra food arrives within about 30 minutes came from noisy audio and may describe the check interval rather than the delivery, so it is kept only as unverified.
- **Re-cook decisions, portioning, leftover reuse and supplier choice** also sit with the operator. The Yuktāhār Wastage Book is used as a rule, "if it runs out at 1:30 we re-cook; if at 2:20 we don't," and leftover idli, dosa batter and milk are reused, with leftover idli turned into idli upma or idli poha for lunch (confirmed). Yuktāhār holds a norm for the grams of dirt and stones expected in each raw item and complains to or changes the supplier when it is exceeded, and it visits the mills that supply its oil (single-sourced). Bakul's operator has switched paneer brands twice after complaints (single-sourced). During the LPG shortage the Kadamba operator cut quantities and bought items from outside on its own initiative (single-sourced). Who holds authority to replace broken equipment is unknown; the Kadamba toaster was broken with no replacement at the time of the visit.
- **Menu content** is the one kitchen domain where the operator's knowledge exceeds its authority. The Bakul manager says students select the menu combinations and that some of them do not work as pairings rather than as dishes: kachori, in his view, belongs with chole, and Raj Kachori, listed with chana, would go better with aloo curry. He reasons from traditional and regional combinations, taste and what people actually eat, citing about 20 years' experience with North Indian food, and describes the students as reasoning from balanced diet, protein, carbohydrate and individual preference; he also mentioned a target of about 30 to 40 g of protein per meal, whose origin is unknown (all single-sourced, his account). He also sees which items move: pav bhaji brings more students to breakfast, and muesli and Chocos go readily. He holds expertise and consumption knowledge that the menu process does not use, and no formal power over it. Whether his view of pairings matches what Bakul residents prefer is untested, and it is not treated here as evidence of what students want.
- **Internal structure** is the sharpest contrast between the two operators with the fullest accounts. Yuktāhār is flat: two project managers, no hierarchy, no storekeeper-only role, and everyone does every job across tasting, cutting and cleaning teams. Records are checked by peers rather than by superiors, and the mess in-charge, who also keeps the accounts, keeps the Wastage Register while a colleague cross-verifies the grocery register (single-sourced; the cross-verifier's signature on a photographed fridge checklist is confirmed). Leave has no formal policy and is shared by agreement in the daily meeting. The site head describes a proposal-and-review practice, "you follow this; if you find any problems come back to me" (confirmed). Kadamba runs the layered chain above, in which the supervisor decides and no staff voice was described. Bakul's internal structure was not described beyond its complaint route and a manager who decides both the first batch and each top-up for a kitchen about 8 km away (single-sourced).

The operator therefore holds high local authority over the quantity, timing and reuse of food, and none over the rules that shape how many students register.

# Identify Formal and Informal Power

Formal power sits with the governance actors and Academic administration, described above. Four informal actors exercise real influence with no formal standing at all:

- **Roommates and friends**: direct social prompting changes individual attendance decisions in a minor way, though it remains a secondary, not decisive, effect (six real student respondents support this reading). Friends also eat in place of registrants who do not come; the Kadamba manager describes this as routine (single-sourced), which corroborates from the operator side that informal redistribution of registrations happens and is tolerated.
- **The Mess Cell WhatsApp community**: a self-organized resale market demonstrating real, working peer-to-peer coordination capacity. This capacity has mostly been applied to individual transactions rather than a collective approach toward governance, with one significant exception, below.
- **Ping**, the student publication, has published on mess issues at least four times (a November 2024 piece on a unilateral non-veg cancellation, a December 2024 opinion survey, a January 2025 follow-up, and a 2023 piece on an unrelated water-quality issue at the same institution), creating public visibility for problems that might otherwise stay private. No source identifies this visibility alone producing a policy response.
- **Unofficial registration-management tools**: at least one public, open-source tool lets a student manage registration through an AI agent rather than the official portal, described in its own documentation as built "for students of IIIT Hyderabad." Its existence is confirmed; its adoption and effect on behavior are not.

Late diners hold a small but routine informal power over the operator. Kadamba's breakfast is meant to close at 9:30, yet when the front door closes, students come in through the back door and demand food, and the operator says it cannot refuse them (single-sourced; 25 came after close on one biryani day). The records show this is not occasional: 9.2% of April breakfast scans, about 37 a day, fall at or after 9:30 (confirmed). An earlier version of this brief described the operator reopening the door at its own discretion; that was revised, because the operator describes a grace period no rule grants and it cannot decline.

The mess operator also exercises informal power beyond its formal remit. Kadamba cooks about 30 portions for 30 to 50 people from outside the student registration, such as night-shift security who do not want to leave to eat, and about half of Kadamba's own staff also eat from the same food (single-sourced). An earlier version of this brief put these diners at 20 to 30 staff eating what is left after students; that was revised on the first-hand account. These are accommodations that no rule grants and no rule forbids, and each absorbs part of the gap between registration and turnout without it ever being reported. The people fed this way have no voice in how much is cooked.

## Incentive chains

1. Billing tied to registration, not attendance, removes any incentive for a student to register only when genuinely intending to attend, a structural cause, not a personal failing, of the registration-attendance gap. Confirmed, and now quantified from records: registrations at Kadamba breakfast stay flat across the week, Sunday included, while turnout falls to 27.3% on Sundays, and veg registrants attend at 32.4% against 48.5% for non-veg registrants.
2. Skip Meal returns nothing to the student, while reselling on Mess Cell returns partial or full value, so a student with advance notice has a rational reason to prefer resale over the system's own designed tool for the same situation. Confirmed directly by real interview data (six respondents: zero used Skip Meal for a return of value; four used Mess Cell in some form).
3. Walk-in pricing set roughly 70% above the registered rate (₹81 against ₹48 for a vegetarian breakfast) creates a standing incentive to register even under real uncertainty about attendance, inflating registration counts independent of any later no-show. All six respondents interviewed register in advance, none day-by-day. Walk-in demand at breakfast is real but small: the Yuktāhār breakfast register records between none and twelve unregistered, paid, faculty and staff diners a day, usually four or fewer, with twelve on the peak day, nine of them paid (confirmed), which fits students choosing to register rather than pay at the door.
4. The Mess Office's confirmed incentive to reduce its own workload produced a policy, tightened cancellation rules, that reduces student flexibility, plausibly increasing rather than decreasing the pattern it targeted. A candidate case of a fix that fails; not yet confirmed by a governance-side account of its actual effect.
5. Academic administration experiences none of the consequences of the class/mess overlap, so the issue never reaches its agenda by any mechanism evidenced so far, and persists with no actor positioned to resolve it unilaterally. Confirmed.
6. The operator's payment basis decides which side of the gap it sits on. If operators are paid per registered plate, the operator is paid for the whole registration but cooks only a fraction of it (70% at Kadamba and 70% to 80% at Bakul, single-sourced), so the gap is margin for the operator and it has no financial reason to push for accurate registration. If operators are paid per served plate, the operator bears the cost of every portion cooked and not eaten, and has every reason to want accurate registration. No operator has stated a position on the cut-off either way. Kadamba's remark that it cooks on registrations because it is "getting paid for it" leans toward the first reading but does not settle it. One operator now reports its own side: the Bakul manager says monthly payment is based on the number of plates served (single-sourced; the word reaches this project through a team member's normalized summary of the conversation, not a transcript). The two statements are in tension and are left unresolved here. Contracts may differ by operator; "served" may be the summary's word for plates billed rather than plates eaten; or Kadamba's remark may describe a duty rather than a payment formula. If Bakul is paid per plate eaten, it bears the cost of the food it holds against a shortage and accepts that cost, which would mean it judges a shortage costlier than waste, and a change that let students leave the booking would not cut its revenue (candidate). Bakul's report is not generalized to the other operators. Under per-registered-plate billing, both Yuktāhār's profit goal and Kadamba's fixed ratio gain from cooking less than is registered; they differ in whether the saving is pursued through records or through a fixed rule. Both readings remain candidate; this is the single most important unresolved incentive question in this analysis.

## Bargaining power and dependency

Students depend on operators for food itself; operators do not depend on any individual student, a strongly asymmetric relationship consistent with a walk-in pricing structure that requires no negotiation. Operators depend on registration only as a ceiling. Their real planning input is their own books at Yuktāhār, a fixed ratio at Kadamba, and at Bakul a ratio the manager adjusts week by week from historical attendance but keeps at about 70% of registrations or more, even on low weekends. This revises an earlier version of this paragraph, which treated Bakul's ratio as fixed like Kadamba's. Neither Kadamba's ratio nor Bakul's floor is calibrated to recorded breakfast turnout. Bakul's manager expects at most about 60% of registered students at breakfast, below both his first batch and his floor, so on his own numbers the first batch exceeds turnout by design (candidate); no Bakul records exist to size the gap. At Kadamba, a first batch of about 70% of registrations against 37.1% turnout in April implies roughly 1.9 times over-preparation before any top-up (candidate, combining records with a single-sourced ratio). Because the ratio is applied to veg and non-veg separately, the gap differs by stream: 70% of about 757 daily veg registrations against about 245 veg meals eaten is about 2.2 times, and 70% of about 315 non-veg registrations against about 153 eaten is about 1.4 times (candidate). The operator reports only 5% to 10% daily waste, on an unstated base (single-sourced). That figure cannot be squared with this surplus unless most of it is eaten by the outside diners and staff, held and reheated into later service, offset by top-ups smaller than the first batch implies, or measured on a different base; which of these holds is not known, and the tension is left open here. Whichever absorber takes the surplus is an actor with a stake in the ratio staying where it is. An earlier version of this paragraph said vendors depend on the Mess Office for a forecast fixed four days ahead; that was revised, because operators procure on their own cycles (groceries fortnightly, vegetables two to three times a week and milk daily at Yuktāhār, confirmed; at Kadamba dry goods one week ahead, perishables such as milk, fruit, meat, eggs and most vegetables the day before, and some vegetables two to three days ahead, single-sourced) and adjust cooking on the day. The Mess Office depends on students to register accurately, but nothing in the billing structure requires accuracy. Academic administration depends on nothing in the mess system, and nothing in the mess system depends on it: not an imbalance, but a total absence of mutual dependency. The Mess Cell market and the formal system are similarly independent of one another, though the market may be quietly absorbing some of the formal system's waste problem without either side acknowledging the relationship.

Operators show clear bargaining power over their own suppliers: Bakul's operator switched paneer brands after complaints, and Yuktāhār holds a per-item norm for impurities in raw stock, complains to or changes the supplier when it is exceeded, visits the mills that make its oil, and has used only rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about six months (all single-sourced). No instance of an operator bargaining with the institution is evidenced. No operator has stated an objection to the four-day cut-off, and none is recorded raising it with a rule owner.

## Historical precedent

**Bakul's creation**, triggered by Kadamba's non-vegetarian capacity reaching its ceiling, resulted in a new mess being established, a confirmed structural response to a limit. Who raised the issue and through what process remain unknown.

**"Let Them Eat Frogs" (November 2024)**: a frog was found in Kadamba's chicken biryani, and the Mess Office responded by unilaterally cancelling non-vegetarian meals at Kadamba without officially communicating the real reason, prompting debate across multiple mail threads. Secondhand and not full-text-verified, but a clean example of unilateral, opaque Mess Office decision-making, preceding the December episode below by about a month.

**The December 2024 cancellation-policy tightening**, triggered by Mess Office workload concerns compounded by a Kadamba renovation, was issued with no evidenced Committee involvement or student consultation beforehand. Organized pushback did occur: Ping ran a student opinion form that returned majority disappointment, and Parliament's mess-related representatives reportedly held two meetings with the Mess Office before the policy took effect, then told Ping they were pushing back. The policy was implemented regardless, a real, partial answer to whether organized student action was effective here, though not yet confirmed to be the same episode referenced elsewhere in this project as an "organized email effort." The current cap of five cancellations per meal type per month, made at least four days ahead, may be the tightened rule's present form; the cap itself is confirmed from the published rule and from students' own experience.

**Non-compulsory registration during shortages and holidays (date unknown)**: during an LPG shortage, and during holidays and fests, registration was made non-compulsory and the cancellation cap raised from five to ten (single-sourced). The standing model returned once the trigger passed. This is the one evidenced case of the registration rules being loosened, and it shows the rule owner can change them quickly when a supply shock or a predictable low-attendance period forces it.

**The Yuktāhār waste-tracking change (August to December of the operator's first year)** is the one evidenced case of an actor in this system deliberately changing its own practice to reduce waste. The operator's management introduced per-item waste recording; staff first resisted, fearing blame ("they are catching us"), and by December staff themselves reported that waste had fallen (confirmed). The project managers deliberately made the weighing blame-free and discussed it every day in the team meeting (single-sourced), and that is the condition under which measurement changed behaviour. The operator also tried and dropped the Zoho ERP system, because "prediction will be based on our data only; I want to remain close to the data" (confirmed), and because paying lakhs of rupees for prediction made no sense when the data already sat in its books and its staff are not digitally inclined (single-sourced). The change stayed inside the kitchen. Nothing shows it reaching any rule owner.

**"Water Mess" (2023)**: a different domain (drinking-water quality) at the same institution. Complaints raised through WhatsApp and email went unaddressed for months despite Parliament requesting water testing, until 38 or more confirmed typhoid cases forced a reactive response. The same complaint-then-ignored-then-crisis-then-reactive-fix shape recurs across two different domains at this institution.

For breakfast timing and class-timing coordination specifically, no precedent could be found. This is recorded as precedent currently unknown, not assumed to be zero.

# Identify Who Can Influence Decisions and Who Cannot

## Power asymmetries

- **The Mess Office has more power than students over registration and cancellation rules**, because it holds unilateral operational authority and students hold none; rules can be tightened, as in December 2024, without a confirmed consultation step.
- **Operators have more power than students over food quality and quantity on a given morning**, because operators decide how much of each registration is cooked, when to re-cook and what to reuse. Students reach operators only through a weak, informal channel: menu selection through a student committee, and complaints at the counter.
- **Operators adjust on the day; students are locked in four days ahead.** The registration cut-off binds students about four days before service, while operators top up within the same meal (Kadamba keeps raw material ready to cook in 10 to 15 minutes; Bakul phones a kitchen 20 to 40 minutes' drive away, with the full lead time unmeasured) and, at Kadamba, order perishables one day ahead and dry goods one week ahead (single-sourced). The flexibility asymmetry runs against the party who knows least about the morning. An earlier version of this brief described the four-day lead time as capping every actor's responsiveness equally; that was revised on the operations evidence.
- **Academic administration has more power than every mess-governance actor combined over the one variable, class start time, most likely to resolve the core overlap**, because it holds total, independent authority over its own domain and mess-side actors have no formal channel to it at all.
- **Students have more power than any formal actor over the accuracy of registration data**, because they are the only source of that data and nothing requires it to be accurate. Yuktāhār replaces it with its own records, Kadamba discounts it by a fixed ratio, and Bakul by a weekly-adjusted ratio with a floor; either way the correction is private, so its inaccuracy shows up in the kitchen and nowhere else.
- **The institution exercises routine oversight power over the kitchen every meal, but only over safety and taste.** Temperature checks, the 72-hour sample, the display plate, the joint tasting, weekly quality control and daily water readings all look at whether food is safe and good; no check anywhere covers how much is cooked or wasted. Formal power is present in the kitchen daily and pointed at a different variable from the one producing the gap.

## Consequence and control

**High consequence, low power:** students bear the most visible cost of this system's central problem (wasted food, lost money, energy crashes) while holding no confirmed authority over any of the domains that produce it: not billing structure, not cancellation rules, not class timing. Registration is mandatory and automatic, applied even when a student is off campus or on holiday, and a student cannot switch off the charge beyond five cancellations a month made four days ahead (confirmed). Skip Meal tells the kitchen not to prepare but does not remove the charge. The standing booking is therefore a default the student cannot opt out of, not a choice. Their influence over menu content is real but runs through a committee whose identity is unconfirmed.

**Consequence absorbed downstream, no power:** operators absorb over-preparation and closing-rush shortages. The Kadamba scan records show one continuously building crowd, with 26.5% of all breakfast scans falling between 9:15 and 9:30, just before close, and the rate still rising at 9:29 (confirmed), and the Kadamba and Yuktāhār operators describe the closing rush as the moment they re-cook or hold extra food in reserve. The Bakul manager places his main rush earlier, at 8:00 to 8:30, with about 10 to 15 people arriving in the last 10 minutes, yet still aims to have food for about 50 people at 9:20 (single-sourced; no Bakul scan records). This revises an earlier version of this paragraph, which said all three operators describe a closing rush: arrival curves differ by hall. A likely reading is that late in the window a top-up from the Hafizpet kitchen could not arrive before close, so food already held is Bakul's only hedge against late arrivals, and the reserve is several times the late tail its manager describes (candidate). The Kadamba operator's own rule of thumb, about 150 diners in the last 10 minutes of any meal, corroborates the crest (single-sourced); the records remain the measure, with about 79 scans a day, 19.8% of the total, between 9:20 and 9:29. Whether over-preparation costs the operator money depends on the unconfirmed payment basis. At Kadamba, 30 to 50 outside diners and about half of the operator's own staff eat from the same food (single-sourced), an uncounted absorber of first-batch surplus with no voice in how much is cooked. The garbage contractor removes the rest at Kadamba, where the operator does no composting (single-sourced), and it has no voice in the system. Cooked food is usable for only three to four hours, so cooked surplus cannot be carried to another day, while Yuktāhār reuses what it can as a new dish, curd or batter (confirmed). Within a meal, Kadamba's surplus is not simply discarded: in the team's own observation, food there is cooked ahead, reheated and moved between vessels repeatedly and degrades fast, so diners bear part of the cost of over-preparation as lower quality (candidate chain; no link to skipping is evidenced). Bakul's diners bear a different quality cost. Labour-heavy items such as aloo paratha may be started around 3 AM at the Hafizpet kitchen for arrival around 7 AM, and the insulated boxes that hold food at about 60 to 72 °C keep it hot but, in the manager's account, let steam soften the parathas (single-sourced). The food kept longest for late diners is likely the softest (candidate).

**Low consequence, high power:** Academic administration holds total authority over the one variable most likely to resolve the core overlap and experiences none of the consequences of leaving it unresolved; nothing in its formal mandate connects it to mess outcomes, which is the structural reason the overlap can persist indefinitely.

## Coalitions and conflicts

Students and operators may share an interest in a shorter registration cut-off. At Kadamba, registration closes about four days ahead while perishables are ordered the day before and dry goods a week ahead (single-sourced); the perishable tier falls after the cut-off and the dry-goods tier before it, so the four-day lock serves no evidenced procurement need of the operator; students, for whom the four-day lock is the source of most unwanted registrations, stand to gain from a shorter one. No operator has stated a position on the cut-off, and neither side has a channel to the rule owner, so this remains a potential coalition (candidate), not an evidenced one. If operators turn out to be paid per registered plate, their financial interest in accurate registration may be weak; this counter-reading is also candidate. Bakul may be the exception: if it is paid on plates eaten, as its manager's report of "plates served" may mean, fewer unwanted registrations would not cut its revenue, and its position on a shorter cut-off would likely be neutral or favourable (candidate, resting on the unresolved payment basis).

A second conflict runs over the criteria for the menu. In the Bakul manager's account, students choose combinations for balanced diet, protein and individual preference, while he would choose for traditional pairing, regional familiarity and what people actually eat. Only his side has been heard; how students weigh these criteria, and whether Bakul residents share his view of pairings, is untested. It is recorded as a conflict between the holder of formal selection power and the holder of consumption knowledge, not as evidence of what students prefer.

Convenience (student interest) against reduced workload (the Mess Office's confirmed incentive) is not a symmetric negotiation: the Office has demonstrated unilateral authority to resolve this conflict in its own favor, and did so in December 2024. Billing certainty against attendance uncertainty (a real feature of student life, not a defect) is a structurally misaligned conflict, not a negotiable one; no rule change on either side removes the underlying uncertainty, only shifts who bears its cost.

## Information flows: who holds what, and where it stops

Information in this system breaks in four places, and each break sits at the operator.

- **Upward, from kitchen to policy.** Yuktāhār keeps the richest record set in the system, from the Wastage Book and weekday attendance register to the grocery, stock, vegetable and utility books, and every one of them terminates inside the kitchen. No source describes any route from these books to CDS, the Mess Office, any Committee, CFS or billing. The institution's one routine channel into the kitchen, its safety and tasting checks, carries nothing about quantity back out. The operator measures the gap locally and repeatedly and acts on it only inside the kitchen. The CFS Chair's own awareness of low breakfast turnout never reached a registration or billing discussion either (confirmed).
- **Downward, from institute to kitchen.** Exams, events and holidays reach the Yuktāhār kitchen only when someone raises them in the planning meeting (confirmed). There is no formal calendar feed.
- **From student to kitchen.** Whether a Skip Meal declaration changes the number Kadamba cooks from is in conflict between two sources. Earlier project findings say Skip Meal declarations reach the kitchen; the Kadamba operator's stated practice is to cook on registrations only (single-sourced). Skip Meal may adjust the count the operator sees while the operator treats whatever count it sees as the obligation, but this is not established, and the question is left open.
- **Laterally, between operators.** Operators do not share figures. The Bakul operator believes Kadamba sees 90% to 95% of registered students (single-sourced, figure unclear in the recording, and possibly about lunch or dinner); the April scan records show 37.1% for Kadamba breakfast (confirmed).
- **Within the holder.** Holding the data is not the same as seeing it at the breakfast level. The Yuktāhār site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50% (confirmed as his stated belief); his own September register shows 17.7% to 40.2%, with a mean of about 28% (confirmed from records). His lunch figure, above 70% on most days, matches the register. The Bakul manager estimates that at most about 60% of registered students come to breakfast (single-sourced); no Bakul records exist to check it. If Bakul resembles the halls whose records exist, where breakfast turnout runs at about 28% to 37%, his 60% is an upper bound rather than the norm (candidate). He explains the shortfall partly by taste and menu and partly by students not waking or being reluctant to come; that is his view, untested with Bakul residents, and only the second reason matches what interviewed students most often say.

## Leverage analysis

Power and leverage are treated as distinct: power is the capacity to influence a decision; leverage is the capacity for a relatively small action to produce a disproportionately large system effect.

An earlier version of this section said the Mess Office holds both. That was revised. The Mess Office holds power and leverage over student flexibility rules, shown by a small operational change producing a system-wide behavioral effect in December 2024, but that is a different lever from waste. The operator holds high leverage and high local power over waste with no rule-making power at all, and two levels of leverage need to be kept apart. Parameter leverage, the first-batch ratio, the top-up threshold and the batching of each item, is held by the operator; at Kadamba the ratio is a standing Prism rule and the fastest lever, the in-service top-up, is held by the supervisor, while at Yuktāhār both sit with the daily team meeting, and at Bakul both sit with the manager, bounded below by his own no-shortage floor. This leverage is fast and local and needs no rule change, but tightening it alone makes the gap even less visible upstream, and cutting the first batch would take food from the outside diners and staff who now eat from it. Information-flow leverage, routing the operator's records and the CDS scan records to whoever owns the cut-off, the cancellation rules and billing, is held by nobody at present. The data already exists; the flow does not. The institution's per-meal tasting and weekly quality control already put it in the kitchen every day, a route that currently carries safety and taste only. Operator culture is a further candidate lever: under one billing structure, a management that ties waste to profit built a record system and a blame-free measurement habit, while a management that treats registration as an obligation did not.

Academic administration holds power without leverage over this specific problem. Students, individually, hold neither, but the Mess Cell market demonstrates that students collectively possess real, functioning organizational capacity, and this capacity has also been directed at governance directly at least once. This is better described as **exercised-but-unmeasured leverage** than purely sleeping leverage.

## Sleeping stakeholders

**Academic administration** remains the clearest case: total authority over class timing, zero relationship to any mess-governance actor, and no incentive pulling it toward engagement.

**The Mess Committee's chair**, if the secondhand report of a faculty chair holds, is a more subtle case: a person with plausible informal, collegial access to other faculty and academic-side actors that no student-only channel possesses, with no evidence this access has ever been used to raise the mess/academic overlap.

**The operator's records** are a sleeping resource rather than a sleeping stakeholder. At Yuktāhār they are already kept per day and per item, reconciled book against book with mismatches raised in the daily meeting, and used inside the kitchen, though the books do not always agree with one another, and no one outside the kitchen has asked for them.

**Students, collectively**, occupy a genuinely ambiguous position: the Mess Cell market proves organizing capacity exists at the peer-to-peer level, and an organized effort has also been directed at governance itself at least once. What that effort targeted and whether it produced any actual change are both unknown. The open question has narrowed from "has this capacity ever been aimed at governance" to "what happened the one time it was."

# Implications for System Behaviour

The distribution of power described above does not merely coexist with the Breakfast Paradox; it plausibly produces and sustains it. Billing without an attendance incentive removes any structural pressure on the one actor (the student) whose behavior most directly determines the outcome. The one confirmed governance action on record was taken to serve an internal operational goal, not the paradox itself, and may have tightened rather than loosened the underlying pattern. The actor best positioned to resolve the paradox's second major driver, the schedule overlap, remains entirely disconnected from every governance actor who might otherwise raise it, confirmed to reflect inattention rather than a refusal.

The most concrete stabilizer found so far sits in the kitchen. Each operator runs its own balancing loop: cook a fraction of registrations, watch the counter, and top up when it runs low. This loop is confirmed at Yuktāhār and single-sourced at Kadamba and Bakul. It keeps the counter from running empty and keeps the gap from ever surfacing as a crisis. The Bakul manager states the loop's goal outright: "there should not be a shortage of food, even if some wastage occurs," and he keeps food for about 70% of registrations even on weekends, when breakfast attendance is very low (single-sourced). A run-out is visible and routes a complaint from the CDS team through the manager towards the managing director; a surplus is silent. The floor therefore reads as the kitchen's own answer to a system that reacts only to what is loud, a stabilizer on the operator side that accepts silent waste to avoid a loud shortage (candidate). At Kadamba the surplus the loop leaves is further absorbed by outside diners and staff who eat from the same food, and by holding and reheating within service, which may explain why the waste the operator reports is small; that explanation is not established. Because its records never travel upward, the rule-setters receive no signal that anything needs changing. The system holds itself in place not because nobody measures the gap but because the one actor who measures it daily cannot change the rules that create it, and no route carries that measurement to those who can.

The one channel that could plausibly generate collective pressure for change, the students themselves, has been aimed at governance at least once, through an organized effort whose exact subject and outcome remain to be confirmed. The paradox may therefore be sustained less by an absence of channels than by channels that exist but whose effectiveness has never been measured or followed up on, alongside one channel, from kitchen records to policy, that does not exist at all.

# Key Findings

1. **Registration without an attendance incentive.** Billing tied to registration count, not attendance, creates no pressure on students to register only when genuinely intending to attend. This is the most solidly evidenced structure in this system, and records now size it: 37.1% breakfast turnout at Kadamba in April 2026, with registrations flat across every weekday.
2. **Unilateral operational authority in the Mess Office.** The Office issued a real policy change (December 2024) with no evidenced Committee or student involvement beforehand. Whether this generalizes to a standing pattern needs governance-source confirmation.
3. **Total independence of Academic administration from mess governance.** No formal mandate connects the two systems in either direction, confirmed to reflect inattention rather than active refusal. The disconnection extends to the kitchen, which learns of exams and holidays only by word of mouth.
4. **An input nothing requires to be accurate.** Students control registration and bear no cost for its inaccuracy. The operators, the only actors who plan against it, correct it privately in opposite ways: Yuktāhār replaces it with its own records, Kadamba cooks a fixed 70% of it as its obligation, and Bakul adjusts a 70% to 80% share week by week while keeping food for about 70% even on low weekends (single-sourced). The institution's scan records measure the inaccuracy, but no rule responds to them, and the institution never sees any of these corrections. This revises the earlier finding that every other actor's forecasting depends on registration, and the later one that every operator distrusts registration.
5. **Skip Meal as a dominated strategy.** Skip Meal returns nothing to the student; resale returns partial or full value, making resale the rational choice whenever advance notice exists, confirmed by real interview data.
6. **Exercised-but-unmeasured collective leverage.** Real self-organizing capacity exists (Mess Cell) and has also been aimed at governance directly at least once; the December 2024 Ping-survey-and-Parliament-negotiation episode is a strong but unconfirmed candidate match.
7. **A confirmed feedback input with an unconfirmed output.** Three student-facing channels (in-app rating, email, Ping coverage) carry input into the system, and complaints demonstrably reach the operator's manager; none has a confirmed instance of producing a rule change.
8. **A data-rich, authority-poor operator.** The operator with the richest records, Yuktāhār, measures the gap daily, per item and per weekday, in books it reconciles every day, yet it cannot change the cut-off, cancellation, billing or menu rules that create it, and no route carries its measurement to those who can.
9. **A flexibility asymmetry against the student.** The registration cut-off binds students about four days out, while operators top up within the same meal and, at Kadamba, order perishables a day ahead (single-sourced). The party who knows least about the morning is locked in earliest, and beyond five cancellations a month it cannot release the booking at all (confirmed).
10. **Oversight pointed at the wrong variable.** The institution is present in the Kadamba kitchen every meal through temperature checks, a retained sample, the display plate and joint tasting, and weekly through quality control, but every check covers safety and taste; none covers how much is cooked or wasted.
11. **Two operators, one billing rule, opposite stances.** Yuktāhār, with an explicit profit and waste goal, a flat team and blame-free measurement, learns from its own books; Kadamba, run by Prism through a layered chain, cooks a fixed ratio of registrations because it is paid for them. That the difference follows management culture rather than the payment rule is candidate, and it makes operator culture a candidate lever. Bakul adds a third stance, shortage first with waste accepted, and its manager reports payment on plates served, in tension with Kadamba's remark; if confirmed, the operators do not all share one billing rule (unresolved).
12. **Menu expertise without menu authority.** The Bakul manager holds consumption knowledge by item and about 20 years' experience with North Indian food, and disputes the pairings students choose, yet has no formal say over the menu (single-sourced). His view of what residents would eat is untested with Bakul residents; the finding is the mismatch between who knows what is eaten and who decides what is served.

# Outstanding Data Collection
