# Overview

The Stakeholder Map says who is involved. This trace says what actually happens, step by step, including the workarounds: how the system actually operates rather than how it is supposed to operate. It follows one breakfast from the student's registration, through the operator's planning and cooking, to the counter, the close, and what happens to the food nobody eats.

Three timescales matter and are kept distinct rather than collapsed into one loop. Registration is locked about four days ahead on a weekly rhythm, and, because every student is registered automatically, it works in practice as a standing month-long default rather than a choice. Cooked quantity is set the day before, in the operator's planning step, as a share of registrations at Kadamba, from the mess's own past records at Yuktāhār, and at Bakul as an item-dependent share that the manager adjusts week by week from past attendance. The decision to eat is made on the morning itself, by each student, and the operator responds to it live at the counter; at Yuktāhār the operator also re-forecasts the rest of the meal from its first half hour, so the morning timescale holds an operator forecast as well as a reaction.

An earlier version of this trace covered only the student-facing half and stopped at the kitchen door, because no source then showed how a registration became a cooked quantity. That limit is revised. Interviews with the operators of three messes, the photographed planning and attendance registers of one of them, a month of timestamped breakfast scans from Kadamba, a walkthrough of the Kadamba kitchen, and the team's own first-hand account of both the Kadamba and Yuktāhār visits now make the operator half visible. The two best-evidenced operators run on different logics, so this trace follows each one end to end rather than merging them into one "operator". The third, Bakul, cooked off site, is now traced end to end as well, on its manager's account alone, since no Bakul records exist. The parts that remain unseen are narrow and are listed at the end.

# Trace Decisions and Interactions

Registration is locked about four days ahead. The CDS handbook requires both registration and cancellation at least four days in advance, and the Kadamba manager describes it as "weekly compulsory". Students, however, describe registering "for a month": three of six respondents specifically say so. The two accounts fit together. The portal locks on a four-day, weekly rhythm, and students set a standing booking once and leave it running. A team member's own experience as a Kadamba student adds that registration is automatic and mandatory, including while off campus and over holidays, and that the only exit is five cancellations per meal type per month made at least four days ahead: "we don't have any opportunity to nullify it." The booking is a default, not a choice. The Kadamba breakfast records for April 2026 confirm the result: registrations sit flat at about 1,070 per day on every weekday, Sunday included (range 928 to 1,123), while actual turnout moves from 42.1% on Tuesdays to 27.3% on Sundays. Registration does not track the daily decision to eat.

## Decision points: operators

The three operators forecast in three different ways. Kadamba cooks a fixed share of registrations and tops up reactively, with no learning record beyond its per-dish gram standards. Yuktāhār learns from its own books, looking up the matching past week before service and re-projecting from the first half hour during it. Bakul sits between the two. This revises an earlier reading that Bakul's operator resembles Kadamba, which rested on a noisy recording that gave only the ratio: its manager varies the share by item and adjusts it week by week from past attendance, by his own judgement, but never below a floor he keeps against shortage. Whether any of that history is written down is not known.

Two readings follow from the manager's own numbers, and both are candidates. His first batch (70 to 80%) and his weekend floor (about 70%) both sit above the highest breakfast turnout he expects (at most about 60%), so the batch exceeds turnout by design; if Bakul resembles the halls with records, where breakfast runs at 28 to 37%, the gap is larger, and without records it cannot be sized. And the late reserve of about 50 is several times the 10 to 15 late arrivals he describes, the difference being insurance against a shortage that a top-up from 8 km away could not fix in time.

**Menu choice, all messes.** The student committee chooses from an operator-supplied menu and the operator follows it. Operators see per-item waste (at Kadamba uttapam, set dosa and upma waste, while puri, bonda, vada and idli move) and can shift quantities between items, as Kadamba did in cutting ragi idli and increasing plain idli, but cannot change the menu itself. The Bakul manager asks for a say, and his objection is to pairings rather than dishes: kachori, in his view, belongs with chole, and he would serve Raj Kachori with aloo curry where the menu lists chana. He describes students choosing from balance, protein and carbohydrate and personal preference, and himself from traditional regional combinations, taste and what people actually eat, drawing on about 20 years with North Indian food; he also mentioned a target of about 30 to 40 g of protein per meal, set by an unknown party. He also sees which items go: he cites pav bhaji as drawing more students, and muesli and Chocos as eaten readily (single-sourced). This is the operator's view of the menu; whether Bakul residents share it is untested.

## Yuktāhār: the book system as a process

Yuktāhār runs on paper books, each photographed by the team and explained by the operator. Every staff member does every job, so "storekeeper" is a function people rotate through rather than a fixed post, and each book is cross-checked by a peer.

The fast one samples the part of the morning least like the close. Applying the Kadamba April curve as an illustration, the first half hour carries about 60 of about 398 daily scans, and projecting that rate flat across the two-hour window gives about 240, some 40% below the real total, because a quarter of diners arrive in the last quarter hour (candidate; Yuktāhār's own arrival curve is not measured).

# Identify Negotiation and Escalation Mechanisms

The earlier version of this section said no escalation path was visible. From the student side that still holds for rules and timing: asked whether they or anyone they know tried to get a mess rule or timing changed, every one of the five new respondents said no. The operator interviews revise the picture in one respect. An operational complaint path exists and works, for same-incident fixes and supplier changes. The two operators route it differently: Kadamba through a command chain, Yuktāhār through its daily meeting with no hierarchy.

The internal operator chain in the earlier version (staff, supervisor, manager, operations manager, director) came from a clip that was cut off and could not be tied to one mess. # Track Exceptions, Workarounds and Dependencies

![Exception and Workaround Paths](assets/d9-exception-paths.png)

## Operator exceptions

- **Scanner failure.** When the QR system fails, Kadamba staff write down ID and name by hand. The April export shows no sign of bulk entry (never more than two scans in the same second), so hand-logged diners may be absent from the records altogether, a candidate undercount.
- **Gas shortage.** During the LPG shortage Kadamba cut quantities a little and bought some items from outside. This is the first operator account of the shortage itself; its cause is still single-sourced.
- **Equipment failure.** The Kadamba bread grill is broken and has not been replaced, which removes an item from service regardless of demand.
- **Events, exams and holidays.** Nothing formal tells the kitchen about them. They reach the Yuktāhār planning meeting only if someone mentions them, and hackathons and exams lower turnout in ways the site head calls unavoidable.
- **Supplier quality.** At Yuktāhār, impurities above the expected grams per raw item are raised in the daily meeting and lead to a complaint or a change of supplier. At Bakul, paneer has been switched between three brands after complaints.
- **Biryani days.** Kadamba cooks biryani lunch at 100% of registrations rather than 70%, with a head chef brought in. The ratio therefore follows the operator's expectation of demand for a dish, not a fixed rule, and portion leakage (three pieces instead of two) still produces late run-outs even at full preparation.
- **Off-site transport.** Every Bakul top-up is a phone call, a cook at the Hafizpet kitchen and a drive of about 20 to 40 minutes, against a fixed close. An earlier reading put the lag at up to 30 minutes; the real lead time is not known. The manager's reserve of about 50 at 9:20 is, as a candidate reading, the consequence: late in the window only food already held can meet late arrivals.
- **Late-comers.** At Kadamba, when the front door closes at 9:30, late-comers enter by the back door and demand food, and staff do not refuse them. Last-minute registrations are described as "a pain". The speaker's wording covers both the 9:30 breakfast close and the 9:30 dinner close; scans after 9:30 on every April day support it at breakfast.
- **Records that disagree.** For breakfast on 3 September at Yuktāhār, the Wastage Book records 77 diners (28%) while the attendance register, on a partly obscured row, records 110 (40%). Which one feeds planning is not known. The two-book reconciliation rule covers materials, not diner counts, and these two books have different keepers (Ajita and the coordinator), which may be why the difference stands unreconciled (candidate).

The operator workarounds that sit alongside these (leftover reuse, outside diners and staff eating from the same food, holding and reheating surplus, late-door service, raw stock held for a quick top-up) are absorption mechanisms: each one soaks up part of the gap before it becomes visible, and each is locally beneficial. The outside diners and the half of Kadamba's staff who eat at the mess are real people fed by the surplus, so any change to the first batch has to plan for them explicitly. Holding and reheating is the exception among the absorbers: the team's own judgement is that Kadamba food is cooked ahead, reheated repeatedly and moved between vessels, so it degrades fast. That links over-preparation to food quality as an open chain; whether poorer quality then feeds skipping is not evidenced. Bakul's hot holding carries a similar cost for a different reason: food is kept hot from an early start through transport to the counter, and the Bakul manager says steam softens parathas on the way. As a candidate reading, the food kept back for late arrivals is also the food held longest. Yuktāhār also tried and dropped Zoho ERP for grocery management and rush prediction, for two reasons: paying "lakhs of rupees" for prediction made no sense when the data already sits in its books ("prediction will be based on our data only… I want to remain close to the data"), and staff are not digitally inclined, with too few people to digitise, so the office digitises later.

## Dependencies

Eating breakfast at the mess depends on waking with enough time and alertness, the single most commonly cited constraint; on the desired item still being available at arrival (run-outs confirmed by at least three of six respondents, directly or by reputation); on no conflicting class-attendance pressure (explicit for one respondent); and on the mess still being open (the 9:30 cut-off is the single most-requested change). The attendance-policy pressure depends on academic administration's own scheduling, which the governance analysis confirmed is disconnected from mess governance.

Item availability in turn depends on a chain this trace can now follow. An earlier version of this section said the Mess Office fixes each vendor's order four days out, so that nothing on the day could change what is cooked. That finding is revised by the operations evidence. The four-day lock applies to registration, not to cooking or buying. Groceries at Yuktāhār are bought fortnightly by the stock register, ahead of use, from many local shops and godowns; vegetables arrive two to three times a week and milk daily. Kadamba buys in three tiers: dry goods a week ahead, perishables the day before, some vegetables two to three days ahead. Cooked quantity is set the day before, as a share of registrations at Kadamba and from past records at Yuktāhār, and at Bakul as an item-dependent share adjusted weekly, and is adjusted live on the day through top-ups and re-cooks. At Bakul the first batch leaves the Hafizpet kitchen before any sign of the morning's turnout, and a top-up can only add food, slowly. The binding constraint on waste is therefore cooked food prepared ahead of demand, not raw procurement: raw stock left over is issued again from the store, while cooked food is usable for only 3 to 4 hours.

The operator side has its own dependencies: supplier cadence and quality; cooking gas, electricity and water, which Yuktāhār logs daily and Kadamba checks for water quality daily; equipment (the Kadamba roti line, multi-item cooker and tilting curry machine, and the broken grill); one person running all Kadamba inventory, a single point of failure; the matching-week Wastage Book page existing, which depends on the two-week menu rotation; the daily meeting as the only channel for event information and for exceptions at Yuktāhār; transport time from the Hafizpet kitchen to Bakul (about 8 km; about 20 minutes early in the morning, about 40 later), which bounds every Bakul top-up; inputs chosen for quality at Yuktāhār (rock salt and cold-pressed oil for about the past six months, with the operator visiting the mills); and staff numbers. Yuktāhār has 16 kitchen staff and two project managers from ABC Hospitality Services, with some site staff sharing project-management work and separate tasting, cutting and cleaning teams, all without a hierarchy. Kadamba has about ten chefs, with Nazir as main veg chef and a head chef for biryani days.

Where uneaten food finally goes is only partly settled. The CFS Chair described production and plate waste going via a garbage collector to an outside compost facility. The Kadamba manager, asked about composting, said there is none: the bin goes to the garbage contractor (single-sourced). The earlier project understanding that composting absorbs the physical surplus is revised accordingly: at Kadamba the surplus is absorbed by outside diners and staff eating it, by holding and reheating into later service, by late-comer top-ups and by the bin, and whether the contractor composts it off site is unverified. Yuktāhār's disposal route after its leftovers are reused is not stated in any source. At Bakul some leftovers return to the Hafizpet kitchen and the rest is thrown (single-sourced); where it goes after that is not stated.

# Key Findings

1. The registration-attendance gap is not one mechanism but several: habitual monthly over-registration made the default by automatic registration, scarcity-driven defensive registration at Kadamba, automatic allocation, and simple forgetting. The Kadamba April breakfast records show their combined effect. Registration is an automatic standing booking, a default rather than a choice, flat across the week while turnout varies by weekday; it is not a daily demand signal.
2. Peer influence, previously treated as a meaningful lever, is disconfirmed as a decision-determining factor by six real respondents; it is at most a motivational add-on.
3. This finding is revised. The earlier version described every operator as applying a private ratio (believed 50%, about 70%, 70 to 80%), and a later version found two forecasting logics, grouping Bakul with Kadamba. There are in fact three. Kadamba cooks a fixed ratio of registrations (70%) and tops up reactively, keeping no learning record beyond per-dish gram standards. Bakul varies an item-dependent share (70 to 80%) week by week from past attendance by the manager's judgement, never below a floor of about 70% kept against shortage, so its correction closes against surplus only down to that floor; whether it rests on any written record is not known. Yuktāhār has no fixed ratio: it learns from its own books by matching week and weekday and re-projects from the first half hour of service, though the site head's belief that breakfast runs at about 50% differs from his own register (about 28%). None of the fixed ratios is set against actual breakfast turnout, which is confirmed at 37.1% for Kadamba in April. The gap is measured, locally and repeatedly: Yuktāhār records registered against eaten and waste per item at every meal. What is missing is any route from that measurement into registration or billing rules, and even inside the best-documented mess the fast correction samples the calm start of service rather than the crest at the close.
4. **Broken restock promises**: staff say an item will return before close and it doesn't. This is a trust and service-quality finding first reported by students. The operator side offers a candidate mechanism: a re-cook cut-off that leaves late run-outs unfilled, top-ups that take 10 to 15 minutes on site, and an unmeasured lead time from an off-site kitchen 20 to 40 minutes' drive away, against a close only minutes away, equipment failures, and items that float above their gram standard, together with a Kadamba chef's "wait 5 minutes" that matches what students describe hearing.
5. This finding is revised by the new records. Two respondents independently claimed that only around 30% of registered students attend and that the mess still struggles to serve even that smaller group, which earlier drafts read as a possible under-provisioning problem and held as an unverified impression. The attendance half is now confirmed: breakfast attendance is about 28% of registrations at Yuktāhār in September and 37.1% at Kadamba in April, a gap of 63 to 72%. The under-provisioning reading does not hold. Every first-batch share operators state sits well above that turnout, and at Bakul the share (70 to 80%) and the weekend floor (about 70%) sit above even the manager's own estimate of at most about 60% (candidate, as no Bakul records exist), and the Yuktāhār Wastage Book records leftovers, so the shortage students experience is not a shortage of total food. It is a timing and item-mix failure concentrated at the close: 26.5% of Kadamba scans arrive in the last quarter hour (the closing crest is recorded at Kadamba and described at Yuktāhār, while the Bakul manager describes an earlier peak at 8:00 to 8:30, so the curve differs by hall), the items students report running out (idli, puri, bhatura, fruit) include those the Kadamba operator says float above their gram standard (puri, idli) while the ones it says waste (uttapam, set dosa, upma) are left over, and late run-outs are not re-cooked. Over-preparation and run-outs happen on the same morning.
6. The escalation-path absence identified in the governance analysis is now corroborated from the operator side as well. An operational complaint path exists and works for same-incident fixes, through a command chain at Kadamba and a daily meeting at Yuktāhār; no path for changing rules or timing works for students or for operators.

**A tension the operator's own figures leave open.** Kadamba's stated practice and its stated waste do not yet add up. In April, Kadamba breakfast averaged about 1,072 registrations and 398 scans a day (confirmed). A 70% first batch is about 750 portions against about 400 eaten, a first-batch surplus of about 350 portions a day before any top-up (candidate arithmetic from the operator's ratio and the records). The operator reports about 5 to 10% daily wastage, with no stated base (single-sourced). The two are compatible only if most of the surplus goes somewhere other than the bin: to the 30 to 50 outside diners and half the staff, to food held and reheated into later service, to top-ups smaller than the ratio implies, or to a waste figure measured on a different base, such as all meals or plate waste only. Which of these holds is not known, and this trace does not assume one.
