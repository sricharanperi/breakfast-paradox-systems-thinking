# Overview

This map asks a different question than the Process Trace: not how the process works, but what pattern emerges when it repeats, across people and across time. It now rests on four kinds of evidence. The first is six student respondents describing their own breakfast habits. The second is two sets of records: thirty days of Kadamba breakfast registrations and timestamped scans from April 2026, and twenty days of Yuktāhār breakfast and lunch attendance registers from September 2026, photographed on site together with the rest of Yuktāhār's daily books. The third is interviews with the operators of three messes (Yuktāhār, Kadamba and a mess run by Vijayalakshmi Caterers), who described how they decide quantities and what has changed in their kitchens. The fourth is a first-hand account by a team member who was present for both the Kadamba and the Yuktāhār operator conversations and saw parts of each kitchen's routine. Numbers read off the records are stated as confirmed, and so is practice the team saw on site. Kadamba operator numbers, heard directly by that team member, are the operator's own single-sourced statements about his practice. Vijayalakshmi numbers come from noisy recordings and remain single-sourced. None of the Vijayalakshmi figures, and not Kadamba's waste percentage, whose base is unstated, is used as a headline figure.

Timescales supported by evidence:

- **Within one morning:** measured. Every Kadamba scan in April carries a timestamp, the Kadamba operator's own rule of thumb has the same shape as those records, and both kitchens described their morning routine from the 5:30 start to the close.
- **Across a week:** measured. Kadamba registrations and turnout are recorded for every weekday, and the Yuktāhār register covers nearly three weeks. One student respondent also describes the early-class trade-off directly. Kadamba's weekly quality inspection and one-week dry-goods purchasing run on the same cycle (single-sourced).
- **Across a fortnight:** confirmed. The menu repeats on a two-week cycle (weeks one and three identical, weeks two and four identical), and Yuktāhār's waste records are organised around that cycle.
- **Across a month:** measured for Kadamba breakfast in April. Monthly registration is also the universal student pattern: students are registered automatically and can cancel only five meals per meal type per month (confirmed). Yuktāhār reads the month's turnout pattern when it forecasts (single-sourced).
- **Across a semester:** recall only. Six respondents each give a semester-level trend statement for themselves; no multi-month records exist yet.
- **Across an operator year:** single-sourced for Yuktāhār in outline, with the Wastage Book pages as the records behind it. The current operator took over the previous year, began per-item waste weighing in August on deliberately blame-free terms, and by December heard staff themselves report lower waste. A project manager also took the staff to his farmhouse for training, at an unknown date. The switch to rock salt and cold-pressed oil dates from about six months before the visit. Software was tried and dropped at an unknown date.

No student records exist across multiple semesters, and no trend at that scale is claimed.

## Within-morning pattern: measured

![Crowd Build Through the Breakfast Window](assets/diagram8-crowd-curve.png)

The crowd curve was first drawn from student recall. What looked like two competing peak-crowding claims (one respondent naming 8:00-9:00, two others naming 9:00-9:30) was reconciled into one continuously building crowd that crests just before the 9:30 close, on the reasoning that the respondent naming the earlier window arrives early specifically to avoid the crowd. The Kadamba scan records now confirm that reading, and it is no longer a working hypothesis.

| Arrival slot (Kadamba, April) | Scans per day, average | Share of all scans | Cumulative share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before 7:30 | 11 | 2.7% | 2.7% |
| 7:30-7:44 | 38 | 9.6% | 12.3% |
| 7:45-7:59 | 23 | 5.7% | 18.0% |
| 8:00-8:14 | 29 | 7.3% | 25.3% |
| 8:15-8:29 | 33 | 8.3% | 33.6% |
| 8:30-8:44 | 33 | 8.2% | 41.9% |
| 8:45-8:59 | 36 | 8.9% | 50.8% |
| 9:00-9:14 | 54 | 13.5% | 64.3% |
| 9:15-9:29 | 105 | 26.5% | 90.8% |
| 9:30 and after | 37 | 9.2% | 100% |

The records add two features the recall version did not have. The curve opens with a burst: the first fifteen minutes after 7:30 carry more diners than any later slot until 9:00. It then runs as a flat plateau from 7:45 to 9:00 before climbing steeply, reaching about nine scans per minute in the final minute before 9:30. And roughly one scan in eleven happens after the nominal 9:30 close, 1,100 scans across the month, most of them before 9:35, with the latest at 10:07. The Kadamba operator says that once the front door closes, late-comers come in by the back door and demand to eat, and the mess cannot refuse them (single-sourced), which matches the records. The effective close is 9:35 to 9:45, not 9:30, and it is set informally at the door. There is no sign of bulk marking in the data: no more than two scans ever share the same second.

The Kadamba operator's own rule of thumb is about 100 diners in the opening phase, about 100 through the middle and about 150 in the last ten minutes, and "for any meal the highest flow is in the last 10 minutes" (single-sourced). That rule has the same shape as the records. The records put the last ten minutes before 9:30 at about 79 scans a day, or about 115 if the minutes just after the close are included, against about 398 a day in total. The rule slightly overstates the final surge and understates the total. The records remain the measurement.

The closing crest is not particular to Kadamba. The Yuktāhār site head says the rush is always at closing time, and his staff monitor the counter every thirty minutes from about forty-five minutes before close (single-sourced). The Vijayalakshmi manager places the breakfast peak at 8:30 to 9:00 and later, arriving in waves (single-sourced; no records exist for that mess).

The two timelines meet badly at one point. Yuktāhār projects the rest of the meal from its first half hour of service. On the measured Kadamba curve, the first half hour of service carries about 15% of the morning's diners and the last half hour about 40% (confirmed for Kadamba). If Yuktāhār's curve has the same shape, a straight projection from the first half hour reads low and pushes top-ups late, toward the crest (candidate; Yuktāhār keeps no arrival timestamps).

## Daily and weekday pattern: measured

The central behaviour-over-time pair for this system is registrations against actual diners, day by day.

| Kadamba breakfast, April 2026 | Registered per day | Ate per day | Share who ate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole month | 928 to 1,123 | 270 to 517 | 37.1% (11,941 of 32,162) |
| Monday | 1,095 | 450 | 41.1% |
| Tuesday | 1,065 | 448 | 42.1% |
| Wednesday | 1,064 | 394 | 37.0% |
| Thursday | 1,076 | 425 | 39.5% |
| Friday | 1,066 | 400 | 37.5% |
| Saturday | 1,070 | 373 | 34.8% |
| Sunday | 1,069 | 292 | 27.3% |

Registrations are flat. From 6 April onward they sit between 1,039 and 1,123 every day, Sunday included, while the number who eat swings with the day of the week. The three lowest days of the month were all Sundays (25.3%, 25.4% and 25.5%), the fourth Sunday came in at 32.7%, and Sunday is also the latest-arriving day, with its median scan at 9:08 to 9:15 against 8:36 to 9:03 on most weekdays. Weekdays together run at 39.3% and weekends at 31.1%. Non-vegetarian registrants turn up far more often than vegetarian ones (48.5% against 32.4%). Registration therefore does not follow the daily decision to eat; it is set once and left standing (confirmed).

Kadamba plans its first batch separately for veg and non-veg (single-sourced), but the two turn up at very different rates. If the same 70% applies to both, veg is prepared at about 2.2 times what veg registrants eat and non-veg at about 1.4 times (candidate; the operator did not say whether the ratio differs by type). The operator's worked example of 400 non-veg and 300 veg registrations does not match the April breakfast mix of roughly 750 veg and 320 non-veg a day, and is read as illustrative or a lunch figure (candidate).

The first week of April shows total registrations rising from 928 to about 1,070, almost entirely because non-vegetarian registrations climb from 137 on 1 April to around 300 by 6 April. The cause is unknown, and this is not read as a trend.

The Yuktāhār register shows the same shape at a second mess, and a comparison with lunch on the same days.

| Yuktāhār, 1-20 September 2026 | Share who ate | Registered per day |
|---|---|---|
| Breakfast (thirteen legible days) | 17.7% to 40.2%, averaging about 28% | 228 to 404 |
| Breakfast, Sundays | 18.0% to 24.2% | 228 to 269 |
| Lunch (all twenty days) | 56% to 89%, at least 70% on fifteen days | 161 to 376 |

Breakfast is the structurally worst meal. On the same days in the same mess, about seven in ten lunch registrants eat and fewer than three in ten breakfast registrants do (confirmed). The Yuktāhār register also shows a pattern the Kadamba export cannot: on Sundays, lunch registrations fall (165, 161 and 232 against 237 to 376 on other days), while Sunday breakfast registrations do not (267, 228 and 269). Students adjust their Sunday lunch booking and leave their Sunday breakfast booking in place, then do not come. That breakfast registration behaves as a standing default in a way lunch registration does not is a candidate pattern on three Sundays of evidence.

The two messes differ on Mondays: Monday is among Kadamba's best breakfast days in April and among Yuktāhār's worst in September (22.1% and 17.7%). Different months and different populations could each explain this; it is not resolved.

The kitchens keep a weekly rhythm of their own. Kadamba buys dry goods a week ahead and has a quality inspection every week (single-sourced). Yuktāhār's operator attributes a Wednesday rise in electricity use to grinding idli batter and uses it to set how long machines run. The meter partly supports this: Wednesday 2 September (134.4 units) and Wednesday 9 September (118.4) were high, but Saturday 12 September (136.2) was higher, so the Wednesday pattern is a candidate.

## Aggregate stability despite individual divergence

The aggregate does not move the way individuals do. Each seven-day block of Kadamba's April sits between 35% and 39%, with no drift across the month, and the Sunday trough recurs week after week. Individual respondents are rising, falling or at zero, and the records cannot see individuals because they carry no student identity. Both statements hold together: heterogeneous individual trajectories sum to a stable aggregate with a strong weekly rhythm (confirmed at the aggregate). The three-to-six-week decline one respondent reports cannot be tested against April data from another month with no identities, and April itself shows no within-month decline.

## Planning-horizon timeline: when each decision is fixed

The gap between registered and actual diners is produced partly by when each quantity is fixed relative to the meal.

| When | What is fixed | Where it is described |
|---|---|---|

| About two weeks ahead | Groceries are bought fortnightly; vegetables arrive two to three times a week. The operator also describes raw materials as bought the week before from many local shops, which sits in mild tension with the fortnightly register and is not resolved | Yuktāhār, confirmed from the stock register; the weekly description single-sourced |

The automatic booking, less any early cancellations, becomes the number every kitchen plans from | Published rule and student experience, confirmed |
| Two to three days ahead | Some vegetables are bought | Kadamba, single-sourced |
| One day ahead | A planning meeting fixes the next day's issue from registrations (regular, Jain and cash meals), the matching Wastage Book page, the weekday attendance register, the month's turnout pattern and any holidays or campus events. Raw materials whose debris exceeds a grams-per-item norm lead to a complaint or a change of supplier | Yuktāhār, confirmed for the meeting and books; the monthly inputs and impurity norm single-sourced |

At the Vijayalakshmi mess the first batch is 80% | Kadamba single-sourced; Vijayalakshmi single-sourced, noisy audio |

| Three to four hours after cooking | Cooked food is no longer usable. At Kadamba, the team member judges that surplus is held, reheated and moved between vessels until then, and degrades quickly | Kadamba operator single-sourced; the reheating judgement candidate |
| Up to 72 hours after the meal | A sample of each item is kept for testing if anyone falls ill | Kadamba, single-sourced |

Kadamba's biryani lunch is the contrast case: it is cooked at 100% of registrations, with a standard of two pieces of 150 to 200 g each and unlimited rice, and later diners run short when some take three pieces (single-sourced).

The real-time demand signals, the scan and the counter staff's view of how much of each dish is left, arrive after the first batch is already cooked. Yuktāhār alone turns the first half hour into a forecast for the rest of the meal. The student's actual decision to eat or skip is made on the morning itself, against a booking made automatically and removable only through five early cancellations a month. Raw stock bought ahead is largely recoverable. Cooked food is not. The binding time constraint on waste is therefore the fixed ratio at which cooked food is prepared against registrations, not the procurement lead time.

The earlier reading of this section was that the current system is not in the middle of changing. That reading was revised by the new operations evidence. # Examine Feedback Loops and Recurring Patterns

**R1: "Shifting the Burden" via Mess Cell** (open chain, held as a candidate reinforcing loop; it would sustain the dysfunction rather than grow it). Confirmed on its central mechanism: zero of six respondents who addressed Skip Meal have ever used it, and the respondent who explained why did so in exactly the terms the incentive analysis predicted: it doesn't return the money. Four of six respondents have used Mess Cell in some form.

![R1: Mess Cell as a Shifting-the-Burden Loop](assets/d10-loop-r1.png)

The informal resale market is a symptomatic relief valve that makes the underlying problem, billing that does not track attendance, tolerable enough that nobody is ever forced to fix the fundamental cause. Every link in this chain is directly evidenced except the final "reduces pressure to fix billing" step, which remains an inference no respondent stated. Until that closing link is evidenced, R1 is held as an open chain with a loop-shaped hypothesis, not a confirmed loop.

**B1: Kitchen waste control** (balancing; revised). The earlier reading was that because no respondent uses Skip Meal, the kitchen-side balancing loop might not close at all. That reading was revised by the new operations evidence. Kitchen waste is balanced by the operator, not by Skip Meal, and each mess runs its own balancing loop with a different delay.

- **Yuktāhār** runs two balancing channels. The first sizes each item from registrations, the Wastage Book page for the matching day two weeks earlier, the attendance register grouped by weekday and the month's turnout, in a planning meeting held twice a day. Having seen 18.7% breakfast attendance on Sunday 6 September, the site head said the quantity would be cut "not next month, next week" (confirmed). This channel's delay is two weeks, and it acts on surplus as well as shortage. The second channel works within the meal: the first half hour of service is used to project the rest (single-sourced), and items are re-cooked or not by a written run-out-time rule, so that an item that runs out at 1:30 is re-cooked and one that runs out at 2:20 is not (confirmed). This channel's delay is minutes, and it acts upward only.
- **Kadamba** cooks a first batch at 70% of registrations, veg and non-veg separately, portioned by a gram standard for each dish. The supervisor watches the counters and orders top-ups that are ready in ten to fifteen minutes (single-sourced). This loop's delay is minutes. Nothing is recorded that would shrink the next first batch.
- **The Vijayalakshmi mess** cooks for 70%, now 80%, of registrations, checks every thirty minutes and has more delivered from its off-site kitchen within about thirty minutes (single-sourced).

The Kadamba and Vijayalakshmi loops close against shortage only: they add food when the counter runs low and have no route that shrinks the first batch when it proves too large. Yuktāhār's first channel is the only one that also closes against surplus, through the Wastage Book and the weekday register, and it does so with a two-week delay. None of the three is calibrated to actual breakfast turnout: the two fixed ratios sit well above it, and the Yuktāhār site head's own working figure for breakfast is about 50% against the roughly 28% his register records. Skip Meal's zero observed student-side use still stands as a separate fact.

Whether Skip Meal reaches Kadamba's kitchen at all is now an open conflict. The earlier finding was that Skip Meal declarations reach the kitchen for same-week preparation. The Kadamba operator's own account is that the kitchen cooks for the number registered, "because it is a responsibility and they are getting paid for it" (single-sourced). The two fit together only if Skip Meal lowers the registration count the kitchen sees; that is unconfirmed, and the conflict is not resolved here.

![B1: Skip Meal Balances Kitchen Preparation, Not Billing](assets/d11-loop-b1.png)

**B2: Capacity Redistribution** (weak or broken). One respondent explicitly registers for the whole month specifically because "Kadamba is hard to get and it gets full": direct, first-person evidence of scarcity-driven demand concentration on the popular mess, rather than organic redistribution to Bakul or Palash.

**Same meal, two failures** (open chain; revised). The earlier candidate was that preparation might be calibrated toward the low true-attendance number, leaving real attendees under-served. That reading was revised by the new operations evidence. At the aggregate, preparation runs well above actual breakfast turnout: Kadamba's first batch is 70% of registrations, about 750 portions a day in April, while 37.1% of registrants actually ate, about 398 a day, so the first batch alone is roughly double what is eaten before any top-up is made (the 70% is the operator's single-sourced statement; the doubling is a candidate inference against the confirmed 37.1%). The Yuktāhār site head believes breakfast turnout is about 50%, while his own register averages about 28%. What survives from the earlier candidate is that real attendees do meet run-outs, but these are driven by timing, not volume: a quarter of the morning's diners arrive in the last fifteen minutes at Kadamba, Yuktāhār cooks mostly in one batch, specific items draw longer queues, and the kitchen responds by re-cooking or serving late-comers at the back door.

The Kadamba operator puts waste at about 5% to 10% a day, without saying whether that is per meal or per day, by weight, or plate waste or kitchen leftover (single-sourced). This sits in tension with a first batch about twice what is eaten. Either most of the surplus is absorbed before it is counted, by the 30 to 50 outside diners such as night security, by about half of Kadamba's own staff who eat at the mess, by reheating into later service and by top-ups smaller than the first batch implies, or the percentage is measured on a different base. The tension is recorded, not resolved.

```
Fixed first batch sized to registrations, which are flat all week
  -> total volume well above actual breakfast turnout -> cooked surplus
     -> held, reheated and moved between vessels, degrading quality (candidate)
     -> outside diners and staff eat from it; the rest goes to the bin
Same first batch meets a steep late-arrival crest
  -> item-level run-outs at 9:15-9:30 -> re-cook, back door open after 9:30
     -> late-comers still served, which keeps arriving late workable
```

Over-preparation in total and shortage at the crest both come from fixed-ratio batch cooking against a curve that crests at close. No evidence links run-outs back to how students register, and no evidence links reheated food back to students skipping, so this is an open chain, not a loop.

**Recurring pattern: the weekly rhythm.** Turnout falls every Sunday at both messes with records (Kadamba 25% to 33%, Yuktāhār 18% to 24%) while registrations stay where they were. The system cycles weekly, and registration does not cycle with it (confirmed).

**Recurring pattern: two kitchens, two forecasting philosophies.** Kadamba prepares a fixed ratio of registrations and reacts at the counter, with no record that carries one morning's surplus into the next morning's first batch. Yuktāhār learns from its own books across a two-week cycle and projects within the meal from its first half hour (single-sourced for both descriptions). The two operators face the same automatic registration, yet state opposite stances: Kadamba cooks for registrations because that is what it is paid for, while Yuktāhār names lower cost and higher profit as goals alongside quality (candidate reading of the difference). Both are exposed to the closing crest, Kadamba through top-ups under time pressure and Yuktāhār through a projection window that sees only about 15% of a morning's diners on the measured curve.

**Recurring pattern: operator beliefs drift from operator records.** The earlier comparison of the Kadamba manager's "300 to 400 by 8:30" with the records was revised: that figure came from a noisy recording and is superseded by his rule of about 100, 100 and then 150 in the last ten minutes, which has the records' shape. At Kadamba, the belief about timing is close to the records. The gap between belief and record is in the level of turnout. The Yuktāhār site head's belief that breakfast turnout is at most about 50% sits beside his own register's 18% to 40%. The Vijayalakshmi operator says Kadamba sees 90% to 95% turnout (single-sourced), against 37% in Kadamba's April records. The numbers are confirmed from records; the narrower pattern, that operators misjudge the level of breakfast turnout more than its timing, is a candidate.

# Key Findings

1. No single trend exists across individuals: respondents move in different directions at once. At the aggregate, Kadamba breakfast turnout was stable at 35% to 39% in every week of April, with a Sunday trough that recurs week after week. Any claim that the paradox is getting worse or better needs multi-month records, not recall.
2. The within-morning crowd is measured, not inferred. It opens with a burst at 7:30, runs flat until 9:00, then climbs steeply: 40% of diners arrive in the last half hour, a quarter in the last fifteen minutes, and about 9% after the nominal close, many of them by the back door. The Kadamba operator's own rule of thumb has the same shape.
3. Skip Meal's observed student-side use is zero, but kitchen waste is balanced anyway, by operator rules with delays ranging from minutes to two weeks. Yuktāhār runs both: a two-week lookup against its own books, which acts on surplus as well as shortage, and a within-meal projection from the first half hour, which acts upward only and sees about 15% of a morning's diners if its curve matches Kadamba's (candidate). Kadamba and the Vijayalakshmi mess act within minutes, against shortage only. None of the rules is calibrated to actual breakfast turnout. This finding was revised by the new operations evidence.
4. The same breakfast fails in two directions at once: too much food in total (candidate, resting on the first-batch ratio, which is the operator's single-sourced statement, set against the confirmed 37.1% turnout), and item-level run-outs at the closing crest. Both come from a fixed first batch meeting a curve that crests at close. Part of the surplus is eaten by 30 to 50 outside diners and about half the Kadamba staff; the operator's figure of 5% to 10% waste has no stated base and is in unresolved tension with a first batch about twice what is eaten. The earlier candidate that preparation is calibrated to the low attendance number, under-serving real diners, was revised by the new operations evidence and does not hold at the aggregate.
5. Registration does not track the daily decision to eat. Kadamba registrations are flat at about 1,070 a day every weekday, Sunday included, while turnout swings from 27% to 42%. Sunday is the lowest and latest-arriving day at both messes with records. Registration is automatic, with five early cancellations per meal type per month as the only way out.
6. Breakfast is the structurally worst meal. At Yuktāhār, about 28% of breakfast registrants eat against 56% to 89% at lunch on the same days, and at Kadamba 62.9% of April breakfast registrations were no-shows. The "about 30%" gap students had cited is a lunch-sized figure; the breakfast gap is 63% to 72%.
7. Apart from the temporary non-compulsory registration during the LPG shortage (whether it has also applied over holidays is disputed), every recent change in this system sits on the operator side, and none of those has touched registration or billing. The one reported reduction in waste (Yuktāhār, August to December, reported by the kitchen staff themselves) came from a paper routine made deliberately blame-free and discussed daily over about four months, alongside team training at a project manager's farmhouse. Software was tried and dropped because it cost more than the data it would add, and staff are not digitally inclined. The switch to rock salt and cold-pressed oil came about six months before the visit, later than the takeover.
8. The two kitchens with the best evidence run opposite forecasting philosophies: a fixed ratio plus reactive top-up at Kadamba, and learning from its own books plus a within-meal projection at Yuktāhār. Both are exposed to the closing crest, one through top-ups under time pressure, the other through a projection window that sees only about a seventh of the morning's diners on the measured curve (candidate transfer to Yuktāhār).
